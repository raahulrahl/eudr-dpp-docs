Rubber processing plants play a crucial role in the supply chain, converting raw materials into products such as block rubber, centrifuged rubber, and natural rubber*<sup>1</sup>* . These processors include state-owned, private, and foreign direct investment (FDI) enterprises. State-owned enterprises typically source raw materials from their own estate plantations, while private and FDI enterprises primarily rely on smallholder plantations. Aiming to stabilise supply and enhance traceability, some companies have established linkages with traders and groups of smallholders.

After preliminary processing, raw rubber is either exported or used domestically to produce finished products such as tyres, gloves, or other industrial goods. The production of rubber products, primarily led by private and FDI enterprises, generates higher added value. Both natural rubber and finished products are consumed locally or exported. For exports, Vietnamese enterprises typically partner with foreign importers rather than handling direct distribution overseas.

![](_page_7_Picture_2.jpeg)

*In this report, "natural rubber" (NR) is a broad term referring to newly harvested rubber, including latex and coagulated latex (collectively known as "unprocessed NR"). It also encompasses rubber that has undergone processing into various forms such as block rubber (SVR), smoked sheet rubber (RSS), and concentrated latex, which are exportable by Viet Nam (collectively known as "processed NR").*

*In other words, "processed rubber" is derived from "unprocessed rubber." However, both serve as raw materials for manufacturing finished products (commonly referred to as "rubber products"), such as tyres, shoe soles, mattresses, and pillows. Collectively, they are categorised as "raw rubber".* 

The supply chain may face challenges aligning with the European Union Deforestation Regulation (EUDR). Issues include the small, fragmented, and dispersed nature of smallholder plantations, the involvement of multiple intermediaries in raw rubber transactions, and the lack of record-keeping practices. Additionally, the mixing of dierent sources complicates traceability. The majority of smallholder plantations lack geographical coordinates. Meanwhile, the absence of a clear forest and land use map (as of 31 December 2020) further hinders eorts to verify that smallholder rubber cultivation did not contribute to deforestation after the cut-o date of December 2020.

Businesses using imported raw materials from Cambodia and Laos also face additional risks from a lack of transparency about origin when dierent sources are mixed, including illegal tapping and stolen latex.

However, the Vietnamese rubber industry also has favourable conditions supporting alignment with the EUDR. Large forestry and export processing enterprises, with strong financial and technical capabilities, have invested in enterprise management systems and developed comprehensive databases. These investments help their businesses to easily meet new requirements, such as traceability and chain of custody (CoC) transparency.

Additionally, large and concentrated plantation areas with available equipment and human resources enable enterprises to quickly fulfill requirements related to boundary and coordinate determination, proving that they do not contribute to deforestation.

Viet Nam is a significant exporter of natural rubber, with China being its largest market from 2019 to 2023. Notably, China is also a major supplier of rubber products to the EU. This indicates that companies in China (as well as those in India and Malaysia, who have been large buyers of Vietnamese rubber) have used or will use Vietnamese rubber to manufacture goods for the EU market. This highlights the global impact of the EUDR on the rubber industry and underscores its influence in driving Viet Nam's supply chain actors to meet EUDR requirements.

In addition, some enterprises, intermediary agents, and smallholder households or groups have implemented partnership models to stabilise and shorten the supply chain from households to processing enterprises. These models adhere to appropriate sustainable forest management certification standards that align with EUDR requirements with financial and technical support from customers. While not yet widespread, these initiatives have shown positive results and potential for replication if given sucient technical and financial support.

![](_page_8_Picture_8.jpeg)

Private sector actors should promote customer engagement in supporting domestic processing manufacturers and their raw material suppliers, enabling them to be ready for EUDR application. These models will help reduce intermediaries; lower transaction costs; shorten the supply chain; and ensure a stable, transparent, and traceable supply flow. Additionally, standardised operational processes and pre-competitive information-sharing systems should be developed and implemented by the industry to ensure that all necessary data needed for EUDR compliance is fully transmitted along the CoC, maintaining traceability throughout the entire supply chain.

Specific support programmes by government and private sector actors should be established to help smallholders enhance their understanding of EUDR and improve their production and trading processes. These programmes should focus on upgrading production skills, ensuring better environmental and social standards, and increasing the transparency of the supply chains in which they participate.

Rubber dealers or intermediary traders purchasing latex from small rubber plantations also need awareness-raising activities and technical support to ensure traceability for the rubber latex they bring into the supply chain.

A mobile app for handheld devices, such as smartphones, should be developed to help smallholders and intermediate raw latex dealers update transaction information in real time. This could be led by either the Vietnamese government or private sector stakeholders, including technology companies, rubber processing enterprises, and industry associations. The government could facilitate development and ensure the accessibility of such an app. Ideally, this app should be free for smallholders, open-source, and with interoperable data formats while ensuring data privacy. Meanwhile, the private sector, especially rubber enterprises, could contribute with outreach through technical development, implementation, and ongoing maintenance of the app.

### Aiming to fully meet EUDR requirements in the Vietnamese rubber industry, the research team proposes the following solutions:

Vietnamese government actors, as well as private sector actors downstream in the supply chain, could promote multi-channel communication to enhance awareness and understanding of the EUDR, especially amongst local enterprises, local management ocials, traders, and smallholders at the provincial, district, and commune levels.

Public authorities could establish a national mechanism to enhance cooperation among local interested parties such as government bodies, business associations, processors, traders, and smallholder groups, leveraging their resources and strengths to contribute to Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and ensure alignment with EUDR requirements across the industry. Also, public-private partnerships can support smallholders with technical and financial resources, while processing companies can provide training and outreach on best practice for deforestation-free sourcing.

Viet Nam is already supported by initiatives such as the SAFE and EUDR ENG projects. These international initiatives and support programmes, especially in the context of the Team Europe Initiative (TEI) on deforestation-free value chains, oer valuable opportunities for cross-country collaboration and knowledge exchange for the rubber industry in Viet Nam. Focus should be on smallholder rubber farmers and intermediate raw rubber dealers, strengthening their capacity, improving access to resources, and involving them in the sustainable rubber supply chain to ensure long-term impact and alignment with Viet Nam's development goals.

A mechanism should be developed to encourage farmer outreach and partnership through groups of farmer households or partnerships among processors, traders, and households/household groups. This could be developed by both public authorities and private sector stakeholders in Viet Nam working together to foster collaboration and create further opportunities for smallholders to organise themselves and adopt more sustainable practices.

Audits for sustainability management certifications compatible with the EUDR present a valuable opportunity for private sector stakeholders to use synergies to provide and collect data relevant to the EUDR context. However, it is crucial to recognise that the EUDR does not consider any certification scheme a green light for compliance. Certain certification schemes might be helpful tools for data collection. Even so, they are not a substitute for the due diligence obligations of operators. Currently, widely recognised sustainability standards do not fully meet all EUDR requirements. If supply chain actors can ensure deforestation-free value chains in line with EUDR requirements through other eective means, and their customers do not specifically require a sustainability certification, obtaining one is unnecessary. This clarification should be widely communicated amongst Vietnamese supply chain actors to prevent misunderstanding.

Local authorities could facilitate the issuance of Land Use Right Certificates (LURCs) for rubber plantation areas stably used by households for a long time, free of disputes, and aligned with existing land use planning. The updated LURC format, which includes geographical coordinates, not only serves as legal proof of ownership but also helps reduce the costs of providing evidence for deforestation-free and legal commodity production in line with EUDR requirements. For eligible areas where LURCs cannot be immediately granted, the government could provide clear guidance and agree on alternative forms of ownership and land-use verification. This will prevent households from being excluded from the supply chain and mitigate potential negative socio-economic impacts on smallholder farmers.

Vietnamese regulatory agencies could expedite the development of a unified and synchronised land database and assign production codes to all rubber plantations and other crops. Additionally, a mechanism for sharing information on sourcing areas could be established to support stakeholders with legitimate and legal needs to ensure compliance with the national legislative framework for legal and deforestation-free commodity production.

Information on FDI companies in the rubber industry remains very limited, with Vietnamese customs

records on imports and exports being the only accessible source. Given their significant role in Viet Nam's rubber supply chain, greater transparency and active participation from these companies are essential for traceability along the value chain. Government stakeholders in Viet Nam should establish a mechanism to encourage coordination and information sharing among FDI companies, starting with European firms.

Government agencies and private sector stakeholders could establish a mechanism to encourage smallholders to proactively organise themselves into household groups—operating independently alongside the existing partnership model between smallholder farmers and enterprises under cooperatives or cooperative groups. This will enhance supply chain transparency, shorten distribution channels, and increase the capacities of stakeholders to produce deforestation-free commodities. Development institutions and non-governmental organisations can play a key role in supporting this initiative.

# <span id="page-12-0"></span>01. Introduction

### 1.1. Background

Nearly 90% of global deforestation is driven by the expansion of agricultural land, with a significant portion of agricultural products from deforested areas being traded internationally. To mitigate its contribution to deforestation and forest degradation, the EU adopted the EUDR.

This regulation aligns with various international declarations and policy objectives of the EU. Additionally, it supports the implementation of key global agreements, including the SDGs, the Paris Agreement, the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework, and the Glasgow Leaders' Declaration on Forests and Land Use, emphasising the urgent need for forest protection worldwide.

Under the EUDR, companies placing relevant products on or exporting them from the EU market (operators) must ensure, through due diligence measures, that these products are legally produced

and do not contribute to deforestation (or, in the case of wood, forest degradation) after December 2020. The EUDR applies to beef, cocoa, coee, natural rubber, palm oil, soy, and timber, as well as certain products derived from these materials. The regulation entered into force on 29 June 2023, and will be applied from 30 December 2025*<sup>2</sup>* . Of the commodities from Viet Nam most aected by this regulation, natural rubber products are grouped alongside coee and timber.

Natural rubber production and processing are key economic sectors in Viet Nam, significantly contributing to the country's export revenue. Europe is Viet Nam's third-largest export market for natural rubber products, behind China and the United States.

*On 17 December 2024, the European Parliament approved a one-year postponement of EUDR adoption, shifting the date from 30 December 2024 to 30 December 2025.*

### <span id="page-14-0"></span>1.3. Research methodology and scope

This study utilises both secondary and primary data, with secondary data serving as the main source of information. Secondary data is drawn from ocial statistics provided by the General Statistics Oce (GSO), Viet Nam Customs, and Departments of Agriculture and Rural Development in provinces with rubber plantations. Additional sources include project reports, relevant research studies from organisations and individuals, as well as analysis and discussion findings and presentations from relevant events, seminars, and technical meetings.

The analysis of what is considered relevant national legislation within Viet Nam's legal framework in the context of the EUDR was conducted using methodology developed by the European Forestry Institute (EFI), which has previously been applied to the wood and coee industries.

Primary data for the study was gathered through surveys and direct interviews with various stakeholders, including government departments, rubber processors, exporters, raw latex traders, small rubber plantation households, and industry experts in Binh Duong province and Ho Chi Minh City. Additionally, online and telephone interviews were conducted with experts and enterprises in the industry.

A consultation meeting was held to present the key findings of the study and gather additional information and feedback from regulatory agencies, organisations, and individuals who contributed to the report. All activities were conducted between November and December 2024 and in January 2025. Appendix 17 provides a list of interviewees.

The Vietnamese rubber industry produces three main categories of output: raw rubber (natural rubber), rubber products, and rubber wood and its derivatives. However, under the EUDR, wood-related aspects fall within the regulatory scope for wood products. Therefore, this study focuses exclusively on the first two categories. Additionally, FDI enterprises are not covered in this report due to limited access to relevant information.

The report consists of five parts. The first part provides an overview of the context, objectives, methodology, and scope of the study. Part 2 presents a comprehensive view of the rubber industry and supply chain in Viet Nam, focusing on two product categories: natural rubber and further rubber products. This section covers the industry's role in the economy, export turnover to the EU market, key enterprises, and sources of raw rubber. It concludes with an overview of the supply chain and its key actors. Part 3 examines relevant national legislation in the Vietnamese context as stated in the EUDR, with a focus on traceability, deforestation-free requirements, legality, and due diligence. Part 4 analyses the current state of the industry, highlighting challenges and opportunities for supply chain actors to meet EUDR requirements for legality and deforestation-free production. The final section summarises key discussion points and oers recommendations to enhance the Vietnamese rubber industry's readiness to prepare for EUDR application across the supply chain.

### 2.1. Rubber industry overview: some key findings

The rubber industry is one of Viet Nam's key export-oriented economic sectors. According to the GSO, in 2023, the industry's total export turnover—including (i) natural rubber; (ii) rubber products*<sup>3</sup>* , and; (iii) rubber wood and its derivatives —reached USD 9.4 billion. According to the Viet Nam Rubber Association (VRA), the industry aimed for an export turnover of USD 10.2 billion in 2024, a 7.9% increase on 2023. Among the key segments, rubber products are expected to generate the highest turnover, at USD 4.6 billion, followed by natural rubber (over USD 3.1 billion), and rubber wood (over USD 2.5 billion). Data from Viet Nam Customs*<sup>6</sup>* indicates that, in the first ten months of 2024, Viet Nam's total export turnover of natural rubber and rubber-based products was estimated at USD 6.6 billion. Of this, natural rubber accounted for USD 2.5 billion, while rubber products contributed USD 4.1 billion, equivalent to 64.7% of the annual target.

### SUPPLY OF INPUT MATERIALS

Viet Nam's rubber processing industry relies on raw rubber (latex/natural rubber) from both domestic and imported sources. According to data from the GSO and Viet Nam Customs, in 2023, the industry consumed nearly 2.47 million tons (dry weight) of natural rubber*<sup>11</sup>* . Of this, domestic rubber plantations supplied 1.27 million tons, accounting for 51% of the total natural rubber used in the processing industry. Meanwhile, imports from around 20 markets contributed 1.2 million tons, making up the remaining 49%.

Cambodia and Laos were the two largest sources of imported natural rubber for Viet Nam in 2023, supplying 38% and 6%, respectively, of the total raw materials used by the Vietnamese rubber processing industry (Figure 2). When considering imports alone, these two markets accounted for over 90% of Viet Nam's total natural rubber imports that year.

Viet Nam's domestic raw rubber supply comes from a total rubber plantation area of 911,200 hectares nationwide (2023, GSO). Smallholder rubber plantations managed by about 265,000 farming households (so-called smallholder rubber) cover 489,700 hectares and produce 800,000 tons of raw rubber (dry weight). The remaining 421,500 hectares are owned by around 160 state-owned, private, and FDI enterprises*<sup>12</sup>* (so-called estate rubber plantations), supplying 470,000 tons. In terms of proportion, in 2023, smallholder rubber plantations accounted for 54% of the area and 63% of the volume of raw rubber. Meanwhile, large-scale rubber plantations accounted for 46% of the area and 37% of the volume. The volume and proportion of raw rubber from smallholder plantations have increased continuously over the past 10 years (since 2014), showing that raw rubber from

smallholder farmers is increasingly important to the Vietnamese rubber processing industry.

A notable trend is the steady decline in Viet Nam's rubber plantation area over the years, from a peak of 985,600 hectares in 2015 to 911,200 hectares in 2023. On average, this area has decreased by over 9,000 hectares per year during the 2015–2023 period. This decline is primarily due to crop restructuring, as rubber plantations are converted to higher-value crops such as durian, avocado, cashew, or short-term cash crops. Additionally, some areas have been repurposed for urban expansion and the development of new industrial zones. ### 3.1. The EUDR in short

### THE EUDR IN SHORT

The EUDR's objective is to: (a) minimise the EU's contribution to global deforestation and forest degradation, thereby reducing global forest loss, and; (b) reduce the EU's contribution to greenhouse gas emissions and global biodiversity loss (Article 1(1)).

The EUDR requires that seven commodities, including livestock, cocoa, coee, palm oil, rubber, soy, and timber (Article 2(1)) can only be placed on the EU market if they meet three conditions: (a) they are deforestation-free; (b) they have been produced in accordance with the relevant legislation of the country of production, and; (c) they are covered by a due diligence statement (Article 3).

Operators and traders, as defined under the EUDR*23*, are obliged to fulfill their due diligence obligations if they want to place a product covered by the EUDR on the EU market for the first time or export them from there. The three-step due diligence process is as follows:

- First, operators must collect relevant information, including the geolocation of all areas of production. The geolocation of each plot only needs to be collected once using widely available and free technology.

- Secondly, they need to conduct a risk assessment based on the information collected.
- Thirdly, they must mitigate and manage risk when a non-negligible risk is identified. This should be confirmed by a due diligence statement and the geolocation of the plots of production submitted.

### DUE DILIGENCE STATEMENT (SEE ANNEX II OF THE EUDR):

A due diligence statement must be submitted to European customs authorities before import. It contains the geolocation of the plot of production as well as the information stated in Annex II of the EUDR.

Under the EUDR, only operators and traders are obliged to fulfill their due diligence obligations. However, there are also implications for other actors along the supply chain. In particular, smallholders and SMEs in the producing countries may be asked by business partners to provide information for due diligence purposes, and this needs to be transferred along the value chain. The EUDR itself gives various measures of support for producers on the ground. For example, in Article 12, companies are asked to support suppliers, particularly smallholders, through investments and capacity building as part of their risk mitigation measures.

'Deforestation-free' means that the relevant products contain, have been fed with, or have been made using relevant commodities that were produced on land that has not been subject to deforestation after 31 December 2020. 'Forest', as defined under the EUDR, means land spanning more than 0.5 hectares with trees higher than 5 metres and a canopy cover of more than 10%, or trees able to reach those thresholds in situ, excluding land that is predominantly under agricultural or urban land use.

"Production in accordance with the relevant laws of the country of production" means the relevant commodities are produced in compliance with local laws on 'land use rights', 'environmental protection', 'third-party rights', 'labour rights', 'human rights under international law', 'Free, Prior, and Informed Consent (FPIC), including regulations under the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples', and 'taxation, anti-corruption, trade, and customs regulations'. As stated in the ocial Guidance Document on the EUDR, only the applicable laws concerning the legal status of the area of production constitute relevant legislation pursuant to Article 2(40) of the EUDR. This means that, generally, the relevance of laws for the legality

requirement in Article 3(b) of the EUDR is not determined by the fact that they may apply generally during the production process of commodities or apply to the supply chains of relevant products and commodities, but by the fact that these laws specifically impact or influence the legal status of the area in which the commodities were produced. Additionally, Article 2(40) of the EUDR must be read in the light of the objectives of the EUDR as laid down in Article 1(1)(a) and (b), meaning that legislation is also relevant if its contents can be linked to halting deforestation and forest degradation in the context of the Union's commitment to address climate change and biodiversity loss.

The most important legal category concerning the legality requirement under the EUDR is LURs. The land administration system in Viet Nam is complex, with various land use modes through which producers—especially smallholders—can secure legal LURs. Several forms of documentation can be used to prove LURs, but the clearest and most secure evidence is the LURC. Regarding LURs, Viet Nam has specific regulations for cases with legal LURs. However, in practice, some smallholders lack full documentation proving their LURs, despite having cultivated their plantation steadily for many years. Moving forward, the government is interested in implementing policies to assist smallholders to obtain LURCs for eligible plantations, particularly those with no deforestation violations since 2020.

Viet Nam has established regulations on 'boundaries' and 'coordinate grids' for plantations. However, these regulations were not enforced until August 2024, meaning that most LUR documents lack this information.

For environmental, forestry, labour, and tax/import/export requirements, compliance and documentation are primarily enforced for larger enterprises. Smallholders, defined as individuals or households owning land or plantations that do not meet the size or revenue thresholds required for enterprise registration, are typically not subject to the same strict legal requirements. While smallholders are theoretically required to comply with certain laws, enforcement is often less stringent.

### 4.1. Challenges

### *4.1.1. Smallholder*

<span id="page-46-0"></span>Since the collection and provision of information for due diligence relate to specific CoC along the supply chain, the following sections are organised by actor.

### **(a) Smallholder rubber is becoming increasingly important in Viet Nam's rubber processing industry. However, it involves hundreds of thousands of households, each with small, scattered plots that may grow various types of trees**

The proportion of rubber output in the total domestic supply has been steadily increasing over the past 10 years. According to 2023 data, smallholder rubber made up 54% of the country's total rubber area, contributing 63% of domestic raw rubber production. However, nearly 87% of the 265,000 households growing rubber nationwide managed less than 3 hectares each*31*. Each household owned one to three rubber plots and, in some cases, up to five plots, often scattered and interspersed with areas growing other crops. These plantations might contain a variety of trees, not just rubber*32*. Due to this fragmentation, along with the limited knowledge and technical capacity of farmers, latex transactions occur daily without providing the required information mandated by the EUDR (such as specific data on the amount of latex harvested from each plot). As a result, it is impossible to meet the traceability requirements for each transaction.

### **(b) Most smallholder plantations lack geolocation for their production plots**

Although most smallholder rubber plantations in Viet Nam have LURCs, these certificates do not include geolocation data. To meet EUDR requirements for traceability and deforestation-free production, collecting geolocation information is crucial. However, achieving this before the EUDR's ocial implementation could be challenging due to high costs, limited technical knowledge among households, and a lack of clear guidance from stakeholders.

As noted, the number of households involved in smallholder rubber is numerous with small, fragmented, and scattered growing areas. Therefore, collecting geolocation data for each plot is dicult for several reasons: (i) the limited technical capacity of households; (ii) lack of documentation verifying the legality of production (for example, LURCs); (iii) boundary disputes, and; (iv) plots where only part of the area is used for rubber. Field coordination often requires local cadastral ocials and cooperation from neighbouring landowners, making it complex and costly*33*. Additionally, the EUDR requirement for geographical coordinates of land plots/rubber plantations adds an extra eort to fieldwork. Specifically, plots under 4 hectares require point coordinates, while larger plots need multiple sets of coordinates, increasing the workload for data collection.

MAE issued Circular 10/2024/TT-BTNMT*34* on 31 July 2024, mandating the recording of the coordinates of plot peaks in LURCs*35* starting from 1 August 2024. However, this applies only to new certificates, leaving most older ones without this information. Although data on district-level land resources can provide plot coordinates, access to this information is limited due to a lack of sharing mechanisms.

Decision No. 3156/QD-BNN-TT*36* of MAE provides temporary guidance on PUC issuance and management. However, this document applies only to crops related to food safety, such as fruit trees, rice, and coee, and has not been extended to rubber trees.*37* As a result, PUC has not been implemented for the rubber industry, limiting support for rubber plantation owners' adaptation eorts with regards to the EUDR.

### **(c) Information about the EUDR is still "new" to most smallholders, many of whom are either not interested in or do not fully understand its requirements**

An interview with smallholders in early December 2024 revealed that, while most households have heard of the EUDR, they do not fully understand its purpose, their responsibilities, or its impact. Many are not concerned about EUDR requirements, as their transactions continue as usual without any noticeable changes. Some households confuse the EUDR with sustainable forest management certification, or view it as part of agricultural extension, forest management, or credit programmes, as it is sometimes mentioned in meetings related to these. This confusion is understandable, as by early December 2024, there has been limited communication and training on EUDR at the household level. Any eorts so far have been small-scale, often "integrated" into other activities or driven by certain processors promoting linkages with smallholders, covering only a fraction of the total households and rubber areas nationwide.

### **(d) Smallholders do not typically collect or store information on latex production and trade transactions**

Smallholder rubber farmers typically do not keep records of production activities, such as the date of tapping or the amount of latex collected, especially coagulated latex, which is sold only after accumulating enough over time. Without such records, households cannot track how much latex was collected on a given tapping day in a certain plot.

In terms of latex trading, smallholder rubber is usually sold to processing factories through raw latex local traders/intermediaries. When a transaction occurs, both the household and the trader record six types of information: (1) The name of the rubber plantation owner; (2) the date of the transaction; (3) density or content of latex; (4) the weight of the latex; (5) the unit price per kg of latex, and; (6) the total transaction value*38*. While this information is recorded, it is insucient for EUDR traceability requirements. For example, details of which specific plantation the latex came from are not collected. This hinders traceability because each household has several plantations.

This practice has been in place for a long time, as no parties have required more detailed information, except in cases where households participate in a sustainable forest management certification programme (to be discussed in the next section).

### **(e) Latex from a smallholder can be sold to various buyers, but without an information-sharing mechanism, traceability of raw rubber becomes increasingly dicult down the supply chain**

Latex from a single smallholder is often supplied to multiple companies for reasons such as dissatisfaction with latex density measurements, uncompetitive prices, or logistical factors like dierent plantations being closer to various collection points. This can lead to latex from one household's multiple plantations being split across dierent traders. However, local traders/intermediaries lack a system for sharing information, making it impossible to track the total supply from a household or determine whether it exceeds the plantation's production capacity of a household in a given season*<sup>39</sup>* .

The six types of information recorded for each transaction (mentioned earlier) are only used between household and trader, and are not shared with others in the supply chain. This data is retained solely for payment purposes, either immediately after a transaction or after a month. However, once payment is made, the information is discarded. As a result, this information is not stored by households and traders or passed along, creating a "loss of traceability" as information is not systematically passed along the value chain. This also opens the door for households to mix latex from other sources, including potentially stolen latex, with their own, further complicating traceability eorts.

### **(f) Some smallholders (and enterprises) are not willing to share/provide information**

In some cases, when processors try to establish linkage models with companies and smallholders (integrating sustainable forest certification and EUDR alignment), they face resistance from smallholders and companies who are reluctant to share personal information (such as IDs, residence details, or LURCs). These parties fear that their personal information may be misused, potentially leading to harm*40*.

Similarly, when the CMO organisation approached local parties to collaborate on sustainable forest management certification for household groups with the involvement of processors and local traders/intermediaries, they encountered reluctance from both processors and traders to share information about their activities*<sup>41</sup>* .

### **(g) Smallholders hire workers based on verbal agreements, without formal contracts, and there is unclear information regarding the use of child or underage labour**

Most smallholders hire workers for tasks such as latex tapping; plantation maintenance (fertilising, weeding, spraying pesticides); and latex collection and transportation to purchasing points. These workers are typically hired informally, without a labour contract or formal agreement*42*. There are reports that some households may employ child labour, but this has not been verified.

### **(h) Some smallholders lack legal documents proving ownership of their rubber plantations, and the land is still disputed with unclear boundaries**

While most smallholders possess legal documents like LURCs or contracts for land lease, some lack proper documentation for their rubber plantations*43*:

- In some areas, land is lent or leased through verbal agreements among households and individuals, with no legal framework governing these transactions.
- The boundaries on LURCs do not always match the actual land boundaries.
- Some plantations have not been issued LURCs, despite long-term, stable, and legitimate use.
- A few cases involve ongoing land disputes.

Other cases include land being bought and sold multiple times, with missing or inconsistent transaction records, making it dicult to establish a basis for granting an LURC. Additionally, some people encroached on forest land for production decades ago, and as it has been used stably for a long time, the State reclassified it as agricultural land according to land use plans. Local authorities have reviewed and verified the land, and government inspectors have concluded it is eligible for use, but an LURC has not been granted.*<sup>44</sup>*

### <span id="page-50-0"></span>*4.1.2. Raw rubber traders*

**(a) Traders play a crucial role as intermediaries in the supply of smallholder rubber. While many traders are operating, their activities are often spontaneous, making them dicult to manage and monitor**

Local traders or intermediaries are Vietnamese individuals or households that purchase latex from smallholders. They often establish one or more collection points at their homes or near the rubber plantations to facilitate purchasing, reduce transportation time, and encourage nearby households to sell latex to them. They buy both liquid and coagulated latex from households, then resell it to other traders or raw rubber processors.

Local trading companies play a crucial role in the Vietnamese rubber supply chain, with over 95% of smallholder rubber entering the supply chain through this link. The number of traders involved in smallholder latex transactions is relatively large, with around 10 typically operating within a commune in rubber-growing areas, depending on the plantation area and the number of rubber-growing households. However, traders' activities are largely spontaneous. They can freely start or stop operations, change locations, or open new purchasing points. This flexibility makes it dicult for local management agencies to eectively oversee and regulate their activities.

Some traders have joined the processor - trader - smallholder linkage. However, the number of local traders participating in this model is still very small compared to the total number of local traders operating.

**(b) Raw rubber is bought and sold through multiple intermediary local traders. Transactions are not fully recorded, and information is not shared with each other or with other actors** 

Intermediary traders collecting raw latex from smallholders operate at multiple levels, buying and selling to one another. Small traders supply to larger dealers who, in turn, supply to even larger dealers, and so on. Similar to transactions between smallholders and traders, transactions between traders involve limited information, restricted to buying, selling, and payment details (the six groups of information described above). Transaction information between the two parties is not shared with third parties, partly to protect their business secrets. Some traders said they have never been asked for additional information and do not require sellers - whether smallholders or other supply chain actors - to provide any documents or information beyond the six specified details.

The household-trader-processor model aims to enhance information collection and storage, separate EUDR-compliant and non-compliant sources, and partially address the issue of "lost" information regarding the origin of raw materials.

**(c) Mixing raw materials from dierent sources, including those of unknown origin, without proper documentation is still common**

When latex is collected at a trader's purchasing point, the six required information groups are recorded, but the latex is often stored in large tanks, typically made of cement or metal. After the transaction is complete, the coagulated latex is transferred to storage. Local traders may sell directly to processors, but it is more common for them to trade with each other. Small traders sell to larger dealers who, in turn, sell to even larger dealers, eventually supplying the processors*<sup>45</sup>* .

This practice of mixing latex from various smallholders and trading between intermediary companies undermines the accuracy and traceability of information about the sources of raw materials.

*<sup>45</sup>On-site survey in Binh Phuoc, December 2024; Quang et al. (2021, 2024).*

### **(d) Labour use without labour contracts**

Traders hire labourers to help with collecting and transporting latex from household plantations to the purchasing/collection points or to manage those points. In many cases, these workers are not given formal labour contracts. Additionally, there is no available information regarding the potential use of child labour.

### **(e) Some local traders may not be ocially registered**

Large-scale traders collecting smallholder latex are typically registered as household businesses or established as companies. However, many small companies operate without formal registration. According to some sources*46*, unregistered traders cannot provide financial invoices to buyers. As a result, buyers (whether other traders or processors) must handle the necessary documentation themselves if they require complete financial records. If this is accurate, it compromises both the legality and traceability of transactions, making proper implementation dicult.

### <span id="page-52-0"></span>4.1.3. Enterprises involved in estate rubber growing, processing, and exporting

**(a) Enterprises are unable to collect necessary information and integrate it into the production process to meet EUDR requirements**

While supporting processors to work with traders and smallholders, the CMO found that most enterprises, including those that both cultivate estate rubber and process it for export, are not equipped to collect information such as the geolocation of rubber plantations and link it to the production process for traceability as per the EUDR*47*. Interviews with enterprises in Binh Phuoc and Dak Lak revealed that, without external support, these enterprises "struggle" to adapt to EUDR requirements, as one representative described it. This challenge is mainly due to the novelty of the EUDR for these enterprises and the lack of specific guidance to help them prepare for EUDR application.

### **(b) The number of rubber enterprises actively working to meet the EUDR remains low**

Only a few large processors that export directly to the EU and their EU customers or importers requesting EUDR compliance are taking an interest. Customs data also reveals that the number of enterprises exporting rubber products to the EU is relatively small compared to the total number of rubber processing enterprises in the country, despite the EU market ranking third in export turnover for the industry in 2023.

A survey conducted by Forest Trends and the VRA showed that many exporters have not begun to prepare for EUDR application. It found that 35% of the responding enterprises, despite being involved in exporting rubber to the EU, have not yet started the necessary preparations to meet EUDR requirements*48*.

### **(c) Many enterprises are aware of the EUDR, but their understanding remains limited**

According to a 2024 survey by Forest Trends and the VRA, most enterprises exporting to the EU are aware of the EUDR, primarily through information from their customers—operators of rubber products. However, the survey indicates that the level of understanding of these regulations among exporting enterprises remains low. Only 14% said that they fully understand the EUDR, 41% believe they understand most of it, and 44% reported only a partial understanding*<sup>49</sup>*

**(d) Processors source raw rubber from a variety of suppliers. The more diverse these sources, the harder it becomes to fully trace the origin of the raw materials across the entire supply chain**

Most enterprises, whether state-owned or private, use raw rubber (latex) from multiple sources, including their own rubber plantations, other enterprises' rubber plantations, commercial raw rubber traders, local traders/intermediaries, and smallholders. As the number of suppliers increases and the sources become more varied, it becomes harder to accurately trace the origin of all purchased latex and raises the risk of failing to meet traceability requirements*<sup>50</sup>* .

**(e) Raw rubber imported from Cambodia and Laos is a key input for domestic processors. However, much of it is of unknown origin and lacks accompanying documentation, often being mixed with other sources, including illegally sourced or stolen latex**

In 2023, Cambodia and Laos were the most important suppliers of rubber, accounting for 44% of the total raw rubber supply for Viet Nam's processing industry. The rubber from these countries comes from various sources, including smallholders, state-owned enterprises, FDI, private, and military sectors. Like in Viet Nam, raw rubber is collected through a multi-level system of intermediary processors and traders before being exported*51*. However, there is a significant lack of traceability and legality in these supplies, with the mixing of dierent raw materials without proper documentation being widespread*52*.

In some rubber-growing areas in Laos, up to 20% of latex is stolen. Much of the natural rubber produced in Laos is exported to Viet Nam, including stolen rubber, as confirmed by some Vietnamese enterprises operating in Laos. This represents a considerable legal risk for Vietnamese enterprises using this imported rubber, as stolen rubber has no origin information, making traceability impossible*<sup>53</sup>* .

**(f) Private processing enterprises, which primarily rely on raw rubber from smallholders or imports, face greater challenges in complying with the EUDR compared to state-owned enterprises with estate rubber plantations**

Private enterprises investing in rubber processing often lack land to establish their own plantations, forcing them to rely on rubber supplied by smallholders or imported from countries like Cambodia and Laos. Due to ongoing issues with sources of smallholder and imported rubber, aligning the supply chains of these private enterprises with EUDR requirements is particularly challenging.

### **(g) There are some misconceptions about the EUDR**

Consultations with processors reveal that some believe the "EUDR is a type of sustainable management certificate" or that "obtaining a sustainable forest management certificate automatically ensures compliance with EUDR*54*."

These misconceptions are misleading for two reasons. Firstly, holding a sustainable forest management certificate does not guarantee EUDR compliance, as the EUDR does not recognise any specific certification. Secondly, sustainable forest certification standards (such as FSC-FM and VFCS/PEFC-FM) do not include all the information required by the EUDR, such as geolocation for plantations, FPIC guidance, or details on taxes and fees*55*. These certification bodies have announced updates to their standards to align with the EUDR. However, certified areas still do not fully meet its requirements. For instance, certification schemes might be helpful in gathering information, but are not a substitute for fulfilling due diligence obligations under the EUDR.

### **(c) Rubber trees currently tapped for latex are not related to deforestation**

Rubber trees in Viet Nam, Cambodia, and Laos (Viet Nam's largest source of imported raw rubber) require an initial development stage of 7 to 10 years before they can be tapped for latex. This "construction stage" means that rubber trees cannot be tapped until their eighth year. As a result, rubber products sold to the EU by the end of 2025 will primarily use raw rubber from trees planted before 31 December 2020.

Therefore, there is no concern that products are linked to deforestation in these countries as of 31 December 2020. Latex collected after 2026 will need to be checked for the risk of deforestation, especially when production expands to new sites where deforestation occurred to establish plantations.

### **(d) Most rubber plantations have legal ownership documentation**

All estate plantations owned by rubber-growing companies typically have complete documentation proving their ownership or LURs, as most originated from state-owned farms. A few private-sector estate plantations also lease land from the state, ensuring legal ownership documents such as LURCs or Land Allocation Decisions.

For smallholder rubber, most plantations also possess full legal documentation, including LURCs or land lease/assignment contracts, with clear boundaries and no disputes. Only a small number lack legal documents.

### **(e) Existing infrastructure facilitates EUDR alignment for enterprises**

Enterprises involved in forest plantation, processing, and export have made significant investments in infrastructure, human resources, and equipment. They have established internal management systems and use software/applications to streamline management, update information, and store databases in a systematic manner, covering everything from planation management to processing and exporting activities.

Many enterprises, especially large-scale ones, hold ISO-9000/9001 and ISO-14000/14001 certifications, as well as sustainable forest management and CoC certifications like PEFC or FSC. Large, concentrated (estate) plantations also make it easier to gather relevant data, such as the geolocation of rubber plantations. ## <span id="page-63-0"></span>05. Discussion, conclusions, and recommendations

### 5.1. Discussion

Current operational practices within the Vietnamese rubber industry supply chain do not require the same level of detail and strictness as the EUDR in terms of storing evidence or providing information across the entire supply chain to ensure traceability. This practice has been in place for a long time and does not violate existing legal regulations. However, preparing all supply chain actors to meet EUDR requirements will take time, as all parties need to understand the EUDR and its implications and prepare for the necessary changes to ensure deforestation-free rubber production.

While the EUDR does not place direct obligations on smallholders, they remain a critical part of the supply chain and are indirectly aected by the regulation. In particular, significant support is needed to help smallholders meet requests from buyers to collect EUDR-relevant information. Given their limited understanding of the regulation, as well as constraints in capacity, habits, and financial and technical resources, targeted assistance for smallholders is essential—especially to enhance their awareness and readiness to contribute to EUDR application throughout supply chains.

The EUDR is relatively new and stakeholder interest in the regulation remains low. Most smallholders, intermediate raw material dealers, and processors are not fully aware of the EUDR and its implications, do not fully understand its requirements, and are not yet prepared to meet them.

The model linking processors, traders, and households/groups of smallholders, along with sustainable management certificates, is an initiative with significant potential to help smallholders and traders prepare for EUDR application. As the information fields in certification standards are updated to align with EUDR requirements, meeting the requirements for a sustainable certificate brings only some of the households closer to EUDR alignment. The gaps in meeting EUDR requirements (within the sustainable certification standards) are expected to be fewer. The existing linkage models often benefit from external technical and financial support, particularly from European customers/importers, demonstrating the shared responsibility of all parties in the supply chain and the ability to leverage support resources to achieve common goals - especially supporting rubber smallholders and traders.

### <span id="page-65-0"></span>5.2. Conclusion

Viet Nam has taken the first steps to prepare supply chain actors for the EUDR. Since mid-2024, the government, MAE, and several international organisations have been promoting EUDR-related activities. However, the EUDR has, generally, not received enough attention from stakeholders, ranging from local authorities to supply chain participants. Understanding of the regulation among these groups is very limited. One reason for this is that dissemination and training activities related to the EUDR have been scarcely implemented at the provincial level, impacting most businesses, traders, and households. The lack of funding for implementation is a key factor contributing to this situation.

Estate plantations have complete documentation proving their ownership and the legality of production, with detailed and clear information on areas, boundaries, and geographic coordinates. However, the majority of small plantations owned by farmers lack geolocation data, except for a few areas supported by processing enterprises that provide technology, human resources, and financial assistance to collect the coordinates, following the linkage model between processors and traders. Additionally, some household-owned areas do not have documentation confirming their ownership and are still involved in boundary disputes.

Smallholders and traders who purchase raw latex play a crucial role in the Vietnamese rubber supply chain. These groups require external support to improve this situation and prepare for EUDR implementation.

Long and complex supply chains, which mix dierent sources of raw materials - including those of unclear legality, origin, and both domestic and imported theft - remain common. This situation increases the challenge of meeting the legality and deforestation-free requirements of the EUDR in the overall value chain.

Some of the initiatives being implemented to support EUDR readiness include enterprises collaborating with traders and smallholders to enhance supply chain transparency, shortening supply chains, and classifying raw material sources as EUDR-compliant or non-compliant during procurement. Sustainable forest management certification providers are revising their standards to align with the EUDR, thereby assisting enterprises in obtaining certification and facilitating EUDR compliance. Some enterprises have committed to supplying EUDR-compliant products to EU customers and importers. However, the number of enterprises, households, and rubber-growing areas participating in these initiatives remains small compared to the total number of these entities across the country.

Vietnamese government bodies and private sector actors downstream in the supply chain could promote multi-channel communication to enhance awareness and understanding of the EUDR, especially amongst local enterprises, local management ocials, traders, and smallholders at the provincial, district, and commune levels.

Viet Nam is already supported by initiatives such as the SAFE and EUDR ENG projects. Focus should be on smallholder rubber farmers and intermediate raw rubber dealers, strengthening their capacity, improving access to resources, and involving them in the sustainable rubber supply chain to ensure long-term impact and alignment with Viet Nam's development goals.

A mechanism should be developed to encourage the development of farmer outreach and partnership through groups of farmer households or partnerships among processors, traders, and households/ household groups. This mechanism could be developed by both public authorities and private sector stakeholders in Viet Nam, working together to rubber enterprises, could contribute with outreach through technical development, implementation, and ongoing maintenance of the app.

Participating in audits for sustainability management certifications compatible with the EUDR presents a valuable opportunity for private sector stakeholders to use synergies to provide and collect data relevant to the EUDR context. However, it is crucial to recognise that the EUDR does not consider any certification scheme a green light for EUDR compliance. While certain certification schemes might be helpful tools for data collection, they are not a substitute for the due diligence obligations of operators. Widely recognised sustainability standards do not fully meet all EUDR requirements. If supply chain actors can ensure deforestation-free value chains in line with EUDR requirements through other eective means, and their customers do not specifically require sustainability certification, obtaining such certification is unnecessary. This clarification should be widely communicated amongst Vietnamese supply chain actors to prevent misunderstanding.

Local authorities could facilitate the issuance of LURCs for rubber plantation areas that have been stably used by households for a long time, are free of disputes, and align with existing land use planning. The updated LURC format, which includes geographical coordinates, not only serves as legal proof of ownership but also helps reduce the costs of providing evidence for deforestation-free and legal commodity production in line with EUDR requirements. For eligible areas where LURCs cannot be immediately granted, the government could provide clear guidance and agree on alternative forms of ownership and land-use verification. This would prevent households from being excluded from the supply chain and mitigate potential negative socio-economic impacts on smallholder farmers.

Vietnamese regulatory agencies could expedite the development of a unified and synchronised land database and assign production codes to all rubber plantations and other crops. Additionally, a mechanism for sharing information on sourcing areas could be established to support stakeholders with legitimate and legal needs to ensure compliance

with the national legislative framework for legal and deforestation-free commodity production.

Information on FDI companies in the rubber industry remains very limited, with Vietnamese customs records on imports and exports being the only accessible source. Given their significant role in Viet Nam's rubber supply chain, greater transparency and active participation from these companies will be essential for traceability along the value chain. Government stakeholders should establish a mechanism to encourage coordination and information sharing from FDI companies, starting with European FDI firms.

Ensuring full traceability across the entire supply chain in a short period is challenging. Therefore, processors and exporters should ensure that EUDR-compliant and non-compliant products (including products of unknown origin) are not mixed along the value chain. Additionally, rubber latex is typically extracted when trees reach 7 to 8 years of age. This means that the latex used in rubber products exported to the EU until the end of 2026 is sourced from rubber trees planted before 31 December 2020. Therefore, the latex collected after 2026 will need to be cross-checked for potential risks of deforestation.

Government agencies and private sector stakeholders could establish a mechanism to encourage smallholders to organise themselves into household groups operating independently alongside the existing partnership model between smallholder farmers and enterprises under cooperatives or cooperative groups. This would enhance supply chain transparency, shorten distribution channels, and increase the capacities of stakeholders to produce deforestation-free commodities. Development institutions and non-governmental organisations can play a key role in supporting this initiative.

### Appendix 8. Rubber plantation area and proportion in Viet Nam by estate and small plantation, 1990-2023

After peaking in 2015, Viet Nam's rubber plantation area has gradually declined in the subsequent years. In 2023, the total rubber planting area nationwide dropped to 911,200 hectares, the smallest area recorded between 2015 and 2023. Despite this decrease, Viet Nam still maintains the fifth-largest rubber plantation area in the world*105*.

Viet Nam's rubber plantations are managed by both enterprises (estate plantations) and individuals (small plantations). Enterprises involved in rubber planting typically operate large areas, ranging from several hundred to thousands of hectares, which are referred to as estate plantations. These enterprises include (i) state-owned enterprises, such as those under the VRG and those managed by local authorities; (ii) private enterprises, and; (iii) FDI enterprises*106*.

Small plantations are managed by individuals or households on their own land (usually covering less than 10 hectares per household). These plantations are often scattered in small plots around residential areas. According to the general survey of rural, agricultural, and fishery data by the GSO in 2016, there are around 265,000 smallholders in Viet Nam*107*.

### Appendix 10. Viet Nam's latex output and proportion by estate and small plantations, 2015–2023 (thousand tons, dry weight)

Viet Nam's annual latex harvest area has consistently accounted for around 70% to 80% of the total rubber-planting area in recent years. Between 2015 and 2023, the country's annual latex output consistently exceeded 1 million tons (dry weight). Latex production showed steady growth during this period, with an average annual increase of over 4%. In 2022, total national output reached nearly 1.34 million tons, the highest level ever recorded. However, in 2023, rubber output decreased to 1.27 million tons, representing a 5.1% decline compared to 2022. This decrease is attributed to unfavourable weather conditions (unusual heat, storms, and floods), which damaged many harvested areas. Additionally, low market prices led some plantation owners to temporarily suspend or reduce the frequency of latex tapping, and some latex-producing areas were repurposed for other land uses. Despite this decline, Viet Nam remains the fourth-largest natural rubber producer globally (after Thailand, Indonesia, and the Ivory Coast)*111*. In 2023, Viet Nam's natural rubber output accounted for 9.1% of the world's total supply*<sup>112</sup>* .

Between 2015 and 2023, areas consistently supplied larger volumes of latex than estate plantation areas, with both volume and proportion steadily increasing. Specifically, rubber latex output from smallholders accounted for 57% to 63% of the total domestic latex supply. This trend is primarily due to the annual growth in small plantation areas, as well as significantly higher productivity on small plantations compared to estate plantations. In other words, smallholders' contribution to the raw rubber supply has become increasingly vital for Viet Nam's rubber processing and trading industry.

### Appendix 12. Import turnover of rubber products in Viet Nam, 2019 – 2023 (million USD)

Imported rubber has become an increasingly vital component of Viet Nam's rubber industry. From 2019 to 2022, Viet Nam's import turnover of rubber products, including both raw rubber and finished rubber products, grew steadily, rising from over USD 2.69 billion in 2019 to nearly USD 5 billion in 2022 (a more than 1.8-fold increase). However, in 2023, the import value dropped to nearly USD 2.9 billion, reflecting a sharp decline of over 41% compared to the previous year.

Between 2019 and 2023, raw rubber accounted for an average of over 56% of the total import turnover of rubber products in Viet Nam. The remaining 43% came from synthetic rubber.

### Appendix 13. Import turnover of Viet Nam's key rubber products, 2018-2022

From 2019 to 2023, Viet Nam imported rubber commodities (including raw rubber and rubber products) from over 90 markets. The top five suppliers, in terms of turnover, were Cambodia, China, Thailand, South Korea, and Japan. Together, these markets contributed to an average of more than 74% of Viet Nam's total annual import turnover in the rubber industry.

Additionally, Viet Nam imported rubber from 18 countries in Africa, although the import turnover from these nations was comparatively low in comparison with other supply sources. In 2022, the year with the highest import turnover from Africa during this period, Viet Nam's imports from the continent totalled just under USD 23 million.

Viet Nam imports a total of 22 rubber products, six of which fall under raw rubber, while 16 are categorised as rubber products. The top four most imported products are: natural rubber (HS 4001), synthetic rubber (HS 4002), rubber spare parts (HS 4016), and tyres (HS 4011). These four products consistently represented over 70% of Viet Nam's total annual import turnover between 2018 and 2022.

### Appendix 14. Import volume and value of Viet Nam's natural rubber (HS 4001), 2019-2023

Among the six categories of raw rubber products imported by Viet Nam, including natural rubber (HS 4001), synthetic/artificial rubber (HS 4002), natural mixed synthetic rubber (HS 400280), recycled rubber (HS 4003), waste rubber (HS 4004), and other mixed rubber (HS 4005), natural rubber (HS 4001) and synthetic/artificial rubber (HS 4002) represent the largest import values. Together, these two products account for more than 95% of total annual import value. However, it is important to note that synthetic/artificial rubber (HS 4002) is derived from petroleum and combined with various chemicals, not natural rubber. Therefore, within the context of this report related to the EUDR, the following section will focus solely on the volume, turnover, and supply of natural rubber (HS 4001).

Viet Nam's imports of natural rubber products primarily consist of technically specified rubber, centrifuged latex, and ribbed smoked sheet. The volume and import turnover of these products saw significant growth from 2019 to 2021. In 2022, import volume remained nearly equivalent to 2021 levels. However, in 2023, it declined sharply by more than 28% compared to the previous year.
