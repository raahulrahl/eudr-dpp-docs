# <span id="page-5-0"></span>**EXECUTIVE 01. SUMMARY**

#### **01. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY**

The European Union (EU) introduced the EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR) to reduce its contribution to global deforestation by setting stricter criteria for commodities linked to deforestation. Under this regulation, supply chain operators must establish robust traceability systems that can track products from farms to end users, including providing plot-level geolocation data. To meet this requirement, eective traceability systems and tools are essential. This study aims to provide an overview and assessment of Vietnamese traceability tools and systems across agricultural commodities with an emphasis on coee and timber value chains.

![](_page_6_Picture_3.jpeg)

#### **Key findings**

Viet Nam serves as a major processing hub in global coee and timber supply chains, and the EU is an important market for both coee and timber sectors. Smallholders are key suppliers of raw materials for both sectors. While coee is primarily sourced from domestic smallholders, timber originates from both domestic (approx. 75%) and imported (approx. 25%) sources.

**In Viet Nam multiple traceability systems and tools for agricultural commodities have been established, but scope, coverage and integration remain limited for meeting EUDR requirements.** Public systems such as the Production Unit Code (PUC) and the Viet Nam Timber Legality Assurance System (VNTLAS) provide a regulatory foundation. At the same time private certification systems are also used. In 2025 around 30% of coee and roughly 15% of plantation timber production are captured under recognised schemes that collect geolocation and/or legality data. The remaining smallholder coee and timber producers remain outside certification systems. Institutional overlap, limited digital interoperability, and inconsistent data governance continue to constrain national traceability coverage. Although the VNTLAS cannot fully meet the information requirements set by the EUDR, it provides useful lessons and highlights the fact that due diligence and a functional traceability system are not simply technical add-ons but a core element that underpins legality and sustainability commitments.

**Institutional and technical characteristics reflect critical technical constraints, including gaps in geolocation precision, legal documentation, and data ownership.** The EUDR requires six-digit coordinate accuracy, and among others, evidence of legal land use. Most existing systems used in Viet Nam store point coordinates but not polygons, and smallholder land-use documentation is often incomplete or outdated. Inter-agency coordination remains largely ad hoc, leaving unclear responsibilities for data verification and system maintenance. Despite these gaps, Viet Nam's legal

framework for the VNTLAS and Forest Resources Monitoring System (FRMS) oers an important platform for scaling traceability beyond timber to agricultural commodities.

**The assessment of traceability tools developed or used in Viet Nam for agricultural commodities and their relevance to EUDR traceability requirements shows that fragmentation and a lack of unified data standards limit information-sharing.** The PUC demonstrates high alignment with the regulatory system and legality verification. NextFarm and TE-FOOD excel in user-friendly mobile interfaces and VietGAP and Vietrade iTrace247 support quality certification and export documentation. However, interoperability across platforms is weak, and data standards for coordinates and meta data dier. The absence of unified Application Programming Interfaces (API) or a national database limits information-sharing between public and private solutions.

**Readiness levels for traceability, i.e. for providing information according to EUDR requirements in the timber and coee sectors, varies substantially between provinces.** In producing provinces such as Gia Lai, traceability systems such as PUCs and certification systems have been applied by stakeholders for around two decades. Nevertheless, smallholders still face challenges in mapping plots and adopting digital tools. Downstream provinces such as Binh Duong, Dong Nai, and Ho Chi Minh City depend heavily on data from sourcing provinces, but verification remains dicult for buyers.

**Mixing of products are key threats to meeting EUDR requirements.** Viet Nam imports large volumes of raw timber from multiple origins and often mixes lots during processing; similar challenges occur in coee blending.

**Interoperability oers the fastest path to EUDR readiness.** A national data exchange protocol to enable data transfer between government tools and systems and private actors could make it easier for operators to provide data for buyers according to EUDR requirements without rebuilding systems from scratch. Pilots could demonstrate proof of concept and inform national scaling.

**Integration of stakeholders:** Smallholders and their associations are central to traceability development, and their inclusion will be critical for buyers to achieve EUDR compliance at scale. This includes building inclusive systems that integrate existing provincial tools, leverage cooperative networks, and align government and private sector eorts.

#### **Recommendations**

The Vietnamese Government should provide institutional and technical support to establish coordination for relevant interventions at regional, national, and local levels. This will ensure the participation of government agencies, enterprises, local authorities, NGOs, smallholders, EU importers, and other key stakeholders. These interventions should:

- Hold regional, national and local dialogues and joint traceability initiatives to increase understanding of the need for cooperation in strengthening traceability and verification of legality for coee and timber products at dierent levels.
- Strengthen the VNTLAS: It is essential to upgrade the VNTLAS to meet EUDR requirements by including geolocation or updating forest/deforestation definitions.
- Adopt a unified national geolocation data framework defining coordinate formats according to international data standards and integrate existing coee and timber data layers into existing national portals or link them with open, interoperable platforms such as FAO's WHISP to enhance the enabling framework for EUDR application.
- Support cooperative-led smallholder mapping programmes that combine GPS polygon capture, simplified land-use verification, and cost-sharing mechanisms.

- Develop a national data gateway linking public systems with private platforms and focus on clarifying stakeholders' roles, aligning systems, and dedicating resources toward shared responsibility for traceability.
- Strengthen segregation of sources for compliant and non-compliant products and verification of legality for imported timber streams.
- Invest in provincial capacity-building on digital traceability for stakeholders in Viet Nam to enhance traceability and EUDR readiness, prioritising high-export provinces.

### <span id="page-12-0"></span>**3. INTRODUCTION**

#### **3.1. Background of the EUDR and relevance for Viet Nam**

Agriculture has significantly expanded and has a close link with deforestation worldwide. It is estimated that agriculture expansion causes 90% of deforestation globally (Pendrill et al., 2022). A significant share of agricultural commodities driving deforestation are traded internationally (Benhin, 2006; Bodo et al., 2021; Franco-Solís & Montanía, 2021; Pendrill et al., 2019). Therefore, the EU has adopted the EUDR to minimise the EU's contribution to deforestation and forest degradation. The EUDR is aligned with several international declarations and EU policy goals, the German Government, and partner countries in the (sub)tropics (Indrasto et al., 2024; Truong et al., 2025). The EUDR contributes to the implementation of various important global agreements such as the Sustainable Development Goals, the Paris Agreement, the Kunming Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework, and the Glasgow Leaders' Declaration on Forests and Land Use, all of which emphasise the critical need to protect the world's forests.

According to the regulation, companies placing relevant products on the EU market or exporting them from there must ensure through due diligence that they are produced in accordance with the relevant legislation of the country of production (Art. 3b) and without deforestation or forest degradation after 31 December 2020 (Art. 3a). The EUDR aects Southeast Asian countries because they export relevant commodities such as palm oil, rubber, coee, cocoa, and timber to the EU market (Indrasto et al., 2024; Nadras et al., 2024; Simonnet, 2023; Truong et al., 2025). The EU is an important market for Vietnamese coee, rubber, and timber.

To comply with EUDR requirements, traceability is essential for verifying the legality and deforestation-free status of Vietnamese coee, rubber and timber products exported to the EU. Geographic information linking products to plots of

land is already used by part of the industry in Viet Nam and several certification organisations. Similar requirements are also a core element of many corporate zero deforestation commitments (Bui, 2020; Iwanaga et al., 2024). Remote sensing data (air photos and satellite images) or other information (e.g., photographs in the field with linked geotags and time stamps) may be used for verification whether the geolocation of declared commodities and products is linked to deforestation or not.

This study provides insights into the development, application, and interoperability of traceability tools across dierent commodities, oering recommendations to guide government agencies, private companies, and producers to improve supply chain transparency. The findings can be linked with similar studies in other countries to inform regional and global discussions on sustainable commodity trade. Eventually, the findings can contribute to strengthening Viet Nam's policy and legal framework for traceability by identifying practical pathways for harmonising national and private traceability systems with EUDR requirements.

#### <span id="page-13-0"></span>**3.2. EUDR requirements**

As stipulated by the EUDR, products must satisfy three key requirements. All products exported to the EU market must be proven to be deforestation-free, meaning they were not produced on land that was deforested or degraded after 31 December 2020. These products must comply with related legal documents from the producing countries. To demonstrate compliance, operators must exercise due diligence to ensure traceability and legality in the countries of production.

Companies, defined as "operators and traders" under the EUDR, must confirm compliance with a due diligence statement accompanying the respective product. Relevant legislation in this context means the national and sub-national laws relevant for the area of production such as land use rights, environmental protection, forest-related rules, third party rights, labour rights, human rights protected under international law, the principle of free, prior and informed consent (FPIC), tax, anti-corruption, trade and customs regulations (see Art. 2 (40) EUDR)*<sup>01</sup>* Under the EUDR, smallholders, private enterprises or government actors in producing countries do not have any direct obligations. Supply chain actors will however be asked by their business partners to provide EUDR-relevant information.

Traceability to the plot of land (with the EUDR requirement to collect geographic coordinates of plots where the commodities were produced with an accuracy of data points of at least 6 digits, and polygons for plots with more than 4 ha) is necessary to demonstrate deforestation is not occurring at a specific location (Tô & Lương, 2023). The traceability requirements apply to each batch of relevant commodities placed on the EU market or exported from there. In addition, according to Article 10 1) (i) and (j) on risk assessment, the operator must also assess the complexity of the supply chain and the risk of circumvention, in particular the risk that relevant products could have been mixed with products of unknown origin or non-compliant products.

The EUDR applies to seven agri-forestry products: cattle, cocoa, coee, natural rubber, palm oil, soy, and wood as well as certain derived products. All products covered by the EUDR are listed in Annex I. The EUDR entered into force on 29 June 2023 and will apply to larger companies from 30 December 2026.

#### <span id="page-14-0"></span>**3.3. Sources and the production of coee and timber in Viet Nam**

Viet Nam plays a dual role in the global agri-forestry commodity supply chain as both producer and processor, relying on the import of raw materials to sustain its processing industry. In 2024, the country imported 139,000 tons of coee worth USD 527 million from sources including Laos, Indonesia, Brazil, serving both production and consumption needs*<sup>02</sup>*. Similarly, Viet Nam imports significant volumes of timber to secure raw material supplies, sourcing wood from around 140 countries worldwide. In 2024, imports included 2.39 million m3 of sawn wood worth USD 923.05 million, 565,820 m3 of plywood worth USD 218.70 million, 268,520 m3 of veneer worth USD 328.45 million, and 837,900 m3 of fibreboard worth USD 201.30 million.*<sup>03</sup>* Imported wood accounted for roughly 40% of the total input materials for wood processors.

Domestic materials still account for the majority of raw inputs in the coee and timber sectors and mainly rely on smallholders. An estimated 88% of coee comes from domestic sources, while about 60% of timber is locally sourced. The raw material supply for Viet Nam's coee and plantation timber sectors primarily depends on smallholder households. The total area under coee cultivation nationwide is approximately 710,000 ha, with smallholders accounting for about 97%, involving around 700,000 farming households (General Statistics Oce, 2024; Tô & Lương, 2023). The total area of plantation forests in the country amounts to 4,730,557 hectares, of which

approximately 1,821,099 are managed by an estimated 1,100,000 households and individuals (Nguyen, 2024; Tô & Lương, 2023). Timber produced by smallholders is estimated to contribute around 60% of the total input supply for the national wood processing industry.

The cultivated land areas of smallholder farmers in the coee and timber sectors share the common characteristic of being small and fragmented. Most smallholders manage plots of about 1-2 hectares, although a few farmers have larger holdings ranging from several dozen to hundreds of hectares (Khuc et al., 2020; Truong et al., 2025). Each household often farms more than one plot of land, with some managing more than ten separate plots (Ha et al., 2025; Truong et al., 2025). On average, coee smallholders cultivate around 0.98 hectares, while smallholders in the forestry sector have an average cultivated area of about 1.3 hectares*<sup>04</sup>*

Given the complexity of both imported and domestic raw material sources, Viet Nam faces several risks. Many of the countries supplying raw materials have weak forest governance without plot-level geolocation, increasing the likelihood of high-risk wood entering the supply chain. The information flow for materials is unclear prior to importation, creating a risk of mixing compliant and non-compliant products.

#### <span id="page-15-0"></span>**3.4. Existing traceability systems for coee and timber in Viet Nam**

There is currently no legally binding national traceability system for the coee sector, and the Government does not yet require coee producers to comply with mandatory traceability regulations/national certification systems. At present, the Ministry of Agriculture and Environment (MAE) is piloting the use of PUCs under Decision 3156/QĐ-BNN-TT to strengthen farm registration and management. PUC is a coding and classification system that identifies farms, plantations, or production sites using standardised code formats. It enables traceability from farm to fork by linking geolocation data, certification databases, and quality control systems. This integration allows the Government to enforce, monitor, and implement policies across the commodity supply chain. However, this system is in the pilot phase, and has not yet been fully institutionalised or scaled nationwide.

It is estimated that voluntary certification systems are applied for around 30% of coee production nationwide. Currently, there are around ten certification systems for coee production in Viet Nam including: Fairtrade, Organic, RA/UTZ, Common Code for the Coee Community (4C) , ISO 9001:2015, HACCP, GMP, VietGAP, TCVN 4193:2014, Smithsonian Bird Friendly, and Chain of Custody (CoC) for Coee, Fruit & Vegetable industries. Four main coee certification systems in Viet Nam (Fairtrade, Organic, RA/UTZ, and 4C) were selected for analysis based on market relevance, adaptation rates in Viet Nam, data availability, and alignment with EUDR requirements.

Actors in certified coee supply chains often participate in more than one system (Đặng et al., 2025). Multi-certification helps actors reach dierent premium markets. A common feature of these certification systems is their strong emphasis on traceability to verify the origin of commodities and ensure compliance with social and environmental sustainability standards. For coee production within these systems, information can generally be traced from farm level to exporters (Đặng et al., 2025; Tô & Lương, 2023). Certified supply chains enable enterprises to maintain internal control

systems that include farm diaries, basic polygon mapping, and transaction records. However, non-certified coee still accounts for approximately 70% of total national production and is typically traded informally, relying on basic paper-based records that lack geolocation data and other key traceability information (Đặng et al., 2025).

In 2018, the Vietnamese Government signed the Voluntary Partnership Agreement on Forest Law Enforcement, Governance and Trade (VPA FLEGT) with the EU. In principle the VPA FLEGT sets out key aspects of the timber legality definition and the overall system to be put in place in Viet Nam to ensure legality along the entire value chain. After an overall system check jointly carried out by the EU and Viet Nam, FLEGT-licenced timber can be exported to the EU without further checks.

To implement the requirements of the VPA FLEGT, Viet Nam has developed the Vietnam Timber Legality Assurance System (VNTLAS). It was first mandated and stipulated by Decree 102/2020/ND-CP and other bylaws applicable to the timber value chain. Relevant bylaws require chain-of-custody (CoC) documentation and verification of legal origin for several articles. The VNTLAS is currently a paper-based system applying strict oversight with mandatory documentation at each stage of the supply chain. timber harvest, processing, and export. Decree 102 was amended on 30 September 2024 by Decree No. 120/2024/ND-CP, which revised and supplemented

The VPA FLEGT and the VNTLAS have significantly strengthened the legality of timber throughout the supply chain. It has enhanced coordination between government agencies and industry actors while raising awareness of legality standards. In line with these commitments, large exporters and certified processors have developed robust systems to maintain and present documentation for inspections and audits. Many enterprises have also adopted barcodes to track timber logs through harvesting,

transport, and processing stages (Dang & Nguyen, 2024; Seidel et al., 2012). While the VNTLAS mandates due diligence, it does not require geolocation data or evidence of deforestation-free status.

#### **4.1. Data collection**

To complete this study, information from both secondary and primary sources was gathered. Secondary data was obtained from a range of materials, including ocial reports, websites, and academic journals. Primary data was collected through semi-structured interviews with 45 key informants from government agencies, NGOs, enterprises, traders, smallholders, and others. Interviewees were selected purposively based on their direct engagement in production, trade, or oversight of traceability initiatives.

Semi-structured interviews were conducted to assess the traceability systems and tools in the coee and timber sectors. These semi-structured questions allowed for consistency across interviews while enabling participants to elaborate on context-specific challenges and practices. The study explores how smallholders and companies record and manage information related to legality and traceability-related aspects (e.g., land use, farm diaries, and production dates). It also investigates the tools used by smallholders, companies, and other relevant stakeholders to maintain information flow along the supply chain.

Missing standardised plot-level geolocation and production dates: No unified system exists for smallholder plots, limiting traceability and verification.

Divergent forest/deforestation definitions: Dierences between national definitions and EUDR criteria create risks for verifying deforestation-free production.

Fragmented data systems and weak interoperability: Existing platforms are not fully linked.

Limited smallholder capacity and digital literacy: Many smallholders lack knowledge, tools, and training for geolocation, legal verification, and digital record-keeping.

## **5. ANALYSIS OF TRACEABILITY SYSTEMS AND TOOLS**

#### **5.1. Traceability systems in Viet Nam**

Viet Nam has developed traceability systems across sectors and levels to monitor agricultural and forestry products. At the national level, the Ministry of Agriculture and Environment (MAE), in collaboration with Business Development Services (BDS) and international organisations, has created tools such as Production Unit Code (PUC), iTwood, and Forest Resources Monitoring System (FRMS) to collect geolocation data. At the local level, some provinces have partnered with companies to develop early-stage traceability tools. In the private sector, businesses are building their own traceability systems, which are mainly for internal controls. Across supply chains, paper-based systems are still widely used by operators, smallholders, and other stakeholders in Viet Nam.

#### <span id="page-28-0"></span>**5.1.1. Technical characteristics of traceability systems in Viet Nam**

Most Vietnamese systems capture point coordinates rather than standardised polygons. Polygon capture is available in the FRMS, but polygon coverage across smallholder plots is limited. Among these systems, the FRMS integrates historical remote-sensing imagery and time-series change detection; agricultural tools rarely have historical, verifiable satellite layers embedded by default.

Both public and private sectors have dierent mechanisms to capture and link data from tools to the traceability system. These traceability systems apply both online and o ine information updates for traceability. While some systems integrate real-time information updates (e.g., NextFarm), others provide mobile apps with o ine capture and later synchronisation (e.g., FRMS). Remote sensing and automated change detection are more advanced in forestry than in agriculture.

Systems vary in their capacity to attach legal documents such as Land Use Right Certificates (LURCs). In the timber sector, VNTLAS requires specific legality documentation for timber supply chains. In the coee sector; however, many coee systems rely on certification documents rather than statutory proofs. Certification schemes (FSC, Common Code for the Coee Community - 4C, and Rainforest Alliance - RA) embed third-party audits, while public systems depend on government verification.

Private platforms oer APIs and export functions, while national public systems (PUC, FRMS, VNTLAS) are traditionally operated in silos with limited data exchange. Recent pilot initiatives aim to establish API gateways and a national interoperability framework, though full implementation is still in progress.

![](_page_28_Picture_6.jpeg)

#### <span id="page-29-0"></span>**5.1.2. Institutional characteristics**

Most traceability systems have no legal obligations except for the timber sector and some agricultural commodities covered by PUC. Timber traceability is anchored in the VNTLAS with clear legal obligations and ocial verification mandates. The government agency administers forest monitoring through the FRMS and coordinates legality checks. PUCs are mandatory for labelled agricultural commodities. Coee traceability, in contrast, is driven by voluntary certification (4C, RA, and Organic). Custodianship is therefore fragmented among cooperatives, certifiers, private platform providers, and provincial authorities.

Government agencies (e.g., Department of Forest and Forest protection, VNFOREST) hold sectoral responsibilities, but cross-ministerial data governance and a single authority for supporting

EUDR readiness are still maturing. Exporters and processors implement digital traceability systems, SMEs and traders lag due to cost and capacity constraints. NGOs, cooperatives, and private service providers support mapping and data collection on a project basis.

Most commodities lack legal frameworks for traceability systems except for the timber sector. VNTLAS provides a statutory chain-of-custody and legality framework integrated with customs and export controls, lowering friction for EUDR compliance. Agricultural traceability relies on the PUC system under Decision 3156/QĐ-BNN-TT, which is still being scaled up across provinces. It can be observed that the EUDR acts as a major driver of reforms in the agricultural sector.

#### **5.1.3. Key dierences between coee and timber traceability**

The timber sector has a robust statutory framework (VNTLAS) with enforcement mechanisms, while the coee sector relies on voluntary systems and is driven by markets. Timber data must link to legal permits, whereas coee data are fragmented across smallholders with limited polygon mapping. Transaction flows in timber are document-heavy and formal; coee transactions are more informal and

dispersed. The risk of mixing is significant in both sectors through timber imports and coee blending, but stronger chain-of-custody controls exist for timber. There is an incentive dierence between coee and timber because timber compliance is legally required, but coee traceability depends on certification premiums and market pressure.

#### <span id="page-30-0"></span>**5.2. Deep dive: EUDR and VNTLAS**

The VNTLAS provides a solid basis and can be built upon to create a legal framework for timber products to meet EUDR requirements. In addition, the VNTLAS supports traceability by requiring actors in the timber supply chain to provide information on the origin of timber production.

The VPA FLEGT establishes a comprehensive definition of "legal timber". This includes timber or timber products that are harvested, imported, transported, traded, processed, or exported in

accordance with Vietnamese law, relevant international treaties to which Viet Nam is a signatory, and relevant laws of the country or territory where the timber is harvested, transited, and exported to Viet Nam. This means that Vietnamese operators are already engaged in processes to ensure timber meets the "relevant legislation of the country of production" requirement of the EUDR, which includes aspects like land-use rights, environmental protection, and forest-related rules.

The VNTLAS mandates the use of packing lists for imported and exported timber and timber origin declarations, and requires enterprises to maintain logbooks for purchases and sales and submit regular reports. #### <span id="page-33-0"></span>**5.3. Enabling factors and challenges for traceability in Viet Nam related to EUDR readiness**

#### **5.3.1. Enabling factors**

Existing certification systems and legal frameworks provide a strong foundation for the coee and timber sectors to meet the requirements set out in the EUDR. They play a critical role in establishing systems for information-sharing and oer valuable lessons for strengthening traceability. Importantly, these systems and frameworks can be expanded and adapted to cover conventional coee and timber production.

**Export market incentives:** The EU is a highly valuable market for both Viet Nam's coee and timber sectors. In 2024, the export value of coee to the EU is estimated at around USD 2.5 billion, while timber exports to the EU are valued at approximately USD 1.1 billion.

**Existing certification systems:** In the coee sector, certifications cover about 30% of total coee production. Meanwhile, the timber sector relies on FSC and PEFC certifications, which account for approximately 15% of plantation forest areas. These certified supply chains already collect some geolocation data and maintain farm diaries for internal control systems, providing a solid basis for meeting sustainability and traceability requirements.

**Experiences on strict traceability:** Exporters and processors in certification systems must segregate certified and non-certified production to avoid mixing and ensure clear traceability. This practice provides positive examples on how to ensure strict traceability along the whole coee and timber supply chains.

**Government push for digitalisation:** The Vietnamese Government is encouraging all sectors to adopt digital applications for improved management and oversight, with agriculture being one of its key priorities. The Government has piloted PUCs and iTwood tools to track commodities, demonstrating both the technical capacity and the potential to scale up digital solutions across a wider range of supply chains.

**Strong legal framework for timber:** The Vietnamese Government has implemented the VNTLAS under the VPA FLEGT, and many operators already meet these requirements some of which align with EUDR criteria. Therefore, operators are already familiar with the processes for traceability, legality, and the necessary paperwork for compliance.

![](_page_33_Picture_7.jpeg)

#### <span id="page-34-0"></span>**5.3.2. Challenges**

The existing certification systems and the VNTLAS provide a strong foundation and help to meet EUDR requirements. However, there remain challenges related to traceability and legality at every node of the supply chain.

**Insucient geolocations:** Many conventional coee farms and timber plantations are highly fragmented and lack geolocation data, leading to disrupted information flows and, at times, mixing problems.

**Complicated land tenure:** Viet Nam has a complex land-use system (Aldinger et al., 2024; Le et al., 2024), and land plots often do not have geolocations. While geolocation can technically be identified using GPS or satellite data, the challenge lies in linking these coordinates to legally recognised land parcels and producers, especially where land use rights are undocumented or overlapping.

**Traditional paper-based records:** Many operators still rely on paper-based records, which increases the workload needed to manage and integrate data into digital traceability tools or to connect records with plot-level geolocation information.

**Fragmented traceability systems:** Viet Nam has various traceability systems developed by the Government and private sectors with no unified standards, leading to inconsistent information flows across sectors and regions.

**Imported timber mixing risk:** Uncertified imported timber poses a clear risk of being mixed with verified "low-risk" timber streams if there is not robust segregation and controls in processing facilities.

#### **6.1. Smallholders**

#### **6.1.1. Geolocation and production documentation**

In both sectors, the interviewed smallholders have limited capacity to identify and record the geolocations of their land parcels. Some smallholders can collect and store the geolocations of their plots on maps and share this information with others to locate their farms. However, many were unable to mark or record the locations of their land parcels using maps or apps. Even when shown how to identify their plots on maps or apps, they often could not save or document the geolocation information. In both sectors, most smallholders involved in conventional coee cultivation and plantation forestry rely mainly on traditional practices and personal experience. Surveyed households typically base their farming methods on habits and knowledge gained from previous years.

Only a small number of households keep records related to planting and maintenance, usually as part of certification programmes. Interviewed smallholders in both sectors participating in certification programmes use either paper-based or digital tools to maintain farm diaries. Some record information in notebooks such as fertilising, weeding, post-harvest processing, selling, economic returns, inputs costs, labours, labour wages, and other social and environmental aspects with clear start and end dates. This data is then shared with traders who link smallholders to enterprises involved in certification systems.

In Viet Nam's coee sector, some smallholder groups use mobile applications such as Nestlé's FFB (Digital Farmer Field Book) to manage farm diaries within certified supply chains. The app allows users to record detailed farming activities, calculate quantities and costs, and generate QR codes

containing personal and plot information. Farmers log in using registered phone numbers linked to Nestlé's database. While the system is user-friendly, the interview showed that nine out of ten did not record information for each separate land parcel, while the remaining household owned only one parcel. In addition to keeping farm diaries, smallholders in certification programmes are also required to share personal information, including national ID and land-use certificate details, with traders and enterprises.

In the coee sector, two out of six interviewed smallholders were hesitant to use online apps for various reasons. They expressed concerns about scams, which are common and can result in financial loss, especially because they have limited experience managing online tools. They worried that pressing the wrong button could expose personal or confidential information, putting them at risk of identity theft or other problems. As a result, they were reluctant to use apps for managing farming activities. In addition, many smallholders have low levels of formal education, and diculties with grammar and spelling make them uncomfortable. They fear that errors in their digital records could lead to embarrassment, further discouraging them from using such tools.

#### <span id="page-38-0"></span>**6.1.2. Internet and smart equipment**

Good internet access and smartphone use in Viet Nam result in a high rate of internet access and smart devices. All interviewed smallholders had access to the internet through Wi-Fi or 4G networks, enabling them to use smart devices for farm management. According to the Ministry of Information and Communications, 88.7% of mobile subscribers use smartphones, 82.3% of households have fibre-optic broadband Internet access, and the 4G network covers 99.8% of the country's territory*<sup>06</sup>* . This high user rate could enable smallholders to regularly update farm management information.

In both sectors, three out of eight interviewed smallholders in certification programmes can handle smart equipment to collect information about land parcels in their farms. Smallholders make use of smart phones to access Apps for land measurement, geolocations, and farm dairies. However, interviewed companies stated that many smallholders require support from cooperative operators to update and keep records and information.

#### <span id="page-39-0"></span>**6.1.3. Cooperation with companies/state management units**

In the coee sector, the long-standing implementation of certification systems has fostered strong institutional collaboration among stakeholders, particularly in data sharing and traceability reporting (Đặng et al., 2025; Đặng & Trần, 2022). Currently, producers often learn about the EUDR through intermediaries and lack a clear understanding of its specific requirements. Fragmented information from various stakeholders adds to smallholders' confusion when procedures are requested under the umbrella of EUDR requirements. As mentioned above, there are significant challenges for smallholders in providing relevant information for buyers and operators for due diligence under the EUDR. Only one out of ten interviewed smallholders mentioned that they mapped their farms themselves with technical support from local companies. Future studies with larger samples are recommended to quantify the extent of these practices nationwide.

#### <span id="page-40-0"></span>**6.2. Enterprises and local traders**

#### **6.2.1. Role of enterprises and local traders**

Vietnamese enterprises play a central role in connecting information between smallholders and overseas buyers, while also facilitating the movement of commodities to the EU market. Vietnamese enterprises have an interest to provide documentation to enable EU importers to fulfil their due diligence obligations. As a result, Vietnamese enterprises have been actively supporting smallholders and local traders by developing apps and collecting information to demonstrate the traceability and legality of commodities produced by smallholders. These practices are primarily observed among enterprises and traders engaged in certification systems (e.g., 4C, RA, FSC, VFCS) or

through VNTLAS requirements. Outside these frameworks, most enterprises continue to use conventional, paper-based documentation, and traceability remains limited to basic transaction records rather than plot-level verification.

Local traders play a key role in connecting smallholders with enterprises by facilitating both the flow of commodities and the exchange of information. They are often residents who have in-depth knowledge of the areas where they purchase coee and timber. Traders help enterprises gather farm locations and sizes from smallholders and provide guidance on how to supply the required information.

#### **6.2.2. Legal documentation**

Companies in both the coee and timber sectors actively collect documentation to verify the legality of their products. Coee enterprises primarily gather information related to land use right certificates, while timber companies must compile a broader and more complex set of legal documents to meet VNTLAS regulatory requirements. The Forest Protection Department at provincial level has sometimes even established a sustainability team responsible for providing guidance and coordinating with other departments and stakeholders to ensure operations meet VNTLAS and future EUDR requirements. This team carries out the assessment (enterprise classification system) and oversee compliance with the VNTLAS based on documentation provided by the companies. Verifying timber legality throughout the value chain is therefore critical for timber companies. The verification process mainly relies on scanned paper along the supply chain.

#### **6.2.3. Geolocations**

In the coee sector, local traders collaborate with Vietnamese enterprises to collect farm-level geolocation data, with active participation from smallholders in certified supply chains. Many enterprises have established dedicated sustainability teams responsible for verifying and mapping farm locations. Local traders compile lists of smallholders supplying raw materials, including identification numbers, addresses, and land parcel areas. Using these lists, the sustainability teams, assisted by traders, collect geolocation data directly from smallholders through mobile applications.

In the timber sector, many companies are currently able to provide geolocation information as required by the EUDR, but challenges remain. Some interviewed companies have submitted a set of documents to EU buyers for verification and are still awaiting feedback to confirm whether the data is sucient and acceptable. It also remains unclear whether there are any gaps in geolocation or legal information among dierent suppliers. The availability and accuracy of geolocation data depends largely on the capacity and knowledge of smallholders. In both coee and timber sectors, while provincial forest planning maps exist, they rely on point-based locations rather than polygon mapping, which makes it dicult to assess overlaps between cultivation areas and forest land. As a result, companies are currently unable to fully confirm or resolve overlapping claims of suppliers, and identifying the exact geolocations of smallholder land parcels remains a major challenge.

#### <span id="page-42-0"></span>**6.2.4. Production tracking for coee, timber and collaboration with smallholders**

Currently, enterprises in the coee sector use two traceability approaches to track the flow of commodities from smallholders to enterprises: paper-based and app-based tools. While paper-based tools are straightforward and easy to use, they create a significant workload for sta. Software and apps, on the other hand, are more ecient for managing large amounts of data quickly. However, limited digital skills of sta and smallholders for consistent data recording and flow make it dicult for enterprises to fully implement these tools across all stakeholders. Additionally, scattered internet access in remote areas can interrupt updates from farm level.

Traders typically use a combination of paper-based records and Microsoft Oce tools to track information for coee and timber. Each certification system has its own paper-based forms with specific fields designed to meet specific requirements. Traders usually purchase certified commodities according to quotas agreed upon with smallholders and enterprises before the harvest season. They only buy the agreed certified quantity; any excess is refused. When smallholders have multiple plots under the same certification system, traders generally do not separate (i.e. record/track) commodities by individual plots. After purchasing commodities from smallholders, traders manually enter the information into Excel files to maintain records and track data. In certified supply chains, enterprises may also receive information directly from smallholders through dedicated apps.

To ensure traceability in coee production, companies have also established a physical system for segregating coee. Certified and non-certified coee are stored in separate areas and distinguished by dierent bag colours and coverings. Within each category, coee from various smallholders is blended, with clear records indicating the proportion contributed by each smallholder. A list of contributing smallholders is attached to every coee shipment.

Companies also created a manual traceability system for the flow of information during transportation for export. Each coee shipment has a label/code and the list of involved stakeholders, which enables traceability. Local companies provide information through PDF files; i.e., they do not have QR or bar codes for traceability.

Companies in the timber sector procure their materials from domestic and international sources. They often implement a traceability system to monitor timber during transportation. Materials are transported in GPS-tracked trucks, with invoices and necessary paperwork for the transported logs maintained to prevent the mixing of dierent materials. Two marking methods are used to directly mark the logs: paint and tagging. Suppliers apply paint markings and attach the necessary documentation (such as invoices and timber declarations), while company uses tags to categorise and manage wood in storage. Some materials are sourced using business intelligence tools, enabling companies to manage and trace imported wood.

Interviewed companies participating in certification systems have invested considerable resources in upgrading their digital databases and documentation systems, which also strengthens their readiness for meeting EUDR requirements. Interviewed companies in the timber sector for this study rely on paper-based traceability systems to track information for wood products exported to the EU. However, documentation for input materials remains sketchy across suppliers. Codes for sub-areas and land parcels are often insucient to verify exact geolocations, making traceability unreliable. Although shipment documents list the stakeholders involved, they frequently lack geolocation data. Additionally, companies must prepare multiple sets of documents containing the same information in dierent formats to meet the requirements of various government agencies.

Although many companies participate in two certification systems and have invested in improving their traceability systems, they still face significant challenges in collecting the data needed to demonstrate deforestation-free timber. Certification systems provide a foundational platform for upgrading traceability and supporting satisfaction with the EUDR, but the current paper-based system cannot integrate all required information.

Regarding collaboration with smallholders, it can be noted that in the coee sector, interviewed enterprises working within certification systems have been actively supporting smallholders in using digital tools to collect geolocation and track production. These enterprises are developing user-friendly apps to help smallholders more easily record geolocation

data, land sizes, and other relevant farming information. Their dedicated teams play a key role in providing training or coordinating with trainers to build the capacity of stakeholders and traders, helping to ensure a smoother flow of information from smallholders.

In the timber sector, companies are also working closely with partners and smallholders to help them meet EUDR requirements. Many companies collaborate the business development services (see chapter below) to support suppliers and traders in identifying geolocations and obtaining relevant data. For suppliers who rent land or forest areas, companies provide training to ensure accurate information flows from smallholders.

#### <span id="page-44-0"></span>**6.2.5. Traceability solutions of coee and timber companies/enterprises**

In the coee sector, FDI enterprises have established nationwide purchasing networks through partnerships with local companies and intermediaries (Đặng et al., 2025). These collaborations enable them to secure a stable supply of coee beans for processing. Consequently, FDI enterprises closely monitor and manage these partnerships to satisfy traceability and sustainability requirements.

To increase traceability, FDI enterprises use traceability tools and establish strong linkages throughout their supply chains, including close partnerships with smallholders. They build a network and supply chains in collaboration with local companies to expand certification systems. In Gia Lai Province, FDI enterprises collaborate with local companies to procure coee beans from smallholders across various communes. Local companies play a key role in connecting smallholders to FDI enterprises' traceability tools. Smallholders use the apps developed by FDI enterprises to ensure real-time information. These specialised traceability solutions increase the consistency of data from smallholders to exporters.

Local companies in the coee sector cooperate with FDI enterprises to support sourcing and certification processes. They do not conduct due diligence on their behalf. These companies assist in collecting farm-level information, verifying documentation, and managing certification records within their supply networks. Their role focuses on providing data required for traceability to buyers while ensuring compliance with certification standards.

Local companies developed their own approaches to manage procurement information, which is complemented by geolocation data collected through professional mapping applications. Since

the only procurement-related information is collected through apps, geolocation data is added from geographical professional apps or tools such as Land Measure Pro and GPS. Dedicated teams are responsible for collecting farm-level geolocation data and producing detailed maps for each area. Smallholders support this process by cooperating with companies, guiding sta to their coee orchards so that company employees can use GPS devices to record accurate geolocation information. Dedicated teams are responsible for farm mapping, while overall due diligence responsibility remains with the purchasing enterprise.

In addition, local companies have also developed QR codes to enhance the traceability of coee supply chains. These codes allow them to compile additional data fields for each coee shipment, providing more comprehensive information to buyers. Customers are required to pay a fee to access the detailed information embedded in the QR codes for each shipment.

Alongside their software and online systems, local companies continue to use manual traceability systems based on Microsoft Oce tools. Each smallholder has purchasing sheets that detail the price, quantity, and total value of each transaction. Companies cannot fully monitor whether smallholders mix certified coee with production from non-certified sources. For smallholders with multiple parcels of land, the company issues unique and non-repeating codes for each parcel, ensuring consistency from the parcel level to the company level. #### <span id="page-46-0"></span>**6.2.6. Concerns from enterprises in coee and timber sectors**

In both coee and timber sectors, interoperability across national and international traceability systems is limited, as dierent supply chain actors often demand dierent data fields and formats. Data flow between suppliers and processors is fragmented, and IT systems are not integrated. Thus, companies must generate multiple sets of documents with overlapping content in varying formats to satisfy dierent authorities and clients. Enterprises are concerned about how to align their internal systems (e.g., FSC codes) with EUDR requirements if suppliers use dierent codes or formats. Interviewed companies demonstrated that EUDR compliant wood sources, i.e., being able to provide the full set of information required, are much more expensive, raising the cost of production for exporters.

In coee and timber sectors, there is limited motivation and awareness of the EUDR and the requirements on the EU market. Smallholders often lack awareness of the concrete benefits for them or see it as irrelevant for domestic sales, leading to limited cooperation with companies to provide essential data for traceability. As mentioned earlier, smallholders might be hesitant to collect and share information digitally as the actual use remains unclear.

Additionally, for the timber sector, many smallholders see no price premium for certified or traceable wood, so they lack motivation to participate in certification systems or EUDR-compliant supply chains. In the timber sector, companies do not buy directly from smallholders but through layers of intermediaries, making it easy for certified and non-certified wood to be mixed. Documentation for domestic wood is more complete than for imports, but correct geolocation is often missing. Interviewees reported that many plots are missing verified GPS coordinates, hindering traceability to the plot level for wood products. In addition, land parcel codes do not match actual polygons, resulting in inaccurate traceability information. Interviewed companies did not know how to verify geolocations for land plots.

In the timber sector, inconsistency in terminology can cause problems for mutual understanding among operators and traceability along supply chains. There are inconsistent terminologies in domestic law such as "forest product," "wood product," and "agricultural product". This can lead to diculties for companies and stakeholders to complete required procedures to comply with regulations.

Therefore, in the timber sector, concerns remain about the reliability and accountability of supplier data. Companies fear being held responsible for false or incomplete data provided by upstream suppliers. In addition, actors must verify their own information and currently rely on trust. Interviewees demonstrated that although enterprises have made significant eorts to ensure smooth information flows, they still face several challenges in using traceability tools eectively. Some enterprises believe that smallholders should bear full responsibility for meeting the requirements of the EUDR.

From desk-based research and fieldwork, this study identified distinct challenges for both domestic and imported timber sources. Domestic timber, largely produced by smallholders, faces persistent diculties due to limited capacity and low awareness of legality and traceability requirements. These constraints hinder downstream operators from ensuring full traceability and integrity of timber production. In addition, incomplete legal documentation as required by the VNTLAS slows down verification.

Business development services (BDSs) play an important role in supporting enterprises and smallholders to participate in certification systems, build capacity, and develop traceability tools. BDSs are developing essential traceability tools to track information on agricultural commodities from individual land plots. BDSs also provide services to verify and assess the compliance of stakeholders with certification criteria. They often strengthen capacity for smallholders and operators and have a thorough understanding of the current situation regarding the capacity of smallholders and operators.

An interviewed BDS said that less than 25% of smallholders working in cooperative relationships with companies have the necessary traceability information or capacity to meet EUDR requirements. Overall, about 99% of smallholders lack the information and capacity needed to align with the EUDR and rely heavily on companies and consultants to fulfil procedural requirements*<sup>09</sup>* .

Preferred by Nature plays an important role as an international organisation providing capacity building, auditing, and digital traceability support to enterprises and smallholders in both the coee and

timber sectors. It supports implementation of FSC, PEFC, and other sustainability standards.

Consultancy firms are developing and refining app features to assist smallholders, traders, companies, and other stakeholders. These apps capture information at multiple levels, including geolocation, farm records, and other relevant data. Green Field developed a web-based app to manage farming activities from planting to selling, production and geolocation. Ecotech 2A is a consultancy firm specialising in system development and traceability solutions for agricultural commodities in Viet Nam, with a focus on the wood plantation sector. The company has piloted two digital systems in four districts across the Central Highlands. This includes the "Smallholder App" to help enterprises to collect EUDR relevant information. Its aim is to enable smallholders to independently map their land parcels, collect and upload geolocation data using smartphones, and access management information for each parcel. With the app, overlapping land-use issues can be identified. Key features include free access for smallholders (while downstream operators must pay fees), the ability to update land parcel information individually, automatic updates, and compatibility with smartphones or manual input options.

#### <span id="page-47-0"></span>**6.3. Business development services**

Support from government stakeholders is an important factor for the application of traceability tools. The Government develops national policies to meet standards for legality and traceability. The Vietnamese Government issued Decision No. 749/QD-TTg, 2020 on the National Digital Transformation Programme and the Forestry Development Strategy 2021–2030. These policies indicate the Government's commitment to enhancing digital traceability and compliance with legal requirements.

Local authorities are key for implementing government policies and systems, such as establishing PUCs and completing natural forest maps. For example, to monitor forest changes, the Department of Forest and Rangers uses the Forest Resource Monitoring System (FRMS) to track forest dynamics. This system enables stakeholders to access and compare information relevant to both the coee and timber sectors. Local authorities also help enterprises and smallholders by issuing documents needed to verify the legal status of land use and production. In short, local authorities mainly perform administrative functions and have limited direct involvement in supply chain traceability.

However, smallholders who manage most of the production area face persistent challenges related to limited digital literacy and low incentives to record data.

#### **6.8.1. Readiness of the timber sector in dierent provinces**

**Institutional framework diers between localities:** In the Central Highlands, provinces have long-standing provincial programmes promoting sustainable coee, quality control, and certification (Đặng & Trần, 2022). These provinces work with enterprises and NGOs to increase traceability. However, relatively new coee-growing provinces, such as Son La and Dien Bien in northern Viet Nam, are in the early stage of adopting traceability tools*<sup>10</sup>* .

**Infrastructure and digital capacity:** Traceability tools dier in maturity and scope of application in provinces across Viet Nam (Dang & Nguyen, 2024; International Bank for Reconstruction and Development, 2022; Le et al., 2025). Provinces in the Central Highlands and the southeast, with strong export orientation and existing certification programmes, show higher readiness, while northern and lowland provinces lag behind in digitalisation and coordination for traceability.

**Stakeholder engagement:** In the Central Highlands, operators and smallholders in certification systems, use tools to capture production data and plot-level geolocation (Đặng et al., 2025; Le et al., 2024; Truong et al., 2025). However, smallholders in other provinces operate mostly outside organised structures. Weak coordination among cooperatives, traders, and local authorities limits collective action and information-sharing.

**Certification and legality coverage:** Certification rates are high in the Central Highlands, and many certified producers already record basic geolocation, production dates, and legality verification (Đặng et al., 2025). However, other provinces with a larger non-certified share of producers often lack geolocation and production data.

**Data availability:** In the Central Highlands, smallholders in certification systems have made use of smart phones and tablets to capture plot-level information for traceability, but coverage remains limited outside certified groups. In other provinces, geolocation data is generally limited to administrative boundaries.

**Market orientation and export exposure:** Provinces with strong export orientation, particularly Dak Lak, Lam Dong, and Gia Lai, show higher readiness due to active engagement with EU buyers and sustainability-certified value chains. Conversely, northern and north-central provinces primarily serve the domestic market or non-EU buyers and thus have weaker incentives to implement EUDR-compatible traceability systems.

EUDR requirements will demand a major leap in data collection and reporting practices at the farm level. The coee sector already has a starting point with its system of voluntary certification systems, which provides a foundational platform that could be scaled up across coee-producing provinces. About 70% of Viet Nam's coee production is lacking systematic information flow and plot-level mapping. While the PUCs introduced by the MAE are a promising initiative, expanding and standardising this approach nationwide remains challenging. Lessons from the seafood and fruit sectors, particularly the use of QR codes and barcode systems, can help address these gaps. In any case, clearly any tools adopted must be easy to use, work oine, and be interoperable across dierent buyers and supply chain actors.

The timber sector can build on the existing legal framework, but gaps remain in fully aligning with EUDR requirements. The VNTLAS has established a strong foundation for ensuring the legality and

traceability of timber, with comprehensive documentation for inspection and audits. Actors are required to provide various documents, including harvesting, transport, and processing permits. However, the EUDR specifically requires plot-level geolocation data, information that is currently absent from VNTLAS procedures. Without this, operators cannot fully trace timber production to individual farms or plots. Furthermore, the risk of mixing compliant and non-compliant timber within the supply chain remains a concern. Stronger controls and full segregation throughout the supply chain will be needed to prevent mixing and to meet the EUDR's strict traceability requirements.

# **DISCUSSION AND 07. RECOMMENDATIONS**

## **07. DISCUSSION AND RECOMMENDATIONS**

This assessment highlights both the progress made and the challenges that remain for Viet Nam's coee and timber sectors in aligning with EUDR requirements. While enterprises, government agencies, and supporting organisations have taken concrete steps to build traceability systems and verification, significant gaps must still be addressed eectively.

A key strength lies in the strong export value of coee and timber, which provides clear economic incentives for stakeholders. The Government's active support, including its push for digital transformation, creates favourable conditions for modernising traceability systems. Existing certification systems also oer a practical starting point, as they already require basic traceability and legality standards. Many enterprises are leveraging these systems to build internal control systems and pilot digital tools that can be scaled to cover broader supply chains.

However, this assessment confirms that smallholders remain the most vulnerable link in the chain. Many smallholders still have limited awareness of traceability requirements under the EUDR, lack digital skills, and bear the cost of dierent activities such as geolocation mapping, record keeping, and paperwork without seeing direct benefits. This makes consistent traceability challenging, especially for non-certified production.

The flow of information through the supply chain remains insucient to guarantee traceability from smallholders to final markets. While certified supply chains have clearer internal control systems, conventional production, which still accounts for the majority, often lacks georeferenced plot data, complete land titles, or systematic record keeping. Smallholders often perceive that traceability is solely the responsibility of enterprises and traders, and many lack the capacity or incentive to maintain detailed records themselves.

Additionally, fragmented and sometimes incomplete geospatial data, the absence of a standardised national forest base map, and unclear guidelines for coordinate systems at national and local level, create technical barriers for both enterprises and auditors when verifying information in the field. The complex land tenure situation, where many plots lack formal titles or involve informal land use, further complicates legal verification. A fundamental concern is that current traceability tools are often fragmented and, in some cases, irrelevant or poorly suited to actual conditions on the ground. Many enterprises still rely heavily on paper-based systems or simple digital spreadsheets, which create administrative burdens and limit scalability. Although some apps and software tools have been developed, they are not always practical for smallholders with limited digital skills or reliable internet access. As a result, the existing tools do not fully address the real challenges of recording and verifying geolocations, production dates, or legal documentation in line with EUDR requirements.

The involvement of NGOs, service providers, and local authorities has helped to bridge some of these gaps by delivering capacity building, developing traceability tools, and supporting policy reforms. Nonetheless, local authorities mainly perform an administrative role and have limited direct engagement in supply chain operations, which constrains their ability to actively strengthen traceability on the ground.

Lessons from the VPA FLEGT process suggest that developing a robust traceability and legality assurance system requires strong coordination between government agencies, private actors, and smallholders. The experience from the timber sector with its VNTLAS shows that traceability is most eective when supported by clear legal frameworks, consistent data standards, and independent verification. Applying these lessons across sectors such as coee and other cultural commodities can promote cross-sectoral learning and harmonise approaches to

legality and sustainability. Building on existing tools could support the development of an interoperable traceability framework.

Strengthening traceability is not just about technology, it hinges on trust, legal clarity, and genuine collaboration among all stakeholders. Clear policy, robust local engagement, practical digital solutions, and fair cost-sharing models will be essential for Viet Nam's coee and timber supply chains.

To address the gaps identified in this assessment and increase participation in supply chains destined for the EU market, the following recommendations are proposed:

#### **Regional cooperation**

Regional Dialogues: Hold ASEAN-level dialogues and joint traceability initiatives to increase mutual understanding of the need for and cooperation in strengthening the traceability and legality of transboundary coee and timber products.

Interoperable systems: Adopt interoperable systems and leverage open-source initiatives, such as DIASCA, to trace the origin of coee and timber and improve interoperability.

![](_page_58_Picture_7.jpeg)

#### **At the national level**

It is essential to upgrade the VNTLAS to ensure that timber production aligns with the information requirements of the EUDR. The VNTLAS should enhance its due diligence procedures to include deforestation-free verification, strengthen supply chain traceability, and require geolocation data consistent with EUDR requirements. Digitalisation is critical to enable the real-time exchange of information among competent authorities, enterprises, and other stakeholders.

A national forest cover monitoring system could integrate risk assessment modules to evaluate deforestation and legality risks of agricultural commodities, while communicating clearly on the definitions of key terms used to avoid any misunderstandings in the EUDR context. The upgraded system should mandate the inclusion of verifiable evidence, such as satellite imagery, land-use certificates, and other supporting documentation.

Geolocation data should be systematically linked to production areas to provide a transparent and traceable record from source to export.

Agree on coordinate systems for dierent maps to ensure interoperability and strengthen commodity traceability support readiness with the EUDR.

Simplify land tenure verification: Streamline procedures for issuing, updating, and digitising LURCs and help smallholders legalise plots currently lacking formal titles in areas where they have cultivated without any conflicts.

Promote coordinates, pre-competitive collaboration among enterprises, traders, smallholders, and local authorities to develop interoperable traceability solutions at national and regional levels. Eorts should focus on clarifying stakeholders' roles, aligning systems, and dedicating resources toward shared traceability and verification objectives.

Integrate coee and timber data layers into existing national traceability portals or link them with open, interoperable platforms, such as FAO's WHISP, to enhance geospatial verification.

Strengthen border checks and require suppliers to provide EUDR-aligned due diligence information, including verified plot-level geolocation; e.g., for imported timber.

Create national coordination: Facilitate dialogue platforms to strengthen coordination among ministries and local authorities, ensuring the inclusion of all relevant agencies in preparations for EUDR readiness.

#### **Local authorities**

Go beyond administration: Proactively support smallholders to register land, verify LURCs with geolocation following the new form, and resolve land disputes.

Ensure legal and data accuracy: Issue clear, practical instructions for verifying farm and forest plot boundaries. Validate land classifications to prevent discrepancies that would undermine due diligence statements.

Act as connectors: Convene local multi-stakeholder working groups with the participation of dierent stakeholders to align traceability practices and solve local barriers collaboratively.

#### **Enterprises and exporters**

Invest in user-friendly digital tools: Develop or adapt mobile apps that allow smallholders to record plot geolocations, farm diaries, and legal documents. These tools should work oine, use simple interfaces, and support multiple local languages.

Align internal control systems with EUDR information requirements: Integrate farm-level geolocation into internal audit systems, ERPs, or blockchain pilots, ensuring certified and non-certified batches are physically and digitally segregated to prevent mixing. Support upstream actors: Cooperate with traders and cooperatives to help smallholders map plots and upload documents. Oer technical and financial support where needed, for example, by co-financing GPS mapping or paying premiums for EUDR-compliant timber or coee.

Cross-sector learning: Adapt good practices from fruit and seafood traceability (for example, using QR codes for batch tracking) and customise for coee and timber.

#### **For smallholders and cooperatives**

Organise for cost-sharing: Form or strengthen cooperatives and farmer groups to share costs of mapping, certification, and maintaining traceability records.

Adopt digital tools: Engage actively in capacity-building and digital literacy programs to use mobile apps or cooperative-managed digital platforms, keep plot-level records, update farming activities, and store legal documents to meet buyer requirements.

Benefit from incentives: Take advantage of certification systems, premiums, and partnership oers that support better market access and price dierentiation for traceable, deforestation-free commodities.

#### **Other related stakeholders (BDS providers, NGOs, and associations)**

Expand training and follow-up support: Deliver tailored capacity-building programmes for smallholders, local traders, and community leaders on plot mapping and digital traceability.

Facilitate knowledge transfer: Organise cross-sector workshops where lessons from the seafood and fruit sectors are shared with timber processors, coee exporters, and farmer cooperatives.

Promote interoperability: Engage with global buyers and invest in interoperable traceability tools to promote their adoption by suppliers and buyers.

#### **For operators**

Provide clear technical guidance: Issue user-friendly manuals that specify the acceptable formats for plot-level geolocation (e.g., shapefiles, GPS coordinates, standard projection).

Work with local partners to provide instructions on information needs in Vietnamese; clarify data required for due diligence statements and geolocation data sheets; and support risk mitigation plans.

Technical support: Support upstream supply chain actors with technical and financial support (e.g., trainings, capacity building, investments).

# <span id="page-62-0"></span>**08.CONCLUSIONS**

### **08. CONCLUSIONS**

Dierent tools and approaches for traceability are currently applied in Viet Nam's coee and timber sectors. As a next step, pilots could be further scaled and targeted interventions strengthened in priority production areas. The immediate focus should be on expanding geolocation mapping and strengthening traceability in highly export-oriented provinces for coee and key plantation and processing hubs for timber and timber products. Provincial authorities, in partnership with MAE, exporters, certification bodies, and international partners, should mobilise blended financing to deliver practical training, digital literacy support, and basic tools to smallholders and cooperatives. Another element could be an interoperable national traceability gateway that integrates data from certification processes, provincial, and national data streams for production areas, and which aligns with EUDR data requirements.

Enhancing geolocation data collection and coverage is a critical priority. Practical solutions include deploying mobile-based GPS mapping applications that enable farmers to self-record plot boundaries, training local extension workers or youth to provide hands-on mapping services, and creating clear protocols for data validation and periodic updates.

Both coee and timber sectors should prioritise organising smallholders into cooperatives or producer groups to enable group certification systems, which reduce costs and make traceability requirements more manageable. Local governments should cooperate with operators to provide practical training, technical assistance, and guidance on digital mapping, record-keeping, and legality. Moreover, pilot programs should support tools with simple interfaces (e.g., smartphones) to help smallholders generate geolocation data.

Developing an open-source, national traceability gateway that can integrate data from various certification bodies, private supply chain

management tools, and government-run systems could oer one possible approach. This unified platform should adopt globally recognised data standards to ensure data sharing across supply chain actors and exporters. Interoperability will enable operators to directly demonstrate compliance more eciently by linking geolocation, legality, and other information/data required for due diligence.

MAE could issue detailed technical guidelines for operators, traders, certification bodies, and smallholders covering data formats, plot mapping methods, due diligence documentation, and timelines appropriate to the Vietnamese context. The existing legal framework, such as Decree 120/2024, should be updated to include EUDR-compatible data requirements for imported raw materials and ensure information flows transparently along the supply chain. At the provincial level, action plan implementation should include dedicated budget allocations for digital tools and training to ensure local stakeholders have sucient capacity to meet the new data and traceability requirements. Partnering with international experts and private actors could be the basis for scaling successful pilots.

### **09. ANNEX**

UTZ was the first certification system introduced in Viet Nam, adopted by enterprises and smallholders in 2001. This certification system requires coee cultivation to comply with sustainability standards to ensure high quality and integrity throughout processing. It is supported by regular inspections and monitoring. In Viet Nam, UTZ certification is issued by the independent certification body Café Control, with headquarters in Ho Chi Minh City. UTZ is now part of the RA, and for a period, the UTZ certification programme and the RA Certified programme operated in parallel. With the launch of the RA 2020 Certification Programme in July 2020 and the introduction of the new RA seal in September that year, the UTZ certification programme and its label are gradually being phased out.

The RA sets out comprehensive sustainability standards. The system requires the coee sector to meet strict criteria related to forest protection, sustainable farming practices, and livelihood improvement for smallholders. To achieve these goals, the system emphasises robust management practices, traceability, including geolocation, and other relevant measures. In addition, it requires near real-time updates of online traceability data. Stakeholders must also comply with social and environmental requirements to support broader sustainability objectives.

The RA defines multiple traceability levels including Identity Preserved (IP), Segregation (SG), and Mass Balance (MB), each with specific timelines and detailed guidance for traceability. The IP level allows consumers to trace a product back to a specific farm Certificate Holder. Mixed IP is a subtype of IP, in which the certified product can be traced back to more than one RA-certified farm Certificate Holder. If a product cannot be traced to a specific farm

Certificate Holder but its content is fully RA certified and kept segregated from non-certified products throughout the supply chain, it falls under the Segregation level. The MB level allows a Certificate Holder to sell a non-certified product as RA certified if an equivalent quantity was sourced as RA certified within the supply chain*<sup>12</sup>*. The RA has developed an online traceability system that allows various actors to update near real-time information on planting, processing, and sales activities.

In 2010, the major Vietnamese coee exporter Simexco Dak Lak Ltd was the first enterprise joining the 4C programme and engaged 4,605 farmers, initially through 4C verification and, from 2018 onward, through the full certification system. This certification system is built on 12 principles covering economic, social, and environmental aspects, based on good agricultural and management practices as well as international conventions and recognised guidelines in the coee and cocoa sectors. In addition, the system defines three levels of compliance, allowing for a smooth transition to certification and supporting the continuous improvement of certified producers.

4C-certified coee is fully traceable through a supply chain supported by digital commercial reporting. Within the 4C system, standard operating procedures for traceability are clearly defined and implemented. The system requires that 4C-certified coee be physically segregated from non-certified coee. Records of 4C-certified coee produced on registered fields or plots, listed in the Business Partner Map of the 4C Unit, must be maintained, although this requirement does not apply to smallholders. All reports must include detailed information on sales, deliveries, and stock of 4C-certified coee, including the date, volume, and the name and address of recipients. These records must align with related documents such as invoices and delivery notes. Furthermore, the volume of 4C-certified coee sold by the Business Partner Producer must correspond to the size of the registered fields or plots in the Business Partner Map. The system strictly prohibits "multiple claiming" of certified coee; for example, the same batch cannot be sold or delivered more than once as certified.

#### **Fairtrade**

Fairtrade works with farming cooperatives, businesses, and governments to make trade fairer. In 2008, Fairtrade-certified coee was introduced in Viet Nam by an enterprise to support a group of farmers through a cooperative*<sup>13</sup>*. Fairtrade-certified coee is produced in accordance with strict standards that promote sustainable livelihoods, safe working conditions, environmental protection, and strong, transparent supply chains*<sup>14</sup>* .

Fairtrade coee is fully traceable and must be identifiable as Fairtrade at every stage of the supply chain and in all relevant documents and packaging*<sup>15</sup>* It must ensure that products sold as Fairtrade are sourced exclusively from Fairtrade producers, meaning they are kept separate from non-Fairtrade products from field to store shelf. The traceability system requires that all operators meet strict documentary traceability requirements. Additionally, physical traceability is mandatory for all operators except those handling cocoa, cane sugar, fruit juices, tea, and Farm Sustainability Platform cotton, which do not require physical traceability*<sup>16</sup>* .

#### **Organic**

Viet Nam has developed a system of organic coee certification systems for both international and domestic markets. The three most common international organic certifications in Viet Nam are United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) Organic, Japanese agricultural standards, and EU Organic, which enable producers to export to the US, Japanese, and EU markets, respectively*<sup>17</sup>*. In addition, Viet Nam has established its own organic standards to meet the country's growing domestic demand for organic products. Cultivation practices must not use synthetic chemical fertilizers, herbicides, or pesticides and must protect soil health and ensure safety for the end consumer*<sup>18</sup>* .

Organic certification systems rely on third-party verification by independent organisations to confirm that producers meet and maintain certification requirements. Producers must manage all aspects of farming and handling, including activities such as tilling, grazing, harvesting, storage, and transportation. The standards also define which substances are permitted during production and handling, set requirements for monitoring practices and recordkeeping, and mandate measures to prevent mixing with non-organic products or exposure to prohibited substances.

A certifying agent conducts on-site inspections of every operation applying for organic certification. For coee, this involves checking fields, soil conditions, crop health, weed and pest management methods, water systems, storage facilities, and equipment. Inspectors also examine the receiving, processing, and storage areas for organic ingredients and finished products at handling or processing sites. After assessing practices and comparing them with the approved organic system plan, the inspector reports their findings to the certifying agent. Additional information may be requested before a final certification decision is made*<sup>19</sup>* .

#### **FSC**

The FSC is a forest certification system that sets standards for responsible forest management. Its criteria require that forests be managed in ways that are environmentally appropriate, socially beneficial, and economically viable. The FSC certification system is designed to be open and transparent, ensuring that stakeholders throughout the forest supply chain adhere to principles that protect healthy and resilient forests for generations. This certification recognises environmentally responsible, socially beneficial, and economically viable forest management. Over the past two decades, Vietnamese firms and smallholders have increasingly participated in the FSC certification system to promote sustainability in the timber sector.

#### **PEFC**

The PEFC aims to promote sustainable forest management through independent third-party certification. It does this by endorsing national certification systems developed by individual countries, ensuring they meet specific sustainability benchmarks. To receive PEFC endorsement, a country's national forest certification system must be reviewed by an expert panel and the PEFC board against international criteria. These national systems are developed through a multi-stakeholder process, allowing them to be tailored to local priorities and conditions. This approach provides forest managers,

both large and small, with the tools to demonstrate their commitment to sustainability and responsible forest management.

Both FSC and PEFC systems use Chain of Custody (CoC) to certify forest products. CoC certification verifies that timber used in production is sourced from responsibly managed forests and remains traceable throughout all stages of production and trade, from the forest manager to the final product manufacturer.
