# THE EVENT

The **Altamira Summit was a global outreach event under the framework of the Team Europe Initiative on Deforestation-Free Value Chains**, held between November 3rd and 7th, 2025, in Altamira, Pará, Brazil. It **brought together 120 participants from public, private, civil society and academic sectors from 25 countries** for a week of global learning on sustainable, deforestation-free value chains.

Among the 25 countries represented at the event were: Argentina, Belgium, Bolivia, Brazil, Cameroon, Colombia, Côte d'Ivoire, Ecuador, Germany, Ghana, Indonesia, Italy, Kenya, Malaysia, Netherlands, Paraguay, Peru, Democratic Republic of Congo, Spain, Uganda, Uruguay, Vietnam, and Zambia.

It was planned and organized by the Zero Deforestation Hub - the secretariat of the Team Europe Initiative (TEI) on Deforestation-Free Value Chains - and the Sustainable Agriculture for Forest Ecosystems (SAFE) project - flagship of the TEI -, with support from the Brazilian Ministry of Agriculture (MAPA) and the Municipality of Altamira. The official Pre-COP30 event took place a week prior to the global climate negotiations in Belém, and was a **strategic forum for dialogue, knowledge exchange, and cross-regional collaboration** to advance sustainable agriculture and efforts to decoupling deforestation from agricultural commodities.

Furthermore, the Summit formed part of the 2025 Partners for Change (P4C) event series, which links global agendas with on-the-ground transformation efforts. These thematic activities will culminate in a 2026 network meeting in Berlin, contributing to a P4C 2030 Position Paper that sets shared priorities for food security and sustainable agri-food systems in the post-SDG context.

The event combined **plenary sessions, field-based learning, and participatory group work to promote inclusive dialogue and effective knowledge exchange among a multilingual and culturally diverse group of participants**. English was the official working language, with simultaneous interpretation provided in Portuguese, Spanish, and French.

## Context

### **Global deforestation driven by land conversion for agricultural production remains high.**

The European Union has developed a milestone in international forest conservation policies by **introducing the European Regulation on Deforestation-Free Products (EUDR)**, to address the responsibility and share of EU consumption on products linked to deforestation. The EUDR came into force in June 2022 and is planned to enter into application in 2026. Article 30 under the EU regulation establishes the responsibility of EU and Member States to support partner countries in their preparation to meet the new EU market requirements on deforestation-free and legal production of key commodities and derived products. **The Team Europe Initiative (TEI) on Deforestation-Free Value Chains aligns global support measures**, implemented by TEI flagship projects. The Sustainable Agriculture for Forest Ecosystems (SAFE) project, implemented by the Deutsche Gesellschaft für Internationale Zusammenarbeit (GIZ), is hosting the secretariat of the TEI and connects TEI flagships and partners with European Authorities via coordination, outreach and knowledge management. The Altamira Summit is part of the TEI outreach activities to facilitate global learning and building partnerships.

**The flagship projects of the Team Europe Initiative** on Deforestation-free Value Chains (TEI DFVC) and **partners from Latin America, Africa, Asia, and Europe gathered in Altamira** to facilitate dialogue, knowledge sharing, and networking across regions, sectors, and initiatives. The event aimed **to promote mutual learning, build on synergies, and advance concrete collaborations** for deforestation-free value chains, with a strong focus on technical exchange. Based on two years since the establishment of the TEI, their partners have dedicated their focus to preserving forests by promoting sustainable agri-food systems. Governments, companies and civil society focus on empowering smallholders and strengthening local capacities for sustainable land use and agriculture.

In Brazil, the SAFE project is implemented by GIZ in partnership with the federal government through the Ministry of Agriculture and Livestock of Brazil (MAPA). **Altamira, in the state of Pará, is at the heart of Brazil's SAFE project pilot region.** In this area, SAFE promotes the sustainability of cocoa and cattle value chains, while ensuring compliance with legal production standards, improving market access, operationalising traceability systems, and facilitating access to finance. Altamira is a living example of how integrated efforts toward sustainability and deforestation-free supply chains can be effectively implemented and it is an emblematic town in the Amazon, located on the banks of the Xingu River, a major tributary of the Amazon.

# EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

## From dialogue to action: advancing deforestation-free value chains

The Altamira Summit demonstrated that meaningful progress toward deforestation-free and legally compliant value chains is already underway across regions, sectors, and commodities. Country experiences, combined with practical insights from the technical sessions, confirmed that the **EU Regulation on Deforestation-Free Products (EUDR) can function as a catalyst for transformation** — provided it is implemented pragmatically, inclusively, and in close cooperation with producer countries.

## Country progress: diverse pathways, shared direction

Country examples highlighted that while starting points differ, a **common direction toward traceability, legality, and sustainability is emerging**. Brazil showcased advanced institutional readiness through integrated public platforms such as AgroBrasil+Sustentável, new deforestation reduction targets in the Amazon and Cerrado, and sectoral preparedness in soy, beef, cocoa, and livestock. Brazil's experience illustrated how national digital infrastructure, market engagement, and policy continuity can reinforce each other.

Peru demonstrated rapid progress through the creation of a multisectoral EUDR working group, a national strategy, and the mapping of more than 115,000 producers using simple digital tools. Ecuador advanced participatory geolocation approaches for smallholders, aligning public and private initiatives such as "Mi finca, mi huella".

Indonesia highlighted both commitment and remaining challenges, particularly regarding geolocation of smallholders, while calling for recognition of existing sustainability systems such as RSPO. Vietnam strengthened national traceability systems for coffee and rubber, while Zambia advanced sustainability in the timber sector. Ghana, Côte d'Ivoire, Cameroon, Uganda, and the DRC **demonstrated how national forest monitoring, cocoa traceability, and pilot geolocation systems can reduce risks of exclusion and improve data quality**.

Across regions, countries emphasized that capacity building, proportionality, and access to finance are critical to ensure that smallholders and SMEs can comply with EUDR requirements without being pushed out of formal markets.

![](_page_6_Picture_16.jpeg)

## Key learnings from technical sessions

**The EUDR Readiness session confirmed that compliance depends on the existence of robust Due Diligence Systems.** Several Dry-Runs conducted in many countries revealed that companies often focus heavily on deforestation risk while underestimating legality risks. Participants agreed that legality cannot be assessed through aggregated national statistics alone; instead, cross-referencing multiple data sources and maps is essential to demonstrate negligible risk, as required under the EUDR. Sector-specific realities—such as palm oil bulk shipments or agroforestry cocoa systems—require tailored approaches to avoid false positives and unnecessary segregation.

**The Legality session highlighted the complexity of fragmented legal frameworks.** Experiences from Colombia and Malaysia illustrated both the challenge and the opportunity: legality risk assessment is not a box-ticking exercise, but a strategic investment to harmonize sectoral approaches and strengthen governance. Tools such as the Legality Due Diligence Navigator support operators and authorities in applying a risk-based approach, while national platforms integrating multiple databases, as in Brazil, increase transparency and confidence.

**The Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI) session demonstrated that interoperable, open-source systems are key enablers of scale and trust.** Country cases showed that national land-cover maps and traceability systems—such as those in Côte d'Ivoire, Ghana, Cameroon, Uganda, and Brazil—often provide more accurate, EUDR-aligned data than global datasets. Strong DPI reduces transaction costs, supports competent authorities, and creates a shared factual basis for risk assessment.

## Private sector engagement and smallholder inclusion

Discussions on private sector engagement emphasized that companies play a central role in operationalizing deforestation-free value chains. In countries such as Brazil, Peru, Malaysia, and Colombia, **private actors are leading experimental shipments, investing in traceability systems, and coordinating across supply chains.** At the same time, participants warned that overly rigid data requirements may push producers into informality or shift compliance costs unfairly onto smallholders. Economic viability and market incentives remain essential.

Smallholder inclusion, gender equity, and social inclusion emerged as non-negotiable elements of sustainable value chains. Experiences from Ecuador, Bolivia, Brazil, and Southeast Asia showed that **participatory approaches to geolocation, strengthened land rights for women, and institutional gender policies improve data quality, trust, and long-term sustainability.**

![](_page_7_Picture_15.jpeg)

## Insights from the field trips

The field trips to cattle and cocoa producers in the Xingu region highlighted the central role of families, associations, and cooperatives in advancing sustainable and deforestation-free production systems. Direct exchanges with producers showed the importance of **starting with simple, practical steps and avoiding overburdening farmers with EUDR complexity**, while prioritizing capacity building and organizational support. Integrated approaches that address livelihoods, access to finance, and natural resource management are essential, as **producers face significant economic constraints and unequal market access**, particularly between conventional and organic systems. Participants observed that organic and agroforestry production systems deliver strong environmental and productivity benefits and should be rewarded with premium prices. The visits also underscored the leadership role of women and the lesson that preserving forests enhances long-term productivity, resilience, and farmer entrepreneurship.

## Roles of key sectors

The Summit clarified complementary roles across sectors:

- ● Governments are responsible for providing coherent and participatory policy frameworks, harmonizing regulations, investing in public data and digital infrastructure, and aligning international support measures.
- ● The private sector drives innovation, traceability, and market access, invests in training and technology, and helps scale deforestation-free production beyond individual supply chains.
- ● Civil society supports capacity building, acts as a watchdog, fosters inclusion, and advocates for smallholders and environmental integrity.
- ● Academia and research institutions translate complex regulatory requirements into practical guidance, generate evidence, and facilitate multi-actor dialogue.

## Final outlook

The Altamira Summit concluded with a shared understanding that **many technical solutions already exist, but their impact depends on coordination, trust, and inclusive implementation**. If aligned across countries and sectors, the EUDR can enable scalable, competitive, and socially inclusive deforestation-free value chains, contributing not only to forest protection but also to resilient livelihoods and sustainable development.

## Q&A

During the discussion, **Fernando Sampaio**, **ABIEC Brazil**, asked about **opportunities in the European Union for the Triple-F mechanism** on the recovery of degraded areas, which Brazil is proposing in the context of COP30 in Belém. In response, **Simon Gmeiner**, **European Commission DG INTPA**, explained that the flagship initiatives under the Team Europe Initiative (TEI) could offer relevant opportunities. He noted that potential interest from EU Member States would need to be further analyzed and explored, adding that he was open to continuing the discussion, particularly in relation to the beef sector.

**Samuel Owusu-Acheaw**, **COCOBOD Ghana**, raised a question regarding whether investments made to support compliance with the EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR) would be monitored. **Simon Gmeiner** replied that the TEI is supporting **alignment among competent authorities across Member States**. He also referred to the current proposal to amend the EUDR, which includes a six-month transition period aimed at facilitating coordinated action among national authorities.

## Report of main moments and discussions

EFI identified two key insights based on the conducted pilots:

1. 1. **Companies need a robust Due Diligence System**, and many still do not fully understand how to build one.
2. 2. **Companies are focusing heavily on Deforestation Risk but not on Legality**, even though both aspects must carry equal weight.

## Record of the main moments and discussions

The Legality session followed up on the Dry Run-session by delving deeper into the EUDR's legal requirements and exploring how producer countries can support market compliance. It addressed the widespread confusion among EU operators, partner countries, and value-chain actors by examining examples from West African cocoa producers (Côte d'Ivoire, Cameroon, Ghana) to stakeholders involved through the Technical Facility in Colombia and Peru. When the EUDR was adopted in 2023, the legality topic initially received limited attention, with interest growing gradually as stakeholders sought to **understand deforestation-risk assessment and what "legal production" entails.**

The discussion clarified that **responsibility lies with the EU operators**, who must ensure that commodities are both deforestation-free and legally produced. Legality, in that regard meaning, that goods need to be produced in accordance with national laws, e.g. covering land-use rights, environmental protection, and labour or tax aspects. Participants examined how these legal frameworks are enforced, where data is available, and which are options for mitigating risk. The session also noted **existing guidance from the EU Commission**, while emphasising ongoing uncertainty among operators, producer-country actors, and national competent authorities about how these requirements should be incorporated into Due Diligence Systems.

![](_page_27_Picture_10.jpeg)

Information has been gathered organically and is now integrated into **the Legality Due Diligence Navigator** ([www.legalitynavigator.efi.int](http://www.legalitynavigator.efi.int)), a publicly available tool whose methodology can be used by any stakeholder group to support legal clarification. Legal mapping has already been completed for Cameroon, Côte d'Ivoire, and Ghana, with strong interest expressed by Paraguay and Tanzania, and ongoing work in Peru, Colombia, and Honduras. This responds to growing demand, and ITC has endorsed the approach to scale up to 30 origins worldwide, allowing due diligence guidance to reach many more countries. As partnerships expand, both operators and NCAs can benefit from the tool, and the material was also accessible at the Altamira Marketplace.

Key learnings show that a stakeholder-driven process greatly improves collective understanding of **due diligence as a risk-based approach rather than a box-ticking exercise**, helping actors identify the actions needed for different levels of risk. It also clarified that legal obligations resting with EU operators do not create new regulations for producers, while value-chain actors in producing countries play a supporting role.

The main challenges include **unclear or fragmented legal frameworks**, differing interpretations of laws, gaps in implementation, and difficulties in determining which provisions are truly relevant, especially since many labour or sector-specific requirements do not apply to smallholders.

## Route 1 – Assurini

The visits along Route 1 Assurini underscored the critical role of enabling public policies, particularly in the cattle sector, alongside producers' clear commitment to forest conservation. However, achieving income security remains a prerequisite for smallholders before compliance with traceability and legality requirements can be expected. Policy implementation should therefore follow a phased approach, starting with basic requirements and avoiding excessive initial regulatory burdens, including those related to the EUDR.

Targeted support for family farming is essential to facilitate access to new markets. While relatively simple technologies can substantially improve productivity, structural production constraints must be addressed before introducing advanced traceability systems. Land degradation and increasing climatic stress highlight the need for sustained technical assistance, multi-stakeholder partnerships, and strong producer organisations. Short-term project cycles are insufficient to deliver long-term, systemic change.

<span id="page-40-1"></span>

## Route 2 – Brasil Novo A

The visits along Route 2 Brasil Novo A demonstrated that sustainable production systems are viable across both livestock and cocoa sectors, including integrated crop–livestock–forestry systems and fine-flavour cocoa agroforestry. Despite generally sound farm management practices, financial constraints remain a significant barrier to further sustainability investments.

Awareness of emerging regulatory frameworks, such as the EUDR, is limited, particularly among cattle producers, underscoring the need for targeted information, capacity-building, and transitional periods. Access to green finance, combined with technical training, is a key enabler of sustainable intensification. Cocoa agroforestry presents a strategic pathway toward deforestation-free supply chains. However, limited economic incentives—particularly for EU export markets—affect farmer engagement. Levels of preparedness for external market and policy requirements remain uneven.

<span id="page-40-2"></span>

## Route 3 – Brasil Novo B

The visits along Route 3, Brasil Novo B, revealed significant disparities between conventional and organic cocoa producers in terms of production capacity, organisational development, and market access. Producers with clear strategic objectives and leadership—especially in organic and agroforestry systems—demonstrated stronger integration across the value chain, including processing and value addition.

![](_page_41_Picture_14.jpeg)

While local processing enhances value capture, commercialisation remains a key bottleneck. Diverse farming models reflect both historical land-use trajectories linked to Amazon colonisation since the 1960s and evolving sustainability transitions. Organic production and agroforestry systems require stronger market recognition and policy incentives. Effective partnerships and demand-driven technical assistance are essential to ensure that support mechanisms align with the operational realities of smallholder farmers.

<span id="page-41-0"></span>

## Route 4 – Medicilândia

The visits along Route 4 Medicilândia highlighted the role of cooperatives and partnerships in enabling the transition from primary production to entrepreneurial and value-added cocoa business models. Cooperativism emerged as a central mechanism for reducing risk, scaling innovation, and enhancing competitiveness within sustainable cocoa value chains, including family-based processing initiatives.

Women's participation, youth engagement, and intergenerational family enterprises were identified as essential drivers of rural development. At the same time, the operating context is affected by structural challenges, including political violence in rural areas. The Transamazônica corridor has played a historical role in shaping settlement and production systems. Narrative-based knowledge sharing and peer learning proved effective in communicating impact and strengthening collective action.

<span id="page-41-1"></span>

## Route 5 – Vitória do Xingu

The visits along Route 5 Vitória do Xingu emphasised the social and cultural dimensions of agricultural value chains, highlighting how different production systems influence livelihoods and territorial resilience. Innovation and entrepreneurship were key to transforming historical challenges into viable economic opportunities.

Limited access to financial resources remains a structural constraint, often driven by information gaps rather than a lack of demand, reinforcing the importance of advisory services and technical support. Women's leadership plays a critical role in organisational development and innovation. Indigenous and agroforestry-based production systems demonstrate that forest conservation and high-quality production can be mutually reinforcing, supporting long-term territorial stability. A smallholder organisation is shaped by collective memory and forward-looking development visions.

**Daniel Kazimierski, ViSEC Argentina**, stressed that the **EUDR has driven improvements in Argentina's public registries and opened new opportunities for cooperation between government and the private sector through VISEC**. He noted that buyer–exporter relations remain a private-sector responsibility, but solid and clear government information is essential, and that VISEC's traceability system, combined with third-party certification, helps ensure the credibility of all traceability data.

Regarding future vision, as emphasised by **Colombia's experience with large cocoa companies**, **the private sector will play a central role in driving traceability**, ensuring compliance, and fostering collaboration across entire supply chains. **Companies are expected to leverage their capacity and agility to coordinate efforts that benefit all actors, from producers to exporters.**

**In Peru and Malaysia, private actors are leading experimental shipments**, developing agroforestry systems, and creating aligned protocols and data exchanges to build trust and harmonize information along the chain. **Key responsibilities include investing in training, innovation, and traceability tools, establishing collaborative structures and aligned standards**, integrating suppliers and promoting transparency while protecting sensitive data.

![](_page_64_Picture_8.jpeg)

Participants also stressed the **private sector's role in supporting market positioning for deforestation-free products, reducing deforestation across landscapes rather than only "cleaning-up" supply chains**, and preparing for future regulatory scrutiny by building robust systems to meet EUDR requirements. Overall, private actors are seen as both implementers and innovators, bridging gaps between public regulations, producer realities, and market expectations.

# Future vision

## Session objective

To plan a desirable future, in this final session, participants collectively thought and dreamed about what future scenario they envision for their countries or regions to achieve sustainable, deforestation-free supply chains.

## Record of the main moments and discussions

### A | Government

Governments can contribute by **implementing public policies that are favourable, coherent, and participatory, supporting family farmers**, fostering an environment conducive to investment, and aligning regulations to facilitate compliance. By improving communication across government levels, they can ignite **developing traceability systems**, foster scientific and qualified information, and engage in training, awareness building, and partnerships to support producers and exporters. The government's role is crucial in advancing deforestation-free value chains and effectively implementing the EUDR, and mobilizing sustainable finance in partnership with the private sector.

- • **Give policy direction**, enable environment, incentivize for deforestation-free value chains, walk the talk
- • **Adapt favourable and coherent public policies** to support family farmers, which are connected to the private sector
- • Seek consensus to generate assertive public policies
- • **Harmonize regulations** to facilitate compliance with the rules
- • Enable the incorporation of small producers into the market
- • Promote a favourable environment for investment and **leverage private finance** for sustainability

### B | Private Sector

The private sector can contribute by **investing in technology, innovation, and sustainable supply chains**, by promoting transparency, fair data-sharing systems, and access to green finance. They can support farmers and small producers, **strengthen inclusive businesses**, to favor expanding market access, and reducing intermediaries. By providing technical assistance

![](_page_69_Picture_12.jpeg)

they can **foster local added value**, disseminating information, and promoting ongoing dialogue to guide results-based decision-making.

- • **Invest in technology**, promote transparency, and foster technical assistance
- • Invest in supply chains, sustainability and local added value
- • **Facilitate market access**, innovation, investments and green finance
- • Develop a fair system for **sharing data with stakeholders**
- • Foster inclusion toward small producers in the production chain and promote inclusive businesses
- • **Initiate dialogue, exchange and discussion** about results with other producers and sectors and disseminate information
- • **Activate better communication** between the government (state, municipal, federal) to ensure compliance with the law
- • **Engage in participatory dialogue** for oriented policies
- • **Qualify information, support science** (scenarios and data to support evidence-based decision-making)
- • **Guide on practical and full implementation of the EUDR**
- • Assess gaps in impact analysis and voice them to the political level
- • Develop a traceability system
- • **Build awareness** and training measures

## C | Civil Society

Civil Society can contribute by **providing technical assistance, fundraising, fostering political and public engagement**, and ensuring social oversight while holding governments and companies accountable. NGO's role in coordinating diverse actors, facilitating dialogue, and mobilizing technical and financial resources can support the adoption of sustainable practices, generate data-driven solutions, advocate for policy improvements, and help ensure compliance with sustainability initiatives.

- • Provide technical assistance, fundraising, political and public engagement
- • **Act as social control entity and watchdog** and **hold governments and companies accountable**
- • Facilitate and **coordinate the activities of different actors**, mobilize technical and financial resources and **build bridges to maintain progress**
- • **Create data solutions**
- • **Advocate for smallholder and/or nature interests** and engage in discussions
- • Promote and **provide trainings** on good agricultural practices to avoid environmental damage

![](_page_70_Picture_14.jpeg)

## D | Academy

Academy can contribute by **translating concepts and practices into simple language**, creating spaces of trust and exchange between civil society and the private sector, and promoting a sustainable land-use narrative that encourages engagement. They can provide clear technical guidelines and **conduct studies and investigations** aligned with the real needs of the productive sector.

- • **Provide technical practices and guidelines** on the implementation of the EUDR and transition to sustainable agricultural practices
- • Translate concepts and practices into simple language
- • Create spaces of trust and exchange between civil society and the private sector and **lead multi-actor dialogues and roundtables**
- • Conduce studies and investigations in accordance with the needs of the productive sector

## CONCLUSIONS

The Altamira Summit highlighted the importance of collaboration, knowledge sharing, and inclusive engagement of smallholders to advance deforestation-free supply chains. Key priorities included improved communication and transparency around the EUDR, capacity building, access to finance, training, and strong governance and traceability systems to balance forest protection with livelihoods.

Looking ahead, **private sector actors** are encouraged to invest in innovation, transparency, and sustainable finance; **governments** to develop coherent, participatory policies and traceability systems; **academia** to provide practical research and clear guidance; and **civil society** to support implementation through coordination, technical assistance, and monitoring.
