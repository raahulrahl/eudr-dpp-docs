1 BACKGROUND On 31 May 2023, the European Union (EU) adopted Regulation 2023/1115 on the placing on the EU market and the export from the EU of certain commodities and products associated with deforestation and forest degradation (EUDR). This regulation requires operators and traders who place products from supply chains associated with deforestation or forest degradation on the EU market to demonstrate that these products are traceable, deforestation-free and legal. The scope of application covers seven commodities: coffee, cocoa, rubber, oil palm, soy, cattle, and timber, as well as derived products such as chocolate and cocoa paste. The regulation is scheduled to enter into force on 30 December 2026 (and 30 June 2027 for micro and small enterprises established as such before 31 December 2020). Businesses affected by the regulation (operators and traders) will be required to carry out due diligence before exporting or trading their products, in order to gather sufficient information to demonstrate that the product presents no or negligible risk of non-compliance.

Consequently, operators trading coffee or derived products on the EU market must demonstrate that these products have been produced in accordance with the relevant legislation of the country of production (Article 3), which is defined as that relating to the legal status of the production area. The EUDR adopts a flexible approach, listing general areas of law rather than specifying particular legal instruments, as these differ from country to country and may change over time. These areas are [for agricultural products, see Article 2.40 of the EUDR]:

- **a.** Land use rights
- **b.** Environmental protection
- **c.** Third parties' rights
- **d.** Labour rights
- **e.** Human rights protected by international law
- **f.** Free, Prior and Informed Consent (FPIC), as set out in the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples
- **g.** Tax, anti-corruption, trade and customs regulations

In this context, understanding the legislative framework of the country of origin, identifying the relevant legal requirements for the product in question, and determining how to verify compliance with these requirements is a challenge not only for operators responsible for due diligence, but also for the competent EU authorities in charge of controls, and other stakeholders.

## The European Union Deforestation Regulation

In Colombia, coffee is recognised as a crop of major cultural, social, economic and environmental importance and, unlike other agricultural activities, is not directly associated with deforestation. Although there are 76,633 coffee-growing Agricultural Production Units (APUs) located near areas of deforestation, representing around 16% of national production, studies agree that coffee is mostly grown under schemes that respect forest cover and may even promote conservation, especially when cultivated under agroforestry and shade systems. In this context, Colombia has made progress in commitments such as the deforestation-free coffee agreement, "Coffee, Forests and Climate" (2021), which aims to strengthen the adaptation and resilience of coffee-growing communities to climate change, promote sustainable livelihoods, and contribute to the protection and restoration of forests. Likewise, when forest areas are converted to coffee cultivation, the country requires rigorous technical and environmental processes that guarantee responsible land use. These practices help Colombian coffee establish its position not only as a high-quality product, but also as a production model that, when well-managed, supports sustainability and ecosystem conservation.

Coffee farming is highly significant at the territorial level. Coffee is grown in 610 municipalities in Colombia, representing more than 54% of all municipalities. The cultivated area is 840,000 hectares (18% of the country's agricultural land) and is distributed across approximately 660,000 plots. Coffee production is carried out by around 560,000 producers, 54.1% of whom cultivate one (1) hectare or less, 97% cultivate less than five (5) hectares, and only the remaining 3.1% can be considered medium or large producers (with more than 5 hectares). This concentration of coffee cultivation on small plots with limited cultivated areas is one of the sociodemographic characteristics of this population and reflects the prevalence of family farming within the coffee production model. The population in coffee-growing households amounts to 1.8 million people, with an average of 3.8 members per household. Most of them live in rural areas (81.2%), have an average of 5.5 years of formal education, and 27.4% live in households that have been affected by the armed conflict.

In economic terms, coffee accounts for 15% of agricultural GDP and between 5% and 8% of the total value of the country's goods exports. Colombia achieved a historic record of 5.4 billion dollars in coffee exports for the 2024/2025 coffee year<sup>1</sup> . The United States was the main destination, followed by the European Union and Canada<sup>2</sup> . Domestic consumption continues to grow steadily and currently averages 2.2 kg per person per year.

## 2 CONTEXTO OBJECTIVES AND GENERAL APPROACH

The objectives of this tool are to promote a national and consensual vision of the Colombian legal requirements applicable to coffee production and trade, and relevant to the EUDR; and to provide recommendations for verifying coffee regulatory implementation and risk management, in support of operators' due diligence.

The tool was developed with the following aims:

To support the harmonisation of operators' due diligence approaches To encourage the simplification of procedures for supply chain actors who may be required to provide data to their customers To facilitate a better understanding of national contexts by the competent authorities of EU Member States responsible for controls To ensure equitable access to information, reduce perceived risks, and position Colombian origin at a competitive advantage.

![](_page_10_Picture_4.jpeg)

It is the responsibility of operators who place coffee or its derived products on the EU market to identify the relevant legal requirements for Colombia, within the meaning of Article 2(40) of the EUDR, and to adapt their due diligence to the risks identified. This tool provides guidance that can assist operators and other stakeholders in the sector in this regard. The tool is likely to evolve over time and be updated in response to various factors: possible legal reforms in the country, developments in public or private certification systems, lessons learned from the practical implementation of national due diligence recommendations, additional guidance provided by the European Commission or competent authorities, integration of best practices, technological advances, etc.

**It is important to note that the results presented are not legally binding, do not impose any obligations on the relevant parties, and do not constitute legal advice.**

## 3 METHODOLOGY FOR DEVELOPING THE TOOL

Based on various analyses of the legal framework for implementation in the country developed by the National Association of Industrialists (ANDI), the Coffee, Forests and Climate Agreement, GIZ, FEDECACAO, FEDEPALMA and FNC, the EFI team, together with Swisscontact Colombia, consolidated and supplemented in a first step all the relevant standards for the palm, coffee and cocoa sectors, comprehensively organised according to the Areas of law defined by the EUDR. These were structured into subcategories under a legal taxonomy developed by EFI in collaboration with Preferred by Nature, in order to facilitate their analysis (see <https://legalitynavigator.efi.int>/).

In addition, bilateral consultations were held with key actors, as well as socialisation and feedback meetings with stakeholder groups, including cooperatives and export companies from the productive sectors (see Annex 3).

For each subcategory, which groups together standards relating to the same objective, a legal requirement was drafted in clear and understandable language for operators, which was validated through multi-stakeholder exercises. Likewise, the relevance of each requirement for the sectors involved was determined collectively and by consensus, thus closing the first step of the process (see Annex 1 which details the legal mapping methodology).

### **What are these recommendations for?**

These recommendations can be used in different ways and at different times depending on the actors involved.

**Operators and traders** can use these recommendations to establish and document their due diligence system, ensuring that the coffee they trade complies with the legal requirements of the EUDR. They can use them to identify the documents and information that need to be collected or other actions that need to be taken with their suppliers in Colombia, and to assess the risks associated with their supply chain.

**Competent authorities in the EU** can use these recommendations as a standard for assessing the compliance of products traded under the EUDR. They can also use them as a basis for harmonising their controls and interpreting documents from Colombia.

**Supply chain actors** in Colombia can use these recommendations to understand the implications of the EUDR requirements for coffee and the expectations of European operators and traders subject to it. The recommendations can be used to structure and document the information they may provide to these operators and traders. They can also help them anticipate non-compliance risks and adapt their agricultural and commercial practices accordingly.

**Colombian public entities** can use these recommendations to strengthen controls on implementation of national legislation by producers and exporters, in order to ensure that coffee traded complies with current Colombian laws. They can also use it to support small producers in implementation by providing them with better access to information on current regulations and buyer requirements. They can build on these recommendations to make data more transparent and accessible, including facilitating access to administrative documents, to help operators demonstrate the legality of coffee.

**Civil society** can use these recommendations to monitor the implementation of Colombian legal requirements by companies and authorities. They can be used to conduct analyses and produce reports on the risks of non-compliance in coffee. They can also be used to raise awareness among producers, companies and consumers about the legality of Colombian coffee.

### **How to use these due diligence recommendations?**

The recommendations presented in this document aim to address all relevant legal requirements identified by stakeholders during the first phase of this study. They cover, in particular, all the legal areas listed in Article 2.40 of the EUDR, in accordance with the precautionary approach adopted in the study (see Annex 1). However, a distinction is made between requirements directly related to the objectives of the EUDR and others, which are marked with an asterisk.

This flexible approach allows users of the recommendations to adapt their due diligence actions based on their interpretation of the scope of the EUDR.

### **Light or standard due diligence**

This study takes a pragmatic approach that seeks to identify due diligence actions proportionate to the risks, in order to limit the burden on those involved in the sector. The level of implementation has been assessed for each requirement considered relevant to coffee production and trade in Colombia. The level of implementation adopted by the study is based on relevant literature, expert knowledge of the sector, field surveys conducted as part of the study, and bilateral and multi-stakeholder consultations organised within the framework of the study.

### Articulación entre nivel de cumplimiento y las recomendaciones de DD

When the level of implementation assessed for a requirement is high, the risk of non-compliance is considered negligible. Therefore, light due diligence actions are recommended. On the other hand, when the level of implementation of a requirement is low or unknown, the risk of non-compliance is considered non-negligible. Therefore, standard due diligence is recommended.

Coffee certification systems are voluntary, public or private mechanisms that seek to ensure that coffee production and trade comply with certain environmental, social and economic standards. They can be established by governments, international organisations, NGOs or private companies.

Third-party certification and verification systems can play an important role in promoting sustainable agricultural and forestry practices and responsible sourcing. There are different types of certifications, which can be implemented at the national level on a mandatory basis or be private and voluntary (e.g. Rainforest Alliance, Fairtrade, 4C or Organic).

According to the EUDR, for the purposes of risk assessment, operators may take into account information from certification schemes or other third-party verified schemes. However, the European Commission's guidelines of 2 October 2024 indicate that self-declaration schemes that are not based on third-party certification procedures are, by definition, less robust due to a lack of independence and impartiality.

The Commission also specifies that certification schemes should not replace the operator's responsibility for due diligence. In other words, private certification schemes can be a tool to help European operators carry out due diligence to comply with legality, deforestation and traceability criteria.

Operators must be able to justify why and how certification schemes comply with EUDR requirements. To do so, three elements must be taken into account:

The coverage of relevant legal requirements for the EUDR by the certification system. The robustness of the certification system: in particular, certification scope (applicable to which actors in the value chain? to which products?); governance (management of internal procedures and updates, for example); accreditation processes of the competent control bodies; auditor qualifications and audit frequency; non-compliance management, impartiality and conflict of interest management, etc. This information must be periodically reassessed by the operator, particularly in relation to EUDR requirements. Traceability and the absence of mixing with non-certified products.

It should be noted that this tool has not carried out an exhaustive comparison of the coverage of the relevant legal requirements by certification systems used in the coffee sector in Colombia.

RECOMMENDATIONS FOR LEGAL DUE DILIGENCE IN THE CONTEXT OF NATIONAL COFFEE PRODUCTION

![](_page_18_Picture_0.jpeg)

In Colombia, access and use of land are organised through various forms of tenure, including individual ownership, collective ownership particularly in indigenous and Afro-descendant communities—as well as leasing and possession arrangements. These schemes allow different actors to carry out productive activities without formal land ownership being the only enabling condition. However, in certain areas of the country, careful analysis is necessary, as there may be a history or evidence of forced displacement and conflicts associated with tenure, which poses additional challenges to ensuring legal certainty and sustainable land use.

Section 1.2 refers specifically to the legal regimes for land use in Colombia. Land use is mainly regulated by the Land Use Plans (Planes de Ordenamiento Territorial, POT), which define how the territory can be occupied and used in each municipality, incorporating environmental, risk and sustainability criteria. Alongside these plans, there is an official technical instrument known as the National Agricultural Frontier (Frontera Agrícola Nacional), which defines the areas in which agricultural activities can be carried out and those subject to legal restrictions or exclusions, such as moorlands, natural parks or forest reserves. This tool is essential, as it enables the identification of risks and limitations in land use, ensuring that production, as in the case of coffee, is carried out in a manner compatible with environmental conservation and current regulations.

In Colombia, coffee growing takes place primarily within the National Agricultural Frontier. Of the 487,353 registered coffee production units, 46% are located within this frontier, 38% in areas with restrictions, and 17% in areas with legal exclusions. Thanks to the Coffee Information System (SICA), which integrates more than 2.2 million georeferenced plots in 23 departments and 610 municipalities, it is possible to accurately determine the location of crops. Exclusions include strategic ecosystems such as moorlands, natural parks, and forest reserves, while restrictions refer to ethnic territories, environmental management areas, and disaster risk zones. This context highlights the need for coffee production to be developed under sustainability criteria and in harmony with environmental conservation and respect for collective territories.

### CATEGORY 2 ENVIRONMENTAL PROTECTION

In Colombia, environmental regulations establish areas where agricultural activities are prohibited or restricted, which is essential for complying with standards such as the EUDR. In exclusion areas, such as moorlands, protected areas, or forest reserves, coffee and other products may not be grown, except in very specific cases where crops existed before the area was declared protected or when agroforests compatible with conservation are integrated. There are also territories with subject to specific restrictions, such as indigenous reserves, Afro-descendent community lands, or wetlands, where productive use may occur under particular rules and sustainable practices. In this context, the National Agricultural Frontier is the official tool that allows for the precise identification of which areas are suitable, which have restrictions, and where production conversion processes may be required, facilitating solid environmental due diligence in the face of non-compliance risks.

In addition to territorial exclusions and constraints, in Colombia it is essential to take into account other key environmental factors for adequate due diligence. These include soil management practices that prevent erosion and maintain fertility, responsible use of agricultural inputs to prevent contamination by agrochemicals, and proper handling of spills and solid waste that may affect nearby water bodies or ecosystems. Likewise, deforestation, pressure on water sources, and natural disasters such as landslides and floods reinforce the need for more sustainable production planning. These elements, together with legal verification of land use, form the core environmental risks and responsibilities that must be assessed when investing or producing in the Colombian agricultural sector.

The Colombian coffee sector does not face significant environmental challenges in terms of areas of production, although it does face challenges in fruit processing. Primary processing requires continuous attention to ensure sustainability, as in some cases it can generate spills into soil and water sources. While these requirements can create legal and operational uncertainty for producers, they have also driven significant advances in cleaner practices, such as ecological processing, micro-watershed management, and water decontamination. Coffee production is not associated with deforestation; studies of deforestation for coffee production based on analyses in departments such as Huila, the main national producer, show a decline in associated deforestation from 2020 to date.

### CATEGORY 3 THIRD PARTIES' RIGHTS AND FPIC

In Colombia, prior consultation and Free, Prior and Informed Consent (FPIC) are distinct but complementary mechanisms for guaranteeing the rights of indigenous and Afro-Colombian communities. FPIC, backed by the 1991 Constitution and ILO Convention 169, is mandatory when a project threatens the physical, cultural or spiritual survival of a people, such as in cases of forced relocation or impact on sacred sites. Agricultural activity must respect cultural and natural heritage, avoiding impacts on collective territories and ensuring effective participation processes. Beyond a legal obligation, FPIC strengthens trust and promotes sustainable and culturally respectful agricultural production, as demonstrated by coffee experiences in Tolima and Cauca. To comply with the EUDR, due diligence must include verification of respect for ethnic and territorial rights.

The risk for the coffee sector in relation to third-party rights is negligible, given that most producers are smallholders located outside collective areas or within them but carried out by members of these communities.

In Colombia, agricultural labour relations are regulated by standards designed to guarantee decent working conditions including formal contracts, respect for working hours and minimum wages, provision of protective equipment, social security registration, freedom of association, and prevention of discrimination and harassment. The coffee sector provides approximately 1.6 million jobs<sup>5</sup> , but most of these are temporary and itinerant, which makes formalisation and social security coverage difficult. Although this does not necessarily imply illegality, it does reflect structural limitations for fully complying with international standards such as those required by the EUDR.

Occupational risks in a predominantly family-based coffee industry are low. However, there may be specific risks in units larger than 5 hectares. For temporary work, typical in the coffee sector, the seasonality of the harvest requires a large workforce for short periods. The Colombian labour system does not yet have simplified mechanisms adapted to the temporary and itinerant nature of agricultural work. Currently, regulations require formal contracts and full monthly social security contributions, even when employment lasts only days or weeks, which is complicated for small producers. The labour reform (Law 2466 of 2025) reinforces formalisation in the agricultural sector but does not introduce flexible schemes for temporary work, although the regulatory decrees have not yet been issued.

According to information from SICA, by 2024, 96.7% of coffee growers in Colombia will cultivate less than 5 hectares of coffee, classifying them as small-scale coffee growers. In these economic units, the use of household labour and financial resources are predominately used to ensure productive activity. As evidence of this, and to refine this analysis, figures from Colombia's National Administrative Department of Statistics (DANE) and its National Agricultural Survey (ENA) show that in 76.7% of coffee-growing UPAS (Agricultural Production Units), 100% of farm labour is provided by adults within the household. Additionally, 74% of these UPAS occupy plots of less than 5 hectares, while 26% of other coffee growers hire at least one permanent worker. This 5-hectare threshold is used in the following due diligence recommendations. It should be noted that the analyses and conclusions obtained were based on general trends and do not preclude exceptions. Overall, 96.7% of coffee growers in Colombia have less than 5 hectares (SICA 2024), which means that most of them are small-scale producers reliant on family and subsistence economies, without prejudice to exceptions.

The EUDR defines the legality of commodities according to two criteria:

National legal requirements must refer to the legal status of the production area. There are seven legal areas [for agricultural products]: land-use rights; environmental protection; the rights of third parties; labour rights; human rights protected under international law; the principle of Free, Prior and Informed Consent, as set out in the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples; and tax, anti-corruption, trade and customs regulations.

On 2 October 2024 (updated in April 2025), the European Commission published a guidance document for the implementation of the EUDR, which interprets the Regulation's provisions on the legality criterion. The document aims to assess the relevance of legal requirements according to the following criteria:

The requirements have a specific impact on the legal status of the commodity production area [with the exception of tax, trade and customs standards]. The requirements are linked to the Regulation's objectives of halting deforestation and forest degradation, as part of the EU's commitment to address climate change and biodiversity loss.

Given that not all labour requirements, as well as certain human rights and third parties' rights, are directly related to the objectives of the EUDR, there is debate as to whether they fall within its scope of application.

In addition, the guidance document extends the scope of tax and anti-corruption regulations to steps in the value chain beyond the area of production. It should be noted that the Commission's guidance document is not legally binding. As specified in the guidance document itself, it does not replace, add to or modify the EUDR's provisions, which establish the legal obligations. Its purpose it to harmonise the implementation of the Regulation across EU member states.

Each EU Member State will adopt its own approach to monitoring operators' implementation of the EUDR. Ultimately, only the courts in each EU country can interpret EU regulations in a legally binding manner and determine the scope of the legality test.

In this context, this tool takes a precautionary approach. It considers all areas of law listed in Article 2.40 and includes all requirements that are considered relevant in these seven areas. It indicates with an asterisk those legal requirements that are not directly linked to the objectives of the EUDR.

This comprehensive approach will allow end users to choose the scope of their work based on their reading of the EUDR and the accompanying guidance.

### **Land tenure regime**

In Colombia, land is a fundamental pillar of rural development, cultural identity, and social justice. The Colombian legal framework recognises various forms of tenure, aiming to balance individual property rights with the collective interest and the social function that property must fulfil. Within this context, two essential concepts are distinguished: **private property**, understood as the legally recognised right to property, and legitimate possession, which refers to the material and continuous exercise of rights over land, even in the absence of a formal title. Both enjoy legal protection and, under certain conditions, may give rise to the consolidation of ownership rights.

Possession represents a meeting point between social reality and legal formality. In rural Colombia, it is common for farmers, rural communities and ethnic groups to occupy and work land without a registered title, while exercising legitimate and prolonged possession. The legal system has recognised this situation and created mechanisms to formalise it, allowing holders to obtain title through administrative or judicial processes. In this way, possession becomes a path to legal certainty and the strengthening of the smallholder farming economy.

Private property is protected by the 1991 Constitution, which enshrines it as an inviolable right, although subject to social and ecological functions. Civil and agrarian laws develop this protection, regulating how property can be acquired, transferred and used, while incorporating principles that guarantee responsible and sustainable use. In this way, land is not conceived solely as an economic asset, but as a vital space for production, culture and environmental balance.

## Land-use rights

The Colombian State has also recognised the collective territorial rights of indigenous peoples, Afro-descendant communities and the Romani or Gypsy people. Through collective titling, the preservation of their ancestral territories, respect for their cultural identity and autonomy in land use are guaranteed. These entities—such as indigenous reserves and Afro-Colombian collective territories—are examples of collective property ownership with historical and legal value, backed by national laws and international instruments such as ILO Convention 169.

In addition, agrarian and civil legislation provides for various forms of use and exploitation of rural plots, such as lease agreements, loan agreements (*comodato*), usufruct, sharecropping (*aparcería*) or *anticresis* (an arrangement whereby a creditor uses a debtor's property to satisfy interest or debt), which allow for different degrees of access, administration and benefit from the land. These mechanisms are essential for promoting rural productivity, facilitating cooperation between landowners and workers, and ensuring sustainable land use.

Finally, land restitution is a central pillar of Colombia's policy of reparation for victims of the armed conflict. Through the Victims Law and complementary decrees, administrative and judicial procedures have been established to return the territories from which communities were dispossessed to smallholder farming families, indigenous communities, and Afro-descendant communities. This policy seeks not only to restore property rights, but also to rebuild the social fabric and ensure the dignified return of displaced populations.

In short, Colombia's legal framework on land reflects an effort to articulate social justice, historical recognition and sustainable rural development. Ownership, possession and collective territorial rights are not isolated categories, but interdependent elements that shape a comprehensive vision of the territory as a common good, a productive resource and a living space.

### **Legal regime for land use**

In Colombia, land management aims to balance agricultural production with environmental protection and sustainable development. One of the key instruments for achieving this is the delimitation of the agricultural frontier, which defines the areas where agricultural activities can be carried out without affecting strategic ecosystems such as moorlands, forests, or wetlands. Formalised by the Ministry of Agriculture in 2018 based on studies by the UPRA, this delimitation makes it possible to identify areas that are suitable, conditional or excluded for production, guiding the scaling up of agriculture and preventing conflicts over land use. In turn, the Policy on Productive and Social Land Use Planning for Rural Property promotes land use that responds to its natural vocation and criteria of sustainability, productivity, and equity.

Within this framework, Peasant Reserve Areas (*Zonas de Reserva Campesina*) play an essential role in protecting the rural economy and preventing the uncontrolled expansion of the agricultural frontier. All these strategies are linked to the commitments of the 2016 Peace Agreement, which proposes a national environmental zoning plan and comprehensive rural reform.

Land use planning in Colombia, primarily regulated by Law 388 of 1997, establishes Land Use Plans (POT) as mandatory planning instruments for municipalities and districts. These plans determine land use, incorporate environmental and risk criteria, and guide the balanced occupation of the territory. Their periodic updating ensures that urbanisation and rural development processes remain within sustainable limits. Together, the delimitation of the agricultural frontier and the POTs provide the foundation of a land use policy that aims to harmonise production, environmental conservation, and the well-being of the country's rural and urban communities.

In Colombia, environmental protection and sustainable management of natural resources are based on the 1991 Constitution, which recognises the right of all people to enjoy a healthy environment and grants special protection to natural parks due to their inalienable and imprescriptible nature. A robust legal framework has been developed that seeks to harmonise ecosystem conservation with the country's productive activities. From Law 2 of 1959, which defined large national forest reserves, to the most recent standards on waste management and emission control, environmental regulation has evolved towards a comprehensive approach to sustainability.

**Protected areas**, regulated by the National System of Protected Areas (SINAP), include different categories ranging from strict conservation to integrated management. Agricultural activities are restricted in these areas, although sustainable uses are permitted in specific contexts, such as Integrated Management Districts or Civil Society Nature Reserves. Complementarily, standards such as Law 1753 of 2015, as well as international commitments derived from the Ramsar Convention, protect wetlands and water sources from the scaling up of agricultural activities and intensive land use.

**Water, soil and waste management** is another essential pillar of environmental policy. Colombian regulations require permits for the use of water resources, with exceptions for dispersed rural dwellings, and promote water conservation and efficient use through specific plans. In relation to spills, Resolution 631 of 2015 establishes maximum pollutant limits for productive activities, including agricultural activities, in order to prevent the contamination of rivers and streams. Although small subsistence producers are exempt from Resolution 631 and from the implementation of complex procedures, they must ensure basic wastewater treatment through adequate systems.

With regard to **solid and hazardous waste**, legislation led by Decree 2981 of 2013 and Decree 4741 of 2005 requires municipalities, companies, and producers to implement comprehensive management plans that prioritise the reduction, reuse, and safe disposal of waste. The most recent resolutions reinforce the shared responsibility of manufacturers and importers in the collection and treatment of containers, packaging and chemicals, extending the chain of environmental responsibility beyond the point of consumption.

**Law 2111 of 2021** amends the Penal Code by **deforestation as a criminal offence in Article 330**. It does not establish a cut-off date or baseline for deforestation, nor does it use geographic information layers in the explanatory memorandum, but it does elevate deforestation to the status of a crime, effective 29 July 2021.

**Soil and air** also have specific protection standards. National policies promote agroecological practices, erosion prevention, and the restoration of degraded land, while air quality regulations prohibit open burning and promote the sustainable use of biomass. These measures are reinforced by criminal penalties, such as the criminalisation of deforestation in 2021, which demonstrates the state's commitment to combating forest cover loss and protecting biodiversity.

This regulatory framework reflects a vision of a country that seeks to balance agricultural productivity with environmental conservation. Rather than isolated prohibitions, environmental standards in Colombia make up a coordinated system that promotes sustainability, responsible use of natural resources, and shared responsibility among the state, rural communities, and the productive sector.

In Colombia, the concepts of "prior consultation" and "Free, Prior and Informed Consent" (FPIC) tend to be used interchangeably, but in law they are not the same. Free, Prior and Informed Consent (FPIC) is an essential principle, superior to consultation, which seeks to ensure that indigenous and Afro-Colombian communities actively participate in decisions that may significantly affect their territories, culture and ways of life. Prior consultation, on the other hand, is the right to be consulted and to participate; it seeks agreement but is not always binding. The 1991 Constitution recognises the ethnic and cultural diversity of the nation, protects historical and archaeological heritage, and grants these communities territorial and jurisdictional autonomy, principles reinforced by ILO Convention 169. Within this framework, agricultural activity must be carried out with respect for cultural and natural heritage, avoiding or minimising impacts on sacred sites, ancestral resources and habitats of cultural or historical importance. This entails recognising territorial rights, ensuring that productive projects comply with conservation standards and, when there is a risk of impact, implementing prior consultation processes that guarantee effective participation and equitable agreements. In Colombia, the Constitutional Court has ruled that Free, Prior and Informed Consent (FPIC) does not replace prior consultation but rather complements and strengthens it when a project or measure threatens the physical, cultural or spiritual survival of a community. In such cases, FPIC is mandatory, including situations involving forced transfers or relocations, the exploitation of resources that compromises the cultural or economic integrity of communities, or impacts on sacred sites essential to their survival.

Beyond being a legal obligation, FPIC is a tool that builds trust between producers, communities and the state, and ensures that agricultural development is guided by sustainability criteria and respect for cultural diversity. Examples such as the coffee initiatives in Tolima and Cauca, where indigenous communities have transformed coffee into a symbol of peace, identity and reconciliation, show that agricultural production can be compatible with cultural and environmental protection when relationships of dialogue and cooperation are established. In this regard, due diligence under the EUDR must include verification of respect for ethnic rights, protection of collective territories and the implementation of FPIC as an indispensable condition for responsible production, particularly whenever there are indications of non-compliance or the presence of indigenous or Afro-Colombian communities.

**The Colombian labour framework**, based on the **Substantive Labour Code (Código Sustantivo de Trabajo, CST)** and supplemented by various laws, establishes the basis for regulating relations between employers and workers in all economic sectors. This code sets out various types of contracts that adapted to the nature of the activities and the duration of the employment relationship. According to Article 23 of the CST, an **employment contract**  requires three essential elements, namely (i) the personal activity of the worker, (ii) the subordination or dependence of the worker on the employer, and (iii) a salary as remuneration for the service. If any of these elements is missing, the employment relationship is not established. It is therefore understood that in cases of subsistence family economy, there are **generally** no employment relationships. A **contract for work or labour** is used for specific tasks or projects and ends once its purpose has been fulfilled, although it is not commonly used in agricultural activities. A **fixed-term contract** has a specific duration that cannot exceed three years and must be formalised in writing, while an **indefinite-term contract** has no end date and can be agreed orally or in writing. There are also special types of contracts, such as **apprenticeship contracts**, which provide practical training for young people for a maximum of two years, and **occasional or temporary contracts**, which are used for non-routine, short-term work lasting up to 30 days.

With regard to **working hours**, Colombian legislation has introduced measures to increase flexibility and gradually reduce working hours. **Law 2101 of 2021** established the maximum weekly working hours at 42 hours, distributed over days agreed between the parties, while **Article 161 of the CST**  sets the daily limit at eight hours. These provisions seek to balance productivity with worker welfare. In terms of wages, the code distinguishes between **fixed, variable, comprehensive and in-kind wages**, ensuring compliance with the **current legal minimum wage,** which is adjusted annually.

The CST also establishes **employers' obligations regarding occupational health** and safety, requiring adequate conditions, protective equipment, and preventive measures to avoid accidents or occupational diseases. At the same time, **equal treatment and non-discrimination** are fundamental principles recognised by the Constitution and international ILO conventions, guaranteeing that all workers — regardless of gender, race or social status — have the same rights and opportunities.

**Freedom of association**, protected by Article 39 of the 1991 Constitution, gives workers and employers the right to associate to defend their interests. The CST specifies the requirements for creating trade unions, promoting dialogue and collective bargaining as pillars of labour relations. In terms of social security, all employers must enrol their workers in **health, pension and occupational risk** systems, while independent contractors are responsible for their own enrolment and contributions, under the supervision of the contracting party.

**Law 2114 of 2021 and Law 1822 of 2017** consolidated the extension of **maternity leave** to 18 weeks and **paternity leave** to eight working days, in addition to recognising leave for bereavement and family emergencies. Finally, **Law 1010 of 2006, supplemented by Law 2365 of 2024,**  strengthened protection against **workplace and sexual harassment**, establishing mechanisms for prevention, reporting and punishment within work environments.

These regulations aim to promote fairer, safer, and more equitable labour relations, balancing respect for workers' dignity with business productivity and the country's economic development. However, it is acknowledged that the system and standards are still not fully adapted to the realities and conditions of rural areas.

In Colombia, labour legislation establishes a solid framework for protecting children and young people from child labour and forms of labour exploitation. The **minimum age for working is 15,** subject to special conditions and prior authorisation from the Ministry of Labour or the competent authority. This framework seeks to ensure that the participation of adolescents in the world of work does not interfere with their right to education, health or holistic development.

Articles 171 and 424 of the **Substantive Labour Code (CST)** expressly prohibit the employment of minors under the age of 18 in work considered dangerous or unhealthy, such as work involving exposure to toxic substances, heavy machinery or physically hazardous conditions. This protection is reinforced by the **Childhood and Adolescence Code (Law 1098 of 2006),** which regulates the working hours of adolescent workers, limiting them to a maximum of six hours per day and 30 hours per week for children aged 15 to 17. In addition, it prohibits night work—defined as work between 6:00 p.m. and 6:00 a.m.—and requires that any productive activity be carried out in safe environments that are compatible with their educational process.

At the international level, Colombia has incorporated fundamental commitments through instruments of the **International Labour Organisation (ILO). Law 515 of 1999 ratified Convention 138**, which sets the minimum age for admission to employment, and **Law 12 of 1991** ratified the **Convention on the Rights of the Child**, consolidating the State's obligation to protect minors from any form of economic exploitation. Similarly, **Law 1622 of 2013,**  which established the Youth Citizenship Statute, recognises special rights and guarantees for young people, strengthening their social and labour participation in dignified conditions.

In rural areas, child labour presents particular characteristics. Colombian law **does not prohibit family agricultural work** if it is occasional, light and within the context of family culture or the household economy. Under these conditions, such activities are considered a form of learning or family collaboration rather than a formal employment relationship. However, the law is clear that these activities must not affect the health, education or integrity of the minor, and must never involve economic exploitation or coercion.

The country has also taken strong measures against **forced labour** and **human trafficking,** in compliance with **ILO Conventions 29 and 105,** approved by **Law 23 of 1967** and developed by **Decree 1280 of 1997.** These instruments commit the Colombian State to eliminating all forms of labour imposed through threat or coercion. Accordingly, **Law 985 of 2005** criminalises and punishes human trafficking, recognising it as a modern form of slavery and labour exploitation.

Finally, **Article 59 of the CST** prohibits practices that may generate economic dependence or coercion, such as improper wage deductions or the withholding of documents, which could constitute evidence of forced labour. This regulatory framework seeks not only to prevent child and forced labour, but also to promote an ethical, dignified and safe working environment for all people, particularly those in vulnerable situations.

## Tax, anti-corruption, trade and customs regulations

In the Colombian agricultural sector, taxation, trade and the fight against corruption are key elements in ensuring legality and transparency within the EUDR framework. Productive activity, particularly coffee production, is regulated by mechanisms such as parafiscal levies, which fund research programmes, technical assistance and institutional strengthening in the sector. These instruments, together with customs and trade controls, facilitate product traceability and strengthen trust in international markets. In addition, the organisational tradition of the coffee industry and the country's institutional strength have contributed to maintaining a low risk of corruption in coffee production and marketing, which supports the implementation of the standard due diligence requirements set by European regulations.

In Colombia, coffee production and trade are governed by an institutional system that clearly regulates taxation, trade and customs, contributing to a low-risk environment for corruption in this activity. Coffee exports are not subject to direct taxes but are subject to parafiscal contributions administered by the National Federation of Coffee Growers. These contributions are used to finance extension services, guarantee coffee purchases through the National Coffee Fund, and support research, promotion and sustainability in the sector. In terms of customs, the country has a solid regulatory framework that guarantees the traceability and legality of exported beans, monitored by DIAN and aligned with internationally recognised standards. This scheme, combined with the highly organised nature of the coffee sector, allows the Colombian coffee value chain to be perceived as transparent and at low risk of corrupt practices, which is relevant in the context of the due diligence requirements established by the EUDR.

Coffee is recognised as a legal and traditional agricultural product, with a strongly institutionalised value chain backed by the National Federation of Coffee Growers and other state entities that ensure its traceability and transparency. Although there are economic sectors in the country that have been used for money laundering or other illicit practices, coffee has not been identified as a high-risk item due to its strict control systems, the formal nature of its exports, and the monitoring of parafiscal and customs contributions. Consequently, the risk of coffee farming being linked to corruption or money laundering is considered low, which strengthens confidence in this sector regarding the legality standards and due diligence required in international markets such as Europe.

The process of identifying the legal requirements applicable to coffee chains under the European Union Regulation on Deforestation-free Products (EUDR) was carried in Colombia as a collaboration between various public, private and civil society entities. The National Federation of Coffee Growers (FNC) provided significant leadership, supported by EFI through the Technical Facility on Deforestation-free Value Chains11, with additional backing for stakeholder management and communications from the Traceability and Sustainability of Export Products project, implemented by the Particip and Idem Concept consortium (TRACE COL), funded by the European Union. EFI, TRACE COL, the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Development, and SwissContact formed the committee that acted as the technical secretariat and coordinated the development of the process.

The purpose of the process was to develop, with technical and political legitimacy, the proposal for legal requirements described in this document to guide operators in the implementation of the EUDR, while ensuring competitiveness in international markets and sustainability in Peruvian Colombian agricultural production. From the outset, the process was conceived as a transparent, inclusive exercise based on open dialogue, integrating the voices of public entities, business associations, export companies, producer organisations, regional governments, civil society and academia.

Although the tool is based on the work of national and international experts in law and due diligence, its greatest contribution has come through technical consultation with all national and international actors in the coffee sector, including the Ministry of Trade, Industry and Tourism, Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Development, Ministry of Environment and Sustainable Development, the Rural Agricultural Planning Unit (UPRA), and ProColombia, as well as trade associations such as the National Federation of Coffee Growers (FNC), Asoexport, the APS Colombia Corporation, the National Association of Industrialists (ANDI), and civil society organisations.

The legal mapping was based on analyses of the Colombian regulatory framework carried out by the Coffee, Forest and Climate Agreement, ANDI and the Federation of Coffee Growers. Based on these analyses and using the taxonomy proposed by EFI and Preferred by Nature, Dr Jaime Augusto Correa Medina, a Colombian lawyer with a PhD in Law from the Externado University of Colombia specialising in procedural and agricultural law, and a researcher and lecturer, was commissioned by EFI to compile the relevant national regulations. He also developed concepts on certain legal requirements, such as regulations related to deforestation and forest use, and participated in deliberation sessions with actors in the chain to clarify the concerns and strengthen the debates.

November 2024 and January 2025: Working sessions of the Technical Working Group on legality to consolidate, review and adjust the "EUDR Colombia Legality Matrix", identifying key aspects of legal implementation in Colombia in relation to the EUDR and addressing issues such as land tenure, restitution and expropriation. 8 April 2025: The technical working group met at the EU delegation for a session on reviewing the legal requirements. 29 May 2025: A hybrid meeting was held at the Bankers' Club-Asoexport, attended by more than 60 participants to discuss progress and receive feedback from stakeholders in the coffee sector, government, trade associations and civil society on the progress of the analysis. 29 July 2025: A hybrid multi-stakeholder meeting was held to review the risk matrix and the level of implementation for the coffee sector. 2 September 2025: Working meeting with the Coffee Growers Federation, the UPRA, the European Union Delegation and the Ministry of Environment and Sustainable Development. 7 October 2025: Meeting with an expanded group to share and obtain feedback on the final results of the analysis of the legal requirements relevant to the coffee sector and the analysis of the level of implementation of these requirements, as well as the proposed actions for due diligence.
