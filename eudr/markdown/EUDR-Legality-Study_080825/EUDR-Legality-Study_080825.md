This report was prepared as part of eΊorts to strengthen understanding of the legality requirements in the *European Union Deforestation Regulation* (EUDR) and its relevance to the regulatory framework in Indonesia.

This regulation requires compliance with legality, deforestation-free, and traceability aspects for key commodities traded in the European Union market. # EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

**The European Union Deforestation Regulation (EUDR)** aims to reduce EU consumption of products that contribute to deforestation and forest degradation. Companies placing relevant products on the EU market must ensure that their commodities are legally produced in accordance with their respective national laws and produced without deforestation after 2020.

This study aims to provide information on existing relevant Indonesian legislations to be considered by operators when doing their due diligence and provide references for supply chain actors to comply with relevant Indonesian regulations in the context of EUDR requirements. The scope of this study is the laws and regulations, both at the national and sub-national levels, that are relevant to the cocoa, coΊee, and natural rubber commodities, with additional coverage on the laws and regulations relevant to the timber sector.

The methodology used in this study involved desk review research, interviews, and focus group discussions with stakeholders. This study is of a quasi-academic, informative, and non-concluding nature. The EU does not provide any guidance on the specific laws and regulations operators and traders must follow in Indonesia. Per the regulation, it is for the operators to ensure that relevant laws and regulations in the country of production are respected in the context of their due diligence obligations and for the competent authorities conducting checks on operators to assess whether operators' due diligence system and relevant documentation are compliant with legal production of relevant products. As such, this legality study only serves to help guide the due diligence process; it is neither exhaustive nor binding.

As stated in the EUDR (Article 2 (40)), *"'relevant legislation of the country of production' means the laws applicable in the country of production concerning the legal status of the area of production in terms of: as (a) land use rights; (b) environmental protection; (c) forest-related rules, including forest management and biodiversity conservation, where directly related to wood harvesting; (d) third parties' rights; (e) labour rights; (f) human rights protected under international law; (g) the principle of free, prior and informed consent (FPIC), including as set out in the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples; (h) tax, anti-corruption, trade and customs regulation".* Some key legislation to consider is as follows:

On **land use rights**, Indonesia follows a state-based land tenure system under Law No. 5/1960, where land is state-controlled but plantation actors can obtain cultivation rights (Hak Guna Usaha or HGU) for up to 35 years, extendable by another 25 years. These rights must be registered and can be revoked if the land is left unused. Plantation land use is subject to spatial planning obligations, with maximum limits defined by commodity type: rubber (23,000 ha), cocoa (13,000 ha), and coΊee (13,000 ha).

In terms of **environmental protection**, multiple laws impose clear obligations on plantation operators to safeguard biodiversity, mitigate environmental harm, and maintain sustainable land use. For example, Law No. 32/2009 and Law No. 22/2019 emphasise responsible cultivation and ecosystem protection. Environmental damage, especially through land clearing or fire, is addressed through Law No. 39/2014 and GR 22/2021. The use of agrochemicals, particularly pesticides, is tightly regulated, requiring registration with the Ministry of Agriculture under the ministry's Regulation 43/2019 and compliance with safety and training protocols. Waste management is governed under Law 32/2009 and GR 22/2021 while water and soil protection are addressed through Law 17/2019, Law 37/2014, and Law 41/1999.

The **forestry legal framework** is anchored in Law 41/1999 and supplemented by GR 23/2021 and Law 18/2013. It lays out comprehensive rules for sustainable forest management, zoning, rehabilitation, and biodiversity protection. Timber legality assurance in Indonesia is regulated through the Timber Legality Assurance System (SVLK), ensuring traceability and legal compliance throughout the supply chain. Indonesia also has a timber legality scheme with the European Union, the Voluntary Partnership Agreement part of the Forest Law Enforcement, Governance and Trade (FLEGT VPA). The EUDR states that "*To respect ongoing bilateral commitments and to preserve the progress achieved with partner countries that have an operating system in place (FLEGT licensing stage) and work, where relevant and agreed, with current VPA partners towards them reaching that stage, this Regulation should include a provision declaring that wood and wood products that are covered by a valid FLEGT license are deemed to comply with the legality requirement of Regulation".*

With respect to **third-party rights**, Law 39/2014 requires plantation companies to engage in inclusive and mutually beneficial partnerships with smallholders and local communities. Companies must allocate 20% of their cultivated area to community plantations and are obligated to provide social facilities and human resource development programs. These obligations are further supported by Law 19/2013 on the Protection and Empowerment of Farmers.

In the area of **labour rights**, law 1/1970 covers occupational safety and health, while Law 21/2000 guarantees unionisation rights and protection from discrimination. Workers are entitled to fair wages, social security, rest periods, maternity leave, and religious accommodations. Child labour is strictly prohibited, and special considerations are extended to workers with disabilities. On **human rights**, the 1945 Constitution and Law 39/1999 guarantee a range of rights, including the right to life, health, education, personal freedom, decent work, and protection for vulnerable groups such as women, children, and persons with disabilities.

The **rights of Indigenous Peoples** are constitutionally recognised and supported by national law. Indonesia legally acknowledges customary land rights, and unauthorised use of Indigenous land can result in heavy penalties, including imprisonment and fines. These rights align with the principle of Free, Prior, and Informed Consent (FPIC) under the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples framework. The Reformasi period has also seen legislative progress inΌuenced by civil society advocacy, although eΊorts for full implementation and recognition are ongoing.

Indonesia's **tax and governance regulations** align with EUDR expectations around transparency, integrity, and anti-corruption. Taxes aΊecting plantation actors include income tax (PPh), VAT (PPN), and land/building tax (PBB), governed by Laws 6/1983, 7/1983, and 7/2021. Anti-corruption is addressed through Law 31/1999 and the role of the Corruption Eradication Commission (KPK), reinforced by public whistleblower protections under GR 43/2018. Trade and customs activities are regulated under Law 7/2014 and GR 29/2021, ensuring standards and licensing are met, particularly for exports. Timber exports are further governed by Presidential Decrees 48/1977 and 39/1979, which aim to stabilise domestic industry development.

**Data protection** is recognised as a constitutional right, codified in Law 27/2022. This law outlines data subject rights, including consent, access, correction, and deletion of personal data. Plantation geospatial data, including HGU maps, is considered public information based on Law 14/2008 and confirmed by a Supreme Court ruling, unless specifically exempted for national security.

Finally, Indonesia's framework for **traceability** is regulated through GR 26/2021 and Ministry of Agriculture Regulation 53/2018, requiring agricultural information systems that support traceability. For timber, Indonesia implements the SILK (Sistem Informasi Legalitas Kayu) platform under SVLK, which allows public access to data on timber legality and supply chain transparency.

In conclusion, while Indonesia's regulatory landscape presents a comprehensive framework for compliance with the EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR), the complexity of overlapping laws, multiple responsible ministries, and varying land tenure systems pose significant challenges, particularly concerning land rights and Indigenous peoples' rights. Plantation business actors must prioritise legal land use verification, community consultation and respect customary land claims. Equally, due diligence processes must ensure traceability, inclusion of smallholders, and adherence to environmental and labour standards.

# 1. INTRODUCTION

The European Union Regulation on Deforestation Free Products, or EUDR has been in force since June 2023. Main objective of the EUDR is to reduce the EU's contribution to global deforestation and forest degradation and encourage the consumption and trade of deforestation-free products'. The EUDR states that, companies placing products on the EU market or export them from there must ensure that the products are produced legally in accordance with the **relevant laws and regulations in the country of production** (EUDR Art. 9 1 (d) and Art. 2 (28)) and without deforestation after the cut-oΊ date.

Commodities included in the EUDR and relevant for Indonesia are cocoa, coΊee, oil palm, natural rubber and timber/wood as well as some derived products. As far as legal production of commodities is concerned, the EUDR states that commodities need to be produced in accordance with national legislation in the country of production. Until now, eΊorts to analyze the extent of such coherence have not been carried out comprehensively in the Indonesian context.

This study was conducted to assess how well Indonesian regulations align with the European Union Deforestation Regulation (EUDR), specifically Article 3(b) and Article 2(40). The goal is to evaluate policy coherence and identify any gaps or actions that might be needed from a policy perspective to ensure compliance with the EUDR.With the EU regulation on deforestation-free products (EUDR), the EU aims to **minimise the EU's contribution to deforestation and forest degradation** worldwide and thereby to minimise its impact on its contribution to mitigate **climate change**, reduce **greenhouse gas emissions** and **biodiversity loss**.

The EUDR obliges companies to ensure through due diligence that relevant commodities and products placed on the EU market or exported from there are **produced without deforestation and, in the case of timber products, without forest degradation after 2020** and in **compliance with relevant legislation of the country of production**. It applies to cattle, cocoa, coΊee, oil palm, rubber, soya and wood as well as certain derived products. In the case of products imported into the EU , the company importing the relevant commodity or product takes the role of an operator and must fulfill due diligence obligations. The EUDR does not contain any direct obligations on companies further upstream.

The **benchmarking** of countries inside and outside the EU will direct checks on EU operators by EU competent authorities based on the level of risk of areas they source from. Countries assigned to a low risk category face reduced checks on operators sourcing from there by EU competent authorities as well as simplified due diligence obligations for operators. A high-risk profiling increases the number of checks on operators by the EU competent authorities.

As defined by Article 30 on "**cooperation with third countries**", the EU will work closely with partner countries on the consumption and production side to achieve the objectives of the Regulation. The regulation will enter into force from the end of 2025 for larger companies and from mid-2026 for small and medium enterprises (SMEs).The basis for determining whether a relevant commodity or relevant product has been produced **in accordance with the relevant legislation of the country of production** is the legislation of the country in which the commodity, or in the case of a product, the commodity contained in a relevant product was grown, harvested, obtained from or raised on relevant plots of land or, as regards cattle, in establishments.

The EUDR takes a Όexible approach by listing a number of areas of law without specifying particular laws, as these diΊer from country to country and may be subject to amendments. However, only the applicable laws **concerning the legal status of the area of production** constitute relevant legislation pursuant to Article 2(40) of the EUDR. This means that generally the relevance of laws for the legality requirement in Article 3(b) of the EUDR is not determined by the fact that they may apply generally during the production process of commodities or apply to the supply chains of relevant products and relevant commodities. Instead, the relevance of legislation is determined by the fact that these laws specifically impact or inΌuence the legal status of the area in which the commodities were produced.

Additionally, Article 2(40) of the EUDR must be read in the light of **the objectives of the EUDR** as laid down in Article 1(1)(a) and (b). That means that legislation is relevant if its contents can be linked to halting deforestation and forest degradation.

This study is of quasi-academic, informative and non-concluding nature. No prescription from the EU will be given regarding the specific laws and regulations in Indonesia that operators must abide by in Indonesia. Per the regulation, it is for the operators to ensure that relevant laws and regulations are respected in the context of their due diligence obligations and for the competent authorities conducting checks on operators to assess whether operators' due diligence system and relevant documentation demonstrate legal production of relevant products. As such, this legality study only serves to help guide the due diligence process.

Operators and interested parties should refer to the EUDR legal text and EUDR Guidance document for ofÏcial EU information on obligations to meet the requirements set out in the Regulation.

# 3. ASSESMENT OF RELEVANT INDONESIAN LAWS AND REGULATIONS WITH THE RULES REQUIRED IN THE EUDR

This chapter outlines the applicable national-level laws and regulations in Indonesia pertaining the plantation sector, especially for the cocoa, coΊee, and natural rubber commodities, with additional coverage on the timber sector, in relevance to the requirements of the EUDR. While this chapter highlights the most relevant aspects in the context of the complex and comprehensive legislative framework, more detailed information on identified relevant legislation can be found in the annex.

Regulations enacted at the regional level as a response to the national legislation are also quoted in the Annex.

# 3.1. Land Use Rights

There are two aspects related to Land use rights, namely:

## (1) Land tenure and management rights are secured

Based on Law No. 5/1960, the legal framework governing land use in Indonesia stipulates that all land is controlled by the state (Article 2(1)), which holds the authority to give land rights to individuals, either as sole owners or jointly with others, as well as to legal entities (Article 4(2)), allowing them to own and utilize land for specific purposes. One of the most significant land rights granted to plantation business actors is the **Right to Cultivate (Hak Guna Usaha/HGU),** a right that enables these entities to utilize state-controlled land for agricultural, fisheries, or livestock activities, including the establishment of plantations, for a legally defined period.

Indonesia imposes diΊerent rules between forestry and agriculture. In the forestry sector, timber produced can be sourced from state forests with forest product production status, including timber. This context is included in the forest area utilization section, where the utilization permit can be given to parties that meet the criteria and can apply the principles of sustainability throughout the process. In addition to state forests, forest timber can also come from private forests. Private forest is land that has legal rights from the state. Private forests are owned by individuals with the status of proprietary forests, not state forests.

There is currently a Minister of Environment and Forestry Regulation No. P.32/ Menlhk-Setjen/2015 on Private Forest which guarantees the rights of farmers who own private forest and produce timber from their land. Additionally, there is Regulation of the Minister of Forestry of the Republic of Indonesia Number: P.31/Menhut-II/2010 concerning Cost Standards for Performance Assessment of Sustainable Production Forest Management and Timber Legality Verification of Permit Holders or Private Forest Holders.

The transformation of regulations on timber legality in Indonesia, including from private forest, since 2009 until now has changed 62 times. and the current regulation is the Minister of Environment and Forestry Regulation Number 8 of 2021 concerning Forest Management and Preparation of Forest Management Plans, as well as Forest Utilization in Protected Forests and Production Forests. The current legal timber regulations in Indonesia have also been enacted by almost all local governments up to the district level, and most have also regulated the use of legal timber for government buildings.

In addition, for matters relating to the agricultural and plantation sectors, there are regulations relating to the licensing of business use rights which are regulated by Government Regulation (PP) No. 18/2021, where it is established that HGU can be granted for a maximum duration of 35 years and extended for an additional 25 years (Article 22, paragraph 1). In order to safeguard legal certainty and ensure the proper execution of plantation business activities, every plot of land used for such purposes, especially those requiring HGU, must be registered in accordance with the procedures outlined in Government Regulation No. 24/1997 which has been amended by Government Regulation Number 18/2021 on Management Rights, Land Rights, Housing Units, and Land Registration. This regulation serves to formalize and validate the legal use of the land by plantation companies.

Beyond the registration requirement, additional regulations, such as those found in Government Regulation No. 20 year 2021 , regulates the obligations of permit/ concession/business licence holders as well as rights holders, management rights holders, and basic land tenure holders; object of controlling abandoned areas and abandoned land; inventory of indicated abandoned areas and land; controlling abandoned areas and abandoned land. Furthermore, plantation business activities are specifically governed by the provisions set forth in Law No. 39/2014 amended by Law No. 6 of 2023 on Stipulating Government Regulation in Lieu of Law No. 2 of 2022 on Job Creation into Law, which grants HGU to plantation business actors (Article 11(1)) while also imposing certain restrictions on the total land area that can be used (Article 14(1)). ## 3.8. Taxation, Anticorruption, Trade and Customs Regulations

## a. Taxes and fees

Taxes in the plantation business in Indonesia are regulated by various laws and regulations that cover diΊerent types of taxes that must be paid by business actors in the plantation sector, such as income tax (PPh), value-added tax (PPN), and land and building tax (PBB). According to Law 12/1985 on Land and Building Tax, as amended by Law No. 12 of 1994, taxpayers are defined as any individual or legal entity with a legitimate right to land, who benefits from the land, or who owns, controls, or benefits from a building (Article 4). This provision implies a direct tax obligation for plantation business ownerss, requiring them to contribute to land tax as part of their operational costs.

In addition to land tax, income and corporate taxes in Indonesia are governed by evolving tax laws, with the latest amendments introduced through the Job Creation Law (Law 6/2023). The foundational legal framework for taxation in Indonesia includes Law 6/1983 on General Provisions and Tax Procedures, Law 7/1983 on Income Tax, and Law 7/2021 on the Harmonization of Tax Regulations. These laws provide structured guidelines for assessing and collecting taxes, ensuring compliance and fairness in tax obligations across all economic sectors.

Income Tax Article 21 (commonly referred to as PPh 21) is a withholding tax on individual income, including salaries, wages, honorariums, allowances, and other forms of remuneration derived from employment, services, or other professional activities within the country. This tax directly impacts individual income earners, ensuring that their income contributions align with national revenue needs.

Unlike income tax, Value-Added Tax (VAT), or PPN, is an indirect tax applied to transactions involving taxable goods and services, regardless of the nature of the taxpayer, whether individual or corporate. Under this system, the seller is responsible for collecting, remitting, and reporting the VAT to the authorities. However, the ultimate burden of VAT falls on the end consumer, as it is incorporated into the final purchase price. This structure not only aids in streamlining tax collection but also ensures that consumer consumption directly contributes to government revenue. These various taxation mechanisms reΌect Indonesia's comprehensive approach to public revenue generation, balancing direct and indirect tax obligations to support economic growth, fund public services, and promote equitable tax contributions across diΊerent sectors of society.

## 3.9. Data Privacy Protection Regulations

The protection of personal data is recognized as a fundamental human right under the 1945 Constitution of Indonesia. Article 28F states that every individual has the right to communicate and obtain information to develop their personal and social environment, as well as the right to seek, acquire, possess, store, process, and convey information through any available means. This protection is further expanded upon in Law 27/2022, addresses personal and corporation data.

According to Article 4 of this law, personal data is categorized into:

- (1) Specific Personal Data, which includes health information, biometric data, genetic data, criminal records, child data, personal financial data, and other data as stipulated by applicable laws.
- (2) General Personal Data, encompassing full name, gender, religion, marital status, and/or personal data that, when combined, can identify an individual.

Article 5 specifies that personal data subjects have the right to obtain information regarding the identity, legal basis, purpose of the request, and accountability of the party requesting their data. Within this context, plantation business owners, as personal data subjects, have the right to allow the use of their general data for activities such as export operations. Should a plantation business owners cease international trade activities, they may request the termination, deletion, or destruction of their personal data voluntarily (Article 8). This indicates that general personal data can be disclosed when necessary for business operations, especially if it pertains to general information.

Furthermore, Article 9 reinforces that personal data subjects maintain the right to withdraw their consent regarding the processing of their data. Article 74 of the law outlines that relevant parties must complete the establishment of a data management system by October 17, 2024. Since this deadline is imminent, a Government Regulation in Lieu of Law (Perpu) may be required to extend the timeline for finalizing the data management system, particularly to support plantation businesses exporting products such as cocoa, rubber, and coΊee to the European Union.

Meanwhile, information related to geospatial data is covered under **Law 4/2011**, where plantation maps are classified under Thematic Geospatial Information (IGT). Article 43, paragraph (1) states that Thematic Geospatial Information produced by government agencies and/or local governments is generally accessible to the public. However, certain IGT created by government entities may be restricted from public access (Article 43, paragraph (1)). In the explanatory section, "restricted access" refers to specific IGT that is excluded based on regulations governing public information transparency. It is well understood that restricted data often pertains to matters of national defense and security; therefore, IGT on plantations does not fall under classified state information and need not be confidential. This aligns with a Supreme Court ruling, which afÏrmed that administrative documents related to Right to Cultivate (Hak Guna Usaha/HGU) are not exempt from public disclosure, as stated in Article 17 of Law 14/2008 on Public Information Transparency.

This legislative framework underlines that while certain geospatial information may be withheld for security reasons, IGT related to plantation areas, including HGU documentation, does not fall within this category of restricted information. Consequently, public access to plantation geospatial data and associated HGU documents aligns with principles of transparency and accountability, reinforcing that such information should be available to the public unless explicitly exempted by law. This legal precedent further underscores Indonesia's commitment to maintaining openness in geospatial data where it does not compromise national security interests.

## 4.2. Recommendations for Smallholder

According to the Guidelines for Issuing Plantation Business Registration Certificates for Cultivation (STD-B), plantation landowners holding a Certificate of Ownership (SHM) may receive immediate recommendations for STD-B issuance by the verification team if their land status is deemed "clean and clear." However, if the land status is under a *girik* certificate, landowners must submit an application accompanied by various supporting documents. This application process includes location verification and payment procedures, ultimately leading to the issuance of an SHM.

In contrast, for Indigenous people lands, a distinct process is followed. The boundaries of the Customary Land parcels of the Indigenous people must first undergo measurement and verification. This is followed by mapping the designated Customary Land parcels onto the ofÏcial land registration map. Each parcel is then assigned a Land Parcel Identification Number, specific to the administrative unit of the regency or city where it is located. Finally, these Customary Land parcels are formally recorded in the land register, ensuring the recognition and legal documentation of Indigenous people lands within the national land administration system. This structured approach reΌects a commitment to upholding land rights while maintaining clarity and legal certainty, particularly for diΊerent types of ownership and community lands under Indonesia's land management framework.

## 4.3. Conclusion

While the legislative framework in Indonesia is comprehensive and very complex, with diΊerent laws and regulations existng and diΊerent ministries responsible for diΊerent thematic fields, two policy fields stand out in terms potential risk for non-compliance for commodities produced in the Indonesian context. When doing their due diligence exercise obligations, operators should therefore be aware on potential risks of non-compliance with regards to land rights and the protention of rights of indigenous peoples, specifically. As information is passed along the supply chain, business actors should also be aware of the potential risks associated. The main aspects are summarized as follows:

## (1) Land Rights

Plantation business actors are granted land rights for plantation operations in accordance with applicable laws and regulations. These rights include ownership rights, rights to cultivate (business use rights), rights to construct buildings (building use rights), and/or rights of utilization (use rights). Key considerations regarding land use rights include the following:

- a. Obtaining approval for land use in customary territories for plantation purposes requires a comprehensive consultation process with Indigenous

communities and relevant local authorities to ensure adherence to legal frameworks and respect for traditional land rights.

- b. Business actors must comply with the limitations on the total area designated for plantation use, ensuring that any expansion of plantation operations remains within the legal boundaries established by Government Regulations to prevent the overexploitation of land resources.
- c. The transfer of land rights over areas designated for plantation cultivation is strictly prohibited to safeguard the ownership and control of land by indigenous peoples or other legitimate owners, ensuring that the land remains dedicated to its intended agricultural use.
- d. At least 30 percent of the land rights area deemed technically suitable for plantation crops must be cultivated within a maximum of three years to promote efÏcient land utilization and prevent the underutilization of valuable agricultural resources.
- e. To ensure maximum productivity, the entire land rights area deemed suitable for plantation crops must be cultivated within a maximum of six years, ensuring full compliance with agricultural development plans and legal obligations.

Meanwhile, The regulation of land use, land utilization, and investment arrangements in the sectors of mining, plantations, and forest utilization is governed by Presidential Regulation No. 70/2023 concerning Land Allocation for Investment Arrangements. The enactment of Presidential Regulation No. 70/2023 is based on two key considerations, namely: (1) to enhance the eΊectiveness and efÏciency of resource utilization, promote equitable investment, and improve community welfare, it is essential to ensure fair and balanced land use management; and (2) the regulation of business licensing in the sectors of mining, plantations, and forest utilization for village-owned enterprises, regionally-owned enterprises, legal entities owned by community organizations, cooperatives, and small and medium enterprises is necessary for structured and sustainable economic development.

In its implementation, the President established the Task Force for Land Use and Investment Arrangement. This task force was formed to ensure the equitable management of land use, to streamline business licensing in the mining, plantation, and forest utilization sectors, and to enhance the eΊectiveness and efÏciency of natural resource optimization.

## (2) Rights of Indigenous people

The rights of indigenous peoples have been granted comprehensive and robust legal recognition through various laws and regulations, reΌecting the deep-rooted commitment of the Indonesian legal system to protect these communities. This strong legal recognition is primarily derived from the special consideration aΊorded to indigenous peoples in the Indonesian Constitution, particularly in the 1945 Constitution, which serves as the foundational legal document of the nation. In this context, Article 18B, paragraph (2), plays a pivotal role in afÏrming the State's commitment to indigenous rights, as it explicitly acknowledges the existence of customary law communities and their traditional rights. The article states: "The State recognizes and respects the units of customary law communities and their traditional rights," underscoring the legal and moral obligation of the State to ensure that these communities can continue to exercise their cultural practices, manage their lands, and maintain their identities within the framework of national law. This constitutional provision serves as the bedrock for subsequent legislation aimed at safeguarding the unique rights and autonomy of indigenous peoples in Indonesia.

Specifically, the support for indigenous peoples is regulated by Law No. 39 of 2014, and Law Number 22 year 2019. The recognition of the rights of indigenous peoples in Law No. 39 of 2014 on Plantations is elaborated in several articles that provide protection and respect for the rights of indigenous peoples, especially concerning land, natural resources, and customary practices. Here are some key points related to the recognition of indigenous peoples' rights in this law:

- **a. Recognition of Land and Natural Resource Rights**: Law No. 39 of 2014 recognizes the existence and rights of indigenous peoples over land and natural resources within their customary territories. This right includes the right to use, manage, and protect the natural resources on customary lands (ulayat lands) controlled by indigenous communities).
- **b. Recognition of Local Wisdom**: This law acknowledges the local wisdom of indigenous peoples, particularly in the sustainable management of natural resources. The law requires both the government and business

actors to consider local wisdom in the development of plantations and must not disregard or harm the rights of indigenous communities.

- **c. Free, Prior, and Informed Consent (FPIC):** In the implementation of plantation activities in customary areas, this law requires the prior consent of the relevant indigenous peoples before initiating activities that may aΊect their customary land. This aligns with the principle of FPIC, which ensures that indigenous peoples are provided with sufÏcient information and given the opportunity to approve or reject business activities in their territory freely and without coercion.
- **d. Prohibition of Unauthorized Seizure of Customary Land:** The law prohibits the unauthorized seizure or use of land controlled by indigenous peoples without their consent. This is intended to protect ulayat lands from unlawful appropriation or exploitation by external parties, including plantation companies.
- **e. Obligations of Business Actors to Protect Indigenous Peoples' Rights**: Plantation companies operating near indigenous territories are required to respect the rights of indigenous peoples, including the right to benefit from the management of natural resources in their customary areas.
- **f. Role of Government**: Both central and regional governments play a crucial role in recognizing and protecting the rights of indigenous peoples. The government is also obligated to ensure that indigenous peoples are involved in the planning and execution of plantation activities related to their customary lands.
