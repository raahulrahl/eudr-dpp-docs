### <span id="page-5-0"></span>Foreword

GreenTex solutions were developed and tested within the INTERREG Danube Region project *Enhancing Danube Green Textiles and Garment Production and Consumption (DRP0200404 Green-Tex)* with the aim of strengthening transnational collaboration and fostering innovation in the Danube region's sustainable textile sector. In this way, the textile sector value chain in partner regions becomes more resilient and competitive as it adapts to more regenerative, circular economy-based green textile practices.

This document outlines a collaborative effort to transform the Danube region's textile sector, presenting both the foundational insights that shaped our approach and the practical pilots that demonstrate scalable solutions for sustainability.

#### Chapter 1. Introduction

In the introduction, we look behind the scenes to understand the context of the textile sector with its opportunities and challenges. These challenges inspired us to gather 12 partners and 4 associated strategic partners from 9 countries, all committed to making meaningful changes, primarily within the Danube region.

#### Chapter 2. Piloting Process

The piloting process followed a structured methodology that included planning, mapping, stakeholder engagement, implementation, monitoring, and evaluation. Each pilot was codesigned with industry partners, public authorities, designers, NGOs, educational institutions and community actors to ensure relevance and realworld applicability. This approach enabled rapid learning, iterative improvement and the identification of barriers and opportunities for scaling sustainable textile solutions.

#### Chapter 3. Pilot 1: Testing Solutions for Textile Waste Collection

This pilot explored new models for collecting postconsumer and postindustrial textile waste. Through targeted collection points, awareness campaigns and collaboration with waste management services, the pilot tested how households and businesses respond to improved textile disposal options.

The results highlight both the potential for increased recovery rates and the need for continued public collaboration, education and infrastructure development towards digitalisation.

#### Chapter 4. Pilot 2: Testing Wider Application of Natural Fibres in Innovative Textile Production

The project investigated how natural fibres, in particular regionally available wool and hemp, can be integrated into traditional artisan production or modern textile manufacturing. By working with academia, cooperatives, producers and material innovators, the pilot assessed material properties, environmentally friendly washing, dyeing with natural fibres, performance, cost and benefits of both materials. The findings confirmed that natural fibres can successfully replace synthetic materials in various applications if processing technologies are adequately chosen and all stakeholders within the value chains are committed to green and circular principles.

#### Chapter 5. Pilot 3: Testing Green Fashion Design Solutions

This pilot focused on embedding sustainability principles into fashion design practices. Designers experimented with zerowaste patterns, upcycling techniques, modular garments and ecofriendly materials. The pilot demonstra-ted that creative design approaches can significantly reduce environmental impact while maintaining aesthetic and commercial value. It also highlighted the importance of raising public awareness, alongside training and capacitybuilding to support designers in transitioning to greener methods.

#### Chapter 6. Pilot Integration and Cross Cutting Learning

Integration across pilots ensured that insights from one activity informed the others, creating a holistic understanding of the textile ecosystem. Crosscutting learning sessions brought together stakeholders from collection, production, and design to share experiences, identify synergies, and codevelop recommendations. This collaborative approach strengthened the overall impact of the project and fostered a shared vision for sustainable textile transformation.

#### Chapter 7. GreenTex Solutions Highlights

The pilots collectively produced three a sets of standout solutions that demonstrate practical pathways towards circularity. These include improved waste collection systems, innovative naturalfibre products, ecodesign methodologies and collaborative frameworks for actors across the value chain. Each solution demonstrates how sustainability can be embedded in everyday textile practices while generating economic and social benefits.

#### Chapter 8. GreenTex Solutions Adaptability and Transferability

A key objective of the project was to ensure that tested solutions could be adapted to different contexts. Adaptability is strengthened through clear guidelines, open knowledge sharing and flexible implementation models. The pilots revealed that many approaches—such as naturalfibre integration, eco-design principles and modern textile waste collection systems—are highly transferable when supported by relevant partnerships. Close collaboration with the TEX-DAN project enabled further transferability.

#### Chapter 9. Final Words

The Green-Tex activities led to the solutions that unify the whole value chain and include innovative solutions across waste prevention and collection, sorting, sustainable production, eco-design, and crosssector collaboration. This part of the document summarises the outcomes of several interconnected activities, each designed to test practical approaches that prevent and reduce waste, promote natural fibres, and encourage greener fashion systems.

### <span id="page-8-0"></span>Green-Tex Summary

*The Green-Tex project facilitated transnational collaboration and innovation in the Danube region's textile sector. By focusing on sustainability and competitiveness, it supported value chain actors in partner regions as they adapted to global shifts—moving from traditional towards regenerative, circular economy-based green textile practices.*

*Green-Tex created and tested new solutions throughout the entire value chain, starting from fashion design to production and usage as well as textile waste collection and processing and verified them in various contexts within the project territory. Digital solutions were an integral part of the Green-Tex project in different partner regions in which an effective textile waste collection system with publicly available online maps of collection points were implemented. Green-Tex solutions have been incorporated into the Green-Tex learning and innovation platform. The project applied a multisectoral approach by integrating partners and stakeholders from the textile production, waste collection and recycling, product design and other relevant sectors. Additionally, it engaged actors within a quadruple innovation helix framework, which is vital for the successful application of circularity in the textile sector.*

# <span id="page-9-0"></span>1. Introduction

*The textile industry is a highly complex, interconnected industrial ecosystem. It sits at the intersection of advanced polymer chemistry, high-precision material engineering, manufacturing excellence, customer intimacy, supply chain orchestration, culture-based creativity and digital design. It is not a single industry but a web of value chains that underpin the functionality of modern society. From the high-tenacity fibres reinforcing wind turbine blades to the aortic filters in heart surgery, and from the geotextiles stabilising our highways to the ballistic protection of our armed forces, textiles are the engineered substrate of the European economy, as well as a critical component of our crisis resilience and strategic autonomy.*

> *Lutz Walter, Secretary General, European Technology Platform for the Future of Textiles and Clothing [https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/towards-european-textile-strategy-part-1](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/towards-european-textile-strategy-part-1-) defining-market-lutz-walter-p4tke/*

According to the latest *EU Strategy for Sustainable and Circular Textiles (2022- 2030)*, the circular economy is expected to deliver major environmental, economic and social advantages by reducing waste, lowering pollution, improving resource efficiency, and driving innovation across the fashion and textile value chain. These benefits are especially relevant today as the global textile industry faces pressure to cut emissions, reduce waste and transition towards sustainable production.

The Green-Tex project addressed circular economy and sustainable production and consumption challenges throughout the entire textile sector value chain, starting with product design and sourcing, followed by the production process, product use and end-of-life (reuse, waste collection and recycling). Key issues include reliance on unsustainable production practices, insufficient sustainable design education and limited waste collection and processing systems. The project advocates a mindset shift, the restructuring of business models towards circularity and fostering greater collaboration across the value chain to achieve social, environmental and economic benefits.

# <span id="page-11-0"></span>2. Piloting Process

The Green-Tex pilot actions focused on textile waste collection and recycling, increasing the use of natural fibres, and developing eco-friendly fashion design. These pilots were tailored to the specific conditions of each region, supporting their transition towards circular business models.

The overall piloting process consisted of several steps:

- (i) mapping,
- (ii) pilot testing preparation and implementation,
- (iii) stakeholders' capacity building and awareness-raising,
- (iv) pilot evaluation.

The Green-Tex mapping methodology followed a multi-layered approach aimed at collecting detailed and actionable data on the state and potential of the textile sector in the partner regions. The process began with meeting local stakeholders (field visits), technicians, managers of local, national and international projects, as well as secondary sources such as studies, statistical data and other records held by educational institutions, government offices and local communities in the area, etc.

Following the mapping process, subsequent activities focused on preparing and implementing pilot actions related to textile waste collection and processing, as well as exploring the use of wool and hemp in more sustainable production. They also included initiatives promoting green fashion to encourage more responsible consumption patterns. In parallel, efforts were made to strengthen stakeholders' capacities and raise awareness across the value chain, supporting a more informed and collaborative transition toward circular textile practices.

The evaluation of pilot testing aimed to assess the effectiveness, integration and replicability of innovative approaches to textile waste reduction, wider application of natural fibres, green fashion promotion and valorisation within a regional circular economy framework. It focused on how technological solutions (such as smart textile waste containers), traditional material use (wool valorisation) and creative industries (green fashion design) contribute synergistically to environmental, social and economic sustainability. The evaluation of pilot testing was designed not only to measure deliverables, but

also to generate learnings and practical recommendations for scaling up and integrating innovative textiles and circular economy practices in future regional initiatives. The evaluation explored:

- what worked well,
- what did not work as expected, and
- what can be improved or adapted for future full-scale implementation.

It captured insights from all actors — project partners, associated strategic partners, local producers, designers, educational and business support institutions and citizens — to ensure that future interventions are more efficient, inclusive and sustainable.

The Green-Tex project jointly developed and tested 3 pilots:

Pilot 1: Testing Textile Waste Collection – was tested in 3 pilot areas: Sarajevo (Bosnia and Herzegovina), Bucharest (Romania) and Prešov (Slovakia);

Pilot 2: Promotion of Natural Fibres in Innovative Textile Production - was tested in 3 pilot areas in Croatia, Serbia and Bulgaria;

Pilot 3: Promotion of Green Fashion Design – was tested in 2 pilot areas: Podgorica (Montenegro) and Postojna (Slovenia).

### **1.1. Mapping of Textile Waste Collection and Sorting in Partner Regions**

In Bulgaria, Slovakia, Hungary, Romania, Croatia and Slovenia there is working system for separate collection of discarded textiles from households. According to the EU *Waste Framework Directive 2025/1892*, as of 1 January 2025, EU Member States are required to ensure that separate collection systems for textiles (including clothing, footwear and similar items) are in place. Retailers are collaborating with collectors and sorters to take-back systems and overstock. Post-industrial waste from production is also being collected. Manufacturers are partnering with collection and sorting actors for better environmental performance. Education and social organisations are increasingly promoting the separate collection of textile waste and textile recovery through awareness campaigns, workshops and community programmes.

In Bulgaria the most prevailing collection stream is from household using specialised containers for street collection. The companies that collect textiles separately in Northeast Bulgaria using containers are: TexCycle, M-texx Textile Recycling and Texlife in partnership with Bulgarian Red Cross.

Textile waste in Slovakia has averaged around 15,800 tonnes per year for several years. There are six organisations engaged in the collection of discarded textiles: HUMANA People to People Slovakia o.z., EKOCHARITA Slovensko Slovensku o.z., TextilEco a.s., organizačná zložka, Zdeno Mišutka, Ľubomír Ludvik WINDOORS, MMPR, s.r.o.

In Hungary, residents can drop off unwanted textiles at numerous collection points or use textile containers managed by MOHU, the country's waste management organisation. Textiles in good condition are reused or donated through organisations such as Humusz Szövetség and the Hungarian Red Cross. Hungarian textile collection and sorting company Textrade, has made significant investments in automation, incorporating advanced robotic technology and machines to sort textile waste alongside manual sorting. The company sells its products both domestically and internationally.

In the Bucharest-Ilfov region, several methods for collecting textile waste have been proven effective. There are few companies that collect textile waste along with other waste. They sort textiles for recycling as part of their waste processing operations. Retail collection points also play an important role. The following recycling companies are currently opera-ting in the region: RER Ecologic Group, Romprest, Supercom SA and Ur-ban SA.

In the Primorsko-notranjska region in Slovenia, there are systems in place for the separate collection of discarded textiles from households. There are two textile waste collection stakeholders in this region: the concessionaire/waste collector Publikus and external contractor HUMANA. Both companies are involved in collecting, sorting and processing textile waste for recycling and reuse.

In Croatia, collection is organised primarily through recycling yards or several collecting points organized by non-profit cooperative Humana Nova, located in Čakovec and Zagreb. Their annual capacity is 400 tonnes of textiles. Additional sorting organisations include Reto Center Zagreb, Croatian Red Cross and Caritas. They all sort waste into reusable and non-usable categories, selling or donating usable items. Regeneracija Zabok, part of Croatian RGNC GROUP, process non-usable textiles into felt and exports more than 90% of 100% recycled products to the global market. Annually, they process approx. 6,000 tonnes of discarded textiles.

In BiH, systems for the separate collection of discarded textiles from households are still in development and are not yet widely implemented. Larger urban areas, such as Sarajevo have more advanced waste management services, incorporating some initiatives for separate textile recycling. However, smaller cities and rural areas experience considerably lower coverage. Unfortunately, organised waste disposal practices have yet to become standard in BiH, resulting in limited interest in recycling discarded textiles. On the other hand, companies are mandated to manage and dispose of waste in accordance with environmental and public health regulations that align with EU standards.

The final phase of the product life cycle represents one of the biggest challenges for the textile industry in the central region of Montenegro, especially due to the lack of systemic programmes for the collection and recycling infrastructure. The collection of textile waste is not organised at the national level, but there are individual initiatives that focus on the collection of old textiles for upcycling and creative projects.

Future plans include the construction of eight regional recycling centres, two of which are planned for the Zlatibor District.

#### **1.2. Pilot Testing Solutions for Textile Waste Collection and Processing**

The pilot evaluated the performance and feasibility of smart textile waste collection systems using sensor-equipped containers, with a focus on technical reliability, operational efficiency and citizens' behaviour.

The pilot was implemented in 3 areas: Sarajevo Canton, Ilfov County within Bucharest area, and in the City of Prešov, Slovakia.

#### Key Learnings:

#### 1. Technical and Logistical Challenges Related to the Installation, Operation and Maintenance of Smart Containers

#### Bosnia and Herzegovina, Sarajevo Canton

The pilot in Bosnia and Herzegovina focused on testing smart containers equipped with fill-level sensors integrated via Narrowband Internet-of-Things (NB-IoT) connectivity. The implementation was carried out by the Sarajevo Economic Region Development Agency (SERDA) in cooperation with the Ministry of Communal Economy of Sarajevo Canton and the public utility company KJKP RAD.

![](_page_16_Picture_4.jpeg)

Integrating the sensors into the NB-IoT network of the local telecom operator requi-red several technical adjustments:

- Network configuration alignment
- Data reading protocol synchronisation
- Backend system configuration
- Testing of data transmission stability.

The piloting process represented a real-life learning situation for all stakeholders:

- Utility company first experience with smart containers
- Telecom provider first large-scale NB-IoT application for waste management
- Project partner first integration of smart textile waste collection system.

#### Operational Monitoring

The following parameters were systematically monitored:

- Fill-level frequency
- Volume of textiles deposited
- Container operational availability
- Sensor data accuracy
- Seasonal variations.

In Bosnia and Herzegovina, smart solutions are still widely perceived as a "*luxury*" rather than as tools for operational optimisation and cost efficiency. Public awareness also remains limited, often resulting in the disposal of unsuitable textile waste (e.g. wet or heavily contaminated items), which makes the collected materials unusable. Textiles are still largely perceived as "*harmless*" waste that can be discarded together with mixed municipal waste, while the belief that "*everything ultimately ends up in the same landfill*" further discourages citizen participation.

Addressing these challenges requires sustained, longterm awarenessraising and communication efforts, as short-term, project-based campaigns, although effective, remain insufficient. Vandalism has also emerged as a significant issue; consequently, containers had to be placed in less frequented but supervised locations to reduce theft and damage. Finally, GSM/IoT connectivity and sensor batteries require stable and continuous maintenance to ensure the reliable functioning of smart containers.

#### Romania, Ilfov County

Smart textile waste collection systems using sensor-equipped containers were installed by the Intercommunity Development Association Bucharest Metropolitan Area (ADIZMB) in the target localities of Ilfov county (Brăneşti, Pantelimon, Popești-Leordeni, Berceni and Vidra).

#### 3. Learnings about Citizen Behaviour, Particularly Regarding Textile Waste Sorting

#### Bosnia and Herzegovina, Sarajevo Canton and Romania, Ilfov County

The analysis of changes in citizen behaviour showed that most residents were initially unaware of the existence of textile waste containers. Even among those who were aware, usage remained occasional, with textile waste still predominantly disposed of together with mixed municipal waste. Nevertheless, increased awareness of dedicated textile containers has led to a partial shift in behaviour, indicating that citizens are willing to adapt their disposal practices when appropriate infrastructure is available.

The findings also reveal a clear demand for a greater number of textile waste containers placed in accessible and visible locations. In addition, citizens emphasise the importance of transparency regarding the further handling of collected textile waste, as well as the need for more frequent and informative awarenessraising campaigns. This suggests that sustained communication efforts and improved accessibility are essential prerequisites for achieving longterm behavioural change and correct textile waste sorting.

#### 4. Measurement of Key Indicators, Including Container Usage Rates, System Uptime, Data Accuracy and the Percentage Increase in Properly Sorted Textile Waste

The results of the measurements of this pilot activity show an increase in container usage rates over time and a higher share of properly sorted textile material, indicating the effectiveness of the pilot awareness-raising campaigns and the reliability of the system.

#### Bosnia and Herzegovina, Sarajevo Canton

The environmental impact of Pilot 1 was assessed by examining the benefits of diverting textile waste from disposal and promoting reuse and recycling.

Based on annual estimates (5,460 kg of collected textile waste), the pilot demonstrates a measurable reduction in potential environmental burdens, including lower greenhouse gas emissions resulting from avoided disposal and material substitution. Preliminary results indicate that even at a pilot scale, the collection system contributes to tangible environmental benefits, such as reduced pressure on landfills, decreased carbon emissions and improved resource efficiency. These findings reinforce the rationale for scaling the model to additional waste categories and municipalities.

#### Romania, Ilfov County

Using the Denkstatt methodology, applied through the Texcycle – ECO-CA-LCULATOR application to calculate the benefits generated by the separate collection of textile waste, citizens from the target areas contributed, among others, to the following outcomes:

- Reduction of carbon dioxide emissions resulting from the production of new clothing and footwear, as well as from the decomposition of textile waste in landfills, by 1,051 tonnes, equivalent to 1,051,464 km of air travel (one passenger).
- Saving of 98,363 m² of arable land, freed from raw material production for new clothing and footwear.
- Saving of 108,193 m³ of water, otherwise required to produce new clothing and footwear – for irrigation of crops used as raw materials and for manufacturing and fibre dyeing processes. This is equivalent to 1,081,927 ten-minute showers.

These results clearly confirm that separate textile collection generates measurable environmental benefits and directly contributes to climate and resource-efficiency objectives.

## Pilot 2: Testing Wider Application of Natural Fibres in Innovative Textile Production

This pilot assessed the engagement, feasibility and added value of reusing local wool and hemp through research and development, educational, artisanal and market-oriented activities. The pilot presentation highlights the economic sustainability of local wool and hemp valorisation, learning outcomes for participants and the effectiveness of local partnerships involved in the pilot.

The pilot was implemented in 3 areas: the Municipality of Majur, Croatia, Zlatibor region in Serbia and Bulgaria.

#### **4.1. Mapping of Sustainable Materials Sourcing in Partner Regions**

The textile and garment industries continue to develop with a renewed focus on the materials used. Increasing attention is being given to the use of natural materials, preferably of local origin, such as wool and hemp, as well as the incorporation of recycled fibres. However, in regions of the Green-Tex project, which were once known for its strong textile sectors, the production of raw materials at the local level has been completely discontinued.

In Bulgaria, the predominant textile material used is cotton, which is now sourced exclusively from Turkey. The country lacks a textile industry that utilises wool and the wool produced by local sheep farms is mostly unsuitable for garment manufacturing. This unsuitability stems from inadequate wool processing infrastructure; currently, there is only one registered wool washing facility in the country. As a result, there is no reliable data

on wool collection and raw wool prices have plummeted to nearly zero. The dominant sheep breeds in the region are primarily raised for milk, meat and only marginally for wool. In Bulgaria, the cultivation of hemp remains on hold pending final governmental decisions.

Although Montenegro has a rich tradition of animal husbandry and wool production, the country faces the problem of excessive import of wool from abroad. This situation results in the discarding or burning of domestic wool, instead of using this valuable resource in the textile industry. ## Pilot Integration and Cross-Cutting Learning

The overall coherence of Green-Tex pilot actions and the extent to which the individual pilots mutually reinforce one another were also addressed. The project aimed to identify synergies across thematic areas, assess the effectiveness of cross-sector collaboration and draw lessons to support future scaling and institutional uptake.

The imperative for all pilots was waste reduction. The success of these activities depended on the efficient engagement of all stakeholders across the eco-value chain, as well as alignment with regional sustainability strategies.

The successful implementation of textile waste depended strongly on the organisation of collection, sorting and processing. Different forms of waste were used: household textile waste (post-consumption) collected through innovative smart containers (Pilot 1), discarded woollen fleece (Pilot 2) and discarded clothes (Pilot 3). Although processing woollen fleece differs from the recycling processes of household discarded textiles, the final products all fell into the same category of new eco products.

Therefore, our Green-Tex solutions include handmade woollen products (e.g. different souvenirs and fashion accessories), rugs made from a mixture of wool and hemp, new fashion collections made from older ones, so as the novel technical textiles made from recyclates which reduce the need for virgin resources and serve as the secondary raw materials.

#### Key Assessment Areas:

#### 1. Degree of Complementarity and Alignment Among the Three Pilot Actions

The three pilot actions-Textile Waste Collection, Promotion of Natural Fibres, and Green Fashion Design-form a coherent and mutually reinforcing set of interventions that address different stages of the textile value chain. Although each pilot focuses on a distinct thematic area, together they create a comprehensive framework for advancing circularity, sustainability and innovation in the textile sector across the participating regions.

Together, the pilots cover collection → production → design, creating a holistic approach that strengthens the circular textile ecosystem.

The pilots were implemented across eight countries, covering EU Member States and IPA partners. This geographic diversity:

- 1. encourages knowledge exchange between regions with different levels of textile sector development.
- 2. supports policy harmonisation and shared learning on circular economy practices.
- 3. strengthens transnational cooperation, a key objective of the broader programme.

The three pilot actions demonstrate a high degree of complementarity and alignment. They are not isolated interventions but interconnected components of a broader strategy to transform the textile sector. By addressing different stages of the value chain and engaging diverse stakeholders, the pilots collectively created stronger impact than any single action could achieve alone.

#### 2. Effectiveness of Cross-Sector and Cross-Institutional Partnerships Developed Through Pilots

The three pilots collectively demonstrated that crosssector and crossinstitutional cooperation is not only beneficial but essential for advancing circularity and sustainability in the textile sector. Their implementation across multiple countries and diverse institutional settings created a rich environment for collaboration, knowledge exchange, and joint problemsolving.

Pilots brought together a mix of stakeholders — waste management companies, textile producers, designers, NGOs, educational institutions, public authorities and local communities. This diversity:

- enabled multidimensional perspectives on textile waste, production, and design.
- ensured that solutions were practical, realistic, and aligned with local needs.
- fostered shared ownership, increasing the likelihood of longterm adoption.

For example, waste collection and processing companies played a key role in waste collection and processing (Pilot 1), while farmers (sheep-breeders), artisans, producers and research institutions were central in natural fibre

innovation (Pilot 2), whereas design schools and designers drove green fashion initiatives (Pilot 3).

The crosssector and crossinstitutional partnerships developed through the pilots were highly effective. They:

- Strengthened the quality and relevance of pilot activities.
- Enabled innovation and practical problemsolving.
- Built longterm networks that will continue to support sustainability transitions.
- Ensured that the pilots had impact not only within their specific locations but across the wider region.

#### 3. Added Value Generated Through Joint Activities and Coordinated Implementation

The coordinated implementation of the three pilots- textile waste collection, natural fibre innovation, and green fashion design- created significant added value that would not have been achievable through isolated, standalone actions. By aligning activities, sharing knowledge and synchronising methodologies across countries and sectors, the project generated a multiplier effect that strengthened outcomes at both local and transnational levels.

Coordinated implementation across eight countries enabled:

- cross-country comparison of approaches, challenges, and results.
- transfer of good practices, such as waste collection models or fibre processing techniques.
- adaptation of solutions to different regulatory, cultural, and market contexts.

#### 4. Stakeholder Satisfaction with Collaboration Processes and Overall Pilot Outcomes

Across all three pilots, stakeholder satisfaction was generally high, reflecting both the quality of collaboration processes and the tangible results achieved. The pilots brought together diverse actors- private companies, designers, NGOs, universities, public authorities, and citizens, who reported positive experiences. Citizens responded positively to the availability of dedicated containers, while the private sector expressed interest in taking over the collected material for further processing.

Stakeholders expressed satisfaction with the concrete output of the pilots, such as:

- improved textile waste collection systems (Pilot 1)
- new or enhanced natural fibre products and processes (Pilot 2)
- eco-design concepts, prototypes and awareness campaigns (Pilot 3).

These outcomes demonstrated that the pilots were not merely theoretical exercises but delivered practical, visible benefits. Stakeholders consistently highlighted the benefits of working within a transnational framework:

- exposure to different regulatory and market contexts
- access to a broader pool of expertise
- opportunities to benchmark and compare approaches
- a sense of belonging to a larger regional movement.

This cross-border dimension significantly increased satisfaction by expanded learning and networking opportunities.

#### 5. Identification of Institutional, Organisational or Policy Adjustments Required to Enable Scaling and Long-Term Uptake

The successful implementation of the three pilots demonstrated strong potential for replication and longterm integration into regional textile systems. However, scaling these initiatives requires targeted adjustments at institutional, organisational and policy levels. These adjustments would help embed circular textile practices into mainstream operations, ensure financial and operational sustainability and create an enabling environment for innovation.

Several policy changes would significantly enhance scaling potential:

- extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) schemes for textiles
- formal inclusion of textile waste in local waste management plans
- standardisation of measurement and reporting methodologies
- incentives for natural fibre production (e.g., subsidies, tax relief)
- eco-design requirements for textile products.

These policies are contributing to stable environment for circular textile activities. It is also necessary to consider financial mechanisms that would ensure the economic sustainability of the system after the completion of the project.

#### Pilot 1: Green-Tex Solutions for Textile Waste Collection

The challenges faced by various regions in the EU in implementing effective textile recycling and circular economy practices are multifaceted, however there are several solutions that can address these issues across different sectors.

To address the infrastructural and logistical challenges related to textile recycling, both advanced and less-developed EU regions need to invest in improved collection, sorting and processing facilities. An important consideration is the selection or design of appropriate containers, considering factors such as resistance to vandalism, capacity and opening mechanisms. Equally important is their proper placement within designated areas. Textile waste collection via containers can be supplemented with other collection methods or used as an alternative where container collection is not feasible.

#### Solution 1: Unique smart textile waste container

Collection containers are an ideal solution for textile collection because they are accessible 24/7 and are typically located at existing container sites, ma-king it convenient for citizens to deposit textiles at any time, regardless of the day or hour. This ease of access encourages greater public participation by removing barriers related to time and location.

#### Solution 2: Digital Sensors and Waste Management Platform for Textile Waste Containers

The use of digital sensors in containers significantly enhances the efficiency of the collection process. These sensors monitor the capacity of each container and enable the system to identify which containers require prompt emptying. By preventing overflow and reducing unnecessary collection trips, this approach optimises resource usage and minimises transportation costs. Furthermore, sensor integration supports environmental sustainability by reducing carbon emissions typically associated with traditional waste collection methods. Containers equipped with these sensors are also visible via dedicated applications, allowing drivers or administrators to plan collection routes efficiently and further streamline operations. This combination of accessibility, technology and resource optimisation makes this method both effective and sustainable.

*Green-Tex solution 1.2: Digital sensor and waste management platform for smart textile waste container*

#### Solution 3: Fibres from Recycled Waste

Recycling textile waste into new fibres minimises the need for virgin materials, saving resources and reducing the carbon footprint associated with textile production. This approach addresses two critical issues: reducing textile waste and creating high-quality, reusable materials for new textile products. The Green-Tex project developed several prototypes demonstrating the feasibility of value-added products from waste, thus proving the effectiveness of mechanical recycling in transforming textiles into functional fibres and products. Textiles that would otherwise be discarded are repurposed, reducing the environmental impact of textile waste, such as landfills and incineration.

Mixed recycled fibres were further processed into yarns or non-woven materials, with potential applications in various technical fields. By prototyping these new fibre blends, manufacturers can experiment with different ratios and combinations to optimise the resulting material's properties, such as strength, texture and durability.

This process not only reduces waste but also creates high-quality materials for industries such as automotive, construction and non-woven textile production.

#### Solution 7: Product Design Prototyping

This solution within Pilot 3 thus integrated the efforts of both - textile waste collection (Pilot 1) and the enhancement of locally sourced wool (Pilot 2).

The main goal of this prototyping was to evaluate the material's suitability for diverse end-use applications. It also aimed at identifying both the opportunities and limitations related to handling, cutting, sewing and finishing processes.

The material was successfully integrated into product design prototypes across various application areas, including fashion, home textiles and pet-related products. This approach allowed for a focused assessment of the material's functional properties, design adaptability and manufacturing feasibility during the prototyping and development stages.

The nonwoven material which was applied features a compact and stable structure, a soft and pleasant surface feel and high thermal insulation properties, making it suitable for products requiring warmth and comfort. However, due to its non-layered construction, the material cannot be split into thinner layers, which affects its processability using conventional texti-le and garment production techniques. As a result, design adaptations and specific construction solutions were applied during the prototyping phase.

## Green-Tex Solutions Adaptability and Transferability

### **Adaptability**

One of the guiding principles of the Green-Tex project approach is adaptability and versatility of its solutions as the project addresses topics of greening textile and garment production and consumption at various levels of advancement. Adaptability also means being willing to learn from each other. Partner regions that had very limited experience in the textile waste collection (the metropolitan area of Bucharest and urban and suburban areas of Sarajevo) had the opportunity to adapt already verified system under the flexible guidance of experienced partners from Bulgaria and Slovakia.

The most important adaptation was the alignment of the developed Green-Tex solutions with EU's novel framework - *Eco-design for Sustainable Products Regulation* (ESPR). This regulation guides all stakeholders in textile sector to design and develop more durable, repairable, recyclable and transparent products. To implement it, the textile industry and brands must redesign products, restructure supplychain data as well as prepare for Digital Product Passports and strict circularity requirements.

Our pilots align excellently with ESPR requirements, starting with promotion of sustainable natural regional materials (wool and hemp) and enhancement of extremely low percentage of textile recyclates to be reused for textile product. This can be obtained only if initial collection of discarded textiles is more efficient and completely separated from mixed containers for house-hold waste.

The next stage of the value chain brings us to the topics of clothing reuse, textile waste up-cycling, clothing repair and refinement which was also tested within our pilots. It is smart consumption and reuse that are effective inhibitors of fast fashion. These trends have not yet taken hold in all partner regions. The project therefore implemented awareness-raising activities to influence consumer behaviour. By engaging successful fashion designers and design students, the project showcased that clothing reuse and repair are not only environmentally friendly but can be fashionable and trendy.

### **Transferability**

Green-Tex solutions, deliverables and outputs were created in a way that will allow transferability to other regions, businesses and stakeholders in ot-her territories with similar characteristics. In particular, the Green-Tex Pilot Actions Report and the Green-Tex Solutions were designed to provide instructions to other stakeholders (public and private) on how to create effective system of textile waste collection, apply locally abundant natural fibres in sustainable production and expand green fashion design solutions in various contexts (territorial, and sectoral).

The experimental activities of the project proved that local natural materials have high-quality, can be utilised in a variety of ways and transferred to very different applications. One of such examples is the collaboration between the Municipality of Majur and the cooperative ZAKON, which has successfully established sustainable model of hemp cultivation. Prior to its collaboration with Green-Tex and the signing of the Green-Tex Pact, the cooperative used hemp stalks solely for reinforcing building blocks. However, through their established partnership with the University of Zagreb, Faculty of Textile Technology, they are now jointly developing novel composite structures composed entirely of eco-friendly and biodegradable materials. Another exam-ple is the establishment of the demo centres VUNKO in Majur, Croatia, and the ReWooling Centre in the Serbian region of Zlatibor, which closed the loop under the umbrella of this project and established a circular model. The value chain includes fleece collection from local farms, storage and small-scale production of wool yarn using equipment provided by the project, sufficient for artisan production of local souvenirs. The integration of education, creativity and public engagement proved to be a scalable model that can be transferred to other cities or regions seeking to promote sustainable fashion and circular textile practices.

Furthermore, the partnerships of the two synergistic Danube Region projects—Green-Tex and TEX-DAN—along with their stakeholders, formalised their commitment to circularity in the textile sector by signing the Green-Tex Pact. This pact reinforces their joint dedication to the continuous promotion and application of circular practices within regional textile systems. The agreement remains open to new, relevant stakeholders who wish to join this initiative. Additionally, Green-Tex partners will participate in the *Danube Circular Textile Cluster* launched by the TEX-DAN project.

The project website functions as a virtual ecosystem that accelerates learning and innovation in green textiles. It also features educational video content produced by the First Hungarian Responsible Innovation Association (EMFIE), which helps raise awareness of the project's outputs. In addition, communication kits and training materials are publicly available and can be adapted to other contexts, thereby extending the project's reach and impact. Overall, this virtual Green-Tex ecosystem strengthens the transferability of project results and supports continued learning and innovation. ### Final Words

The GreenTex project has clearly demonstrated that innovative approaches 9. to textile circularity - by combining smart technologies, local material valorisation and creative design - can significantly contribute to sustainable regional development.

A central focus of the project was the innovative and environmental impact of effective textile waste collection systems, textile waste recycling potential and eco design.

The value of the pilot which was testing solutions for textile waste collection lies in the fact that municipal utility companies and project partners gained their first handson experience with smart solutions. The pilot identified the need for staff training and the introduction of new operational procedures within the utility companies. It also brought the issue of textile waste into public and institutional discourse, enabled evidencebased learning, created a foundation for future policy development and strategic planning. It can therefore be considered a starting point that opened opportunities for furt-her advancement.

Within the pilot testing of the wider application of natural fibres in innovative textile production, particular attention was given to the potential of local hemp and wool, which stand out for their exceptional sustainability benefits, including low water requirements, high carbon sequestration capacity and full biodegradability. By introducing sustainable methods for cleaning, spinning, dyeing and eco-washing wool, the project not only reduced environmental impact but also enhanced the quality of the final products, ma-king them more attractive to eco-conscious consumers and investors.

The pilot testing of green fashion design solutions significantly strengthened the public visibility of sustainable fashion practices and reinforced the position of small brands, supportive of creative and sustainable innovation. It also demonstrated a strong and transferable model through its integrated structure, which included regional designer collaboration, targeted textile waste collection campaigns, a public fashion showcase, national and regional media engagement and practical capacity-building workshops.

As innovation often emerges at the intersection of diverse fields, the project brought together a strategic mix of expertise—from textiles and technology to fashion design and waste management. This multidisciplinary collaboration enabled the development of a wide range of comprehensive Green-Tex solutions.
