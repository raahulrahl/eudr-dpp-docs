# <span id="page-9-0"></span>**1 Executive summary**

Since the last deliverable on analysis of value chains in September 2022 (update of D4.1), a new value chain was added to the project as associate partner (Fragrances represented by IFRA, The International Fragrance Association) and this deliverable describes different subvalue chains of the different value chains and associated SSbD challenges, as well as an update of some of the sub-value chains from D4.1 when it was considered necessary (Plastics Packaging, wind energy and electronics).

# <span id="page-9-1"></span>**1.1 Packaging**

The update on plastics packaging is summarized below and covers both Product Safety Priorities from the SSbD perspective:

#### Safety Priorities:

For raw materials in packaging, regulations tend to prioritize declarations over certifications. This approach can inadvertently open the door for potential false claims. When considering production and processing, the prevailing regulations are rooted in the most recent scientific knowledge. However, this can pose challenges: for example, when different materials are mixed, the resulting product could be hazardous. Additionally, the influx of recycled material in production heightens the intricacy of ensuring safety. In the product processing phase, there's potential for the misuse of hazardous substances, and clear communication between design and production teams is often lacking, especially when it comes to safety concerns associated with non-EU imports. During transport, any significant loss, especially during maritime shipments, introduces both safety and sustainability challenges. In terms of product use, the implications and potential dangers of micro-plastics, particularly in the food industry, remain an area of concern. Finally, when these products reach their end-of-life, there is a palpable risk of contamination from improperly sorted waste, and some materials, while approved for certain uses, might conflict with other established regulations.

In the search for moving away from raw material extraction, there's a noticeable trend towards the use of bio-based materials. Life Cycle Assessments (LCA) sometimes might not fully account for substances that are present in only minor quantities. As we transition into production and processing, there's a concerted effort to reduce the use of certain substances and to promote the adoption of mono-material packaging. Transport considerations include a push to reduce the weight and volume of packaging, coupled with rising concerns about CO<sup>2</sup> emissions stemming from sourcing outside of Europe. The effects of micro-plastics on both humans and the broader ecosystem are still under thorough investigation. When considering the end-of-life phase for these materials, there is a clear need for enhanced sorting techniques and more effective decontamination processes in recycling. However, it's also important to note that economic interests often dictate the extent and direction of recycling initiatives.

A first description and associated SSbD challenges was given to the glass packaging sub-value chain:

In 2022, the total production of glass exceeded 39.5 million tonnes. The biggest sector in this production was container glass, which is also known as packaging glass. The European Container Glass Federation, or FEVE, produces glass containers that serve a variety of sectors, including food, beverage, cosmetics, and pharmaceuticals.

Glass is both recyclable and reusable, operating within a closed loop. Most of the raw materials utilized in its production are naturally occurring. The industry has set ambitious environmental goals: it aims to offer fully climate-neutral packaging by the year 2050 and has a target of achieving a 61% reduction in CO<sup>2</sup> emissions by 2030, using 2005 as a benchmark. To meet these goals, substantial investments are essential, especially for transitioning production processes to rely on renewable energies.

#### Value Chain Steps:

The production of glass is a complex process involving a combination of both raw materials and recycled glass. Once produced, these glass containers are filled with various products either by brands themselves or by bottling plants. Subsequently, retailers market these products to consumers, simultaneously encouraging them to recycle after use. When collected, this glass undergoes a series of sorting and recycling processes. Post this, it either gets repurposed for production or is used for other applications.

#### Safe and Sustainable by Design (SSbD) Challenges:

Producing glass poses several challenges. Environmentally, the production process has an inherently high energy demand and results in the emission of various gases. One of the significant challenges faced by the industry is the transition from relying on fossil fuels to adopting renewable energy sources. Furthermore, transportation of glass products contributes to CO<sup>2</sup> emissions. From a circular economy perspective, not all glass gets collected separately, which can impact both the quality and quantity of the secondary raw material. Economically, transitioning to renewable energy sources requires high investments, particularly in building the necessary infrastructures for production.

# <span id="page-10-0"></span>**1.2 The Textiles value chain**

# <span id="page-10-1"></span>**1.2.1 Smart textiles sub-value chain**

Smart textiles, also known as e-textiles, are textile materials integrated with electronic components that offer unique functionalities, ranging from heat absorption and color change to controlled substance release. Although there are materials called functional or multifunctional textiles that achieve these functionalities without electronics, smart textiles have a distinct place in the market. This category extends beyond just wearables and flexible electronics, encompassing non-wearable applications like building structures, tents, and interiors.

For almost 25 years, smart textile products have targeted sectors like medical, sports, and smart interiors, marking an intersection of the textile and microelectronics sectors. The complexity of their value chain is evident as it draws from the electronics, textiles, and ICT (Information Communications Technology) sectors, emphasizing the importance and challenges of cross-sectoral collaborations.

There's a pressing need for innovative equipment and chemical supplies tailored for smart textiles. Knowledge gaps, especially in electronics and new processes, hinder production.

In the software realm, connectivity, platform building, and data management emerge as crucial facets of smart textiles. As these textiles connect and form networks, security, analytics, and data processing come to the forefront. The current retail landscape leans towards niche segments, with the entire sub-value chain being predominantly technologydriven.

Environmental considerations could lead to waste which is difficult to recycle, making ecodesign and life-cycle important for sustainable development. From a regulatory perspective, gaps and fragmentation in testing, assessment, and labeling exist. There's a growing demand for international regulatory control and standardization, which is essential for stakeholder interactions, fair competition, and intended usage scenarios.

In terms of Safety and Sustainability challenges, there's a noticeable mismatch between the textile and electronics sectors when it comes to assessing safety and sustainability. Some of the major safety concerns include potential skin irritations from the product, the potential toxicity from metals like silver and copper (coming from the electronic components), and a heightened risk of fire due to electronic components. On the sustainability front, these textiles present challenges such as generating higher waste, difficulties in recycling, and the absence of specific collection systems designed for smart textiles. Additionally, organizing cross-sectoral supply chains can be difficult due to the inherent complexities of both sectors. Standardizing processes becomes a significant challenge because of the wide range of standards that each sector follows. Furthermore, developers, particularly those operating on a smaller scale, often find it challenging to strictly adhere to comprehensive safety and sustainability guidelines, especially as the development of smart textiles often involves still a lot of trial-and-error, prototyping, and user testing.

# <span id="page-11-0"></span>**1.3 Construction value chain**

# <span id="page-11-1"></span>**1.3.1 Insulation materials sub-value chain**

Buildings account for 40% of the EU's primary energy demand and produce about 35% of greenhouse gas emissions (GHG). There's significant potential for energy-saving through building renovation and upgrading. Insulation materials, particularly those made of wool minerals and polymer foams like Polystyrene and Polyurethane (PU), are key to enhancing energy efficiency.

The focus here, in this deliverable, was chosen to be on the PU foams sub-value chain which is primarily utilized in building insulation and have specific SSbD challenges.

PU foams are a popular choice for insulation because of their lightweight and efficient insulating properties. The production chain for PU foams involves several stages, from the manufacture of raw materials (such as diisocyanates and polyols) to their application and eventual recycling or reuse. Large chemical companies typically handle the manufacturing of raw materials, while small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) often deal with the application at the construction site.

The primary ingredients of PU are derived from crude oil and require di-isocyanates, which are the main safety concern in their use, because they are classified as Substances of Very High Concern (SVHC). The industry is shifting towards safer chemical alternatives and processes. For instance, instead of using ozone-depleting HydroFluoro-Carbon (HFC) as a blowing agent, the industry has transitioned to HydroFluoro-Olefins (HFO), which are more environmentally friendly. Additionally, research projects like ['Circular Foam'](https://circular-foam.eu/) 1 aim to enhance the circularity of PU foams and develop communication systems for production process data.

However, the PU foams play a crucial role in reducing GHG emissions, given their insulating properties. They can reduce global CO<sup>2</sup> emissions significantly, aligning with the goals of the European Green Deal [20]. Another advantage is their durability, offering longer lifetimes than other insulation materials. On the sustainability side, a LCA conducted by PU Europe highlighted the significantly reduced environmental burden of insulation products compared to the benefits they offer during the building use phase.

# <span id="page-12-0"></span>**1.4 Automotive value chain**

# <span id="page-12-1"></span>**1.4.1 Car seats sub-value chain (PU moulded foams)**

Moulded PU (polyurethane) foams play a significant role in car manufacturing, particularly in car seats. In fact, PU is the second-most popular plastic/polymer in vehicles, with over 20% of plastic used in this capacity. Approximately 60% of this foam is utilized for car seats, predominantly through a moulding process.

As for other applications (as previously described in Construction insulation materials), it involves specific raw materials like di-isocyanates and polyols, mostly derived from crude oil. However, some are derived from renewable resources. Specifically, vehicle seating primarily uses di-isocyanate types Diphenylmethane diisocyanate (MDI) (60-70%), methylene diphenyl diisocyanate and Toluene diisocyanate (TDI) (30-40%) production involves injecting a specific amount of reactant blend into a heated mould. These components are reacting in the mould with the pre-addition of other substances like additives, catalysts, and colorants through a blender, which lead to the PU foam.

Automotive foams are ensured to meet comfort and safety standards. Some of these foams are naturally fire-resistant, while others are treated to attain this property. When used in vehicles, flexible PU foam is noted for its durability. It retains its original properties throughout the car's lifetime, barring significant damage. Notably, it can also withstand the rigorous tests in alignment with OEM (original equipment manufacturer) specifications.

However, when it comes to recycling, automotive moulded foams present challenges. While they can be recycled, current practices don't usually include automotive foams, unlike

<sup>1</sup> <https://circular-foam.eu/>

mattresses in some European Member States. This discrepancy arises from the complexity of foam usage in cars – it's scattered, combined with other materials, and often mixed with other substances/materials, making recycling tricky. Yet, promising research is ongoing to chemically recycle automotive foams, potentially offering a more efficient method than the current mechanical processes.

Regarding SSbD and its application in various value chains, several advancements and practices have been implemented. The International Material Data System (IMDS) has emerged as a vital digital tool in the automotive supply chain. It facilitates the reporting of materials used in vehicle production worldwide. With rising regulations around substances and recycling, the IMDS helps streamlining this process. As of May 2023, the IMDS now includes enhanced features that allow users to specify chemical recycling and portions derived from bio-based sources. Additionally, it can also indicate adherence to specific standards such as ISCC+ and Redcert, along with details on the content of bio-based and chemically recycled materials.

Specifically for the PU foam production, producers have used uses bio-based raw materials like soybean, castor, and rapeseed oil for several years. The option of CO2 based polyols also exists . Such sustainable approaches point towards the growing incorporation of renewable or recycled content with mass-balanced raw materials in the industry.

There are several relevant funding initiatives, which stand out and show the concerted efforts across different stakeholders in the automotive industry to prioritize sustainability and adapt SSbD principles.

# <span id="page-13-0"></span>**1.5 The Energy value chain**

# <span id="page-13-1"></span>**1.5.1 The sub-value chain: Solar**

Photovoltaic Systems (PV) encompass a range of technologies. These include crystalline silicon cells divided into single crystalline silicon (often called monocrystalline), multi crystalline silicon (often referred to as polycrystalline) and thin film technologies. Multi Junction solar cells combine various materials, such as crystalline silicon with thin films.

As of 2017, crystalline silicon technologies dominated the global market, holding 95% of the total production, of which multi crystalline technology constituted 62%. Thin film technologies accounted for 5% of the market in that year. While the European PV market has grown rapidly, still, the European industry's market share declined from 35% in 2009 to around 5% due to intense competition, especially from China.

Silicon-based PV panels are largely safe with minimal toxicity concerns, primarily from small amounts of lead, which remains sealed during the panel's operational lifespan. However, thin film PV cells and modules' manufacturing involves numerous hazardous materials and toxic gases. Despite these challenges, the industry emphasizes safety, minimizing waste, and adopting safer technologies. Future prospects include prioritizing recycling to lower environmental impacts and recover metals, with active research into recycling methods.

There are several design principles to bolster solar PV sustainability. Recommendations include consuming minimal energy during manufacturing, utilizing low carbon electricity, enhancing module efficiency, reducing waste, focusing on locations that offer the highest emission reductions, maximizing recycled content, extending product life, and evaluating non-module components for their carbon footprints.

Cadmium in Cadmium telluride (CdTe) Thin Film systems is noted as a concern, but the market share of CdTe remains low. Even though 95% of a PV module can be recycled, the economics of end-of-life (EOL) handling currently don't favor recycling.

References to safety standards and LCAs are also given for PV panels in this deliverable.

## <span id="page-14-0"></span>**1.5.2 The sub-value chain: Wind**

The wind energy value chain comprises several stages, offering opportunities for various stakeholders ranging from material suppliers to manufacturers and sectors benefiting from a cleaner energy mix. As of 2019, Europe stands as a global leader in wind turbine manufacturing and applications. In recent times, lightweight materials, especially Fiber-Reinforced Polymers (FRP), have seen substantial advancements. Innovations in material formulations and designs, have led to improved mechanical performance and reliability, like enhanced strength-to-weight ratios and corrosion resistance. Larger wind turbine blades are made, capturing more wind energy due to their increased size and heightened reliability. The current focus is on ensuring these materials remain safe, durable and recyclable.

Wind energy is making progress in areas like life cycle assessment of composites, reducing the use of Critical Raw Materials (CRMs) especially in magnets, and improving the recyclability of blade composites.

Currently, 85% of a wind turbine's mass is recyclable. There is a push to improve the longevity of turbines and reduce noise levels. Moreover, efforts are being made to enhance turbine performance, making them more powerful yet lighter.

Despite steel constituting 80% of the turbine's mass, blade recycling remains a challenge, and multiple systems are under development.

The wind industry is pivoting to innovative materials, such as polyurethane, offering benefits in cost and the production process. The sector aims to minimize production costs, enhance energy efficiency, reduce CO<sup>2</sup> footprints, and design turbines that are more sustainable at the end of their life cycle.

There are identified occupational risks like falls, electrocutions, fires. Furthermore, comprehensive LCA have been carried out to gauge the environmental footprint of wind energy installations.

Social evaluations note the benefits of wind energy as a clean, cost-effective electricity source. However, concerns remain about noise, aesthetic appeal, and potential environmental impacts.

The design phase is marked by exploring new materials and chemicals, with an emphasis on safety and recyclability. One significant concern is the inhalation risks posed by certain compounds used in the blade manufacturing process.

In essence, the wind energy sector is rapidly advancing in design, efficiency, and sustainability. While challenges persist, ongoing research and innovations promise a more sustainable (including recyclability) and efficient future for wind energy.

## <span id="page-15-1"></span>**1.6.1 The sub-value chain: Semiconductor for power control**

Semiconductor designed for power control are a significant subset of the electronics value chain, having unique SSbD requirements. Central to sustainability objectives in sectors like Energy and Automotive, these chips enable the shift from fossil fuels to renewable energy sources. This energy transition is essential for long-term sustainability, allowing the adoption of electrified systems with smart features such as adaptive lighting and temperature control. Efficient power management, spanning generation to consumption, is a fundamental challenge met by these chips. The lifecycle of this sub-value chain is similar to the one of the broader electronics value chain, but given their introduction after 2020, many haven't reached their end-of-life phase.

Predominant raw materials for these semiconductors are silicon carbide (SiC) and gallium nitride (GaN). Their sourcing is key, especially in light of recent gallium export restrictions by China. The production process for SiC and GaN wafers, currently more expensive than silicon wafers due to their energy-intensive nature, expects to see reducing costs with volume growth. These chips, when integrated into devices, contribute significantly to system energy efficiency and present minimal user safety concerns.

However, challenges lie in the resource and energy-intensive nature of both the material purification and chip production processes. Optimizations are expected, but major sustainability improvements will come from the adoption of renewable energy and enhanced resource recycling in production. the main SSbD challenges are the use of critical and toxic materials, the high energy intensity of chip production, and the hazardous processing of e-waste in economically challenged regions. The complex nature of these chips also raises issues about the recovery of materials, particularly at the nano-scale.

## <span id="page-26-1"></span><span id="page-26-0"></span>**5.1.2 The sub-value chain: Glass**

Glass sub-value chain analysis is provided by Mr Fabrice Rivet and Vanessa Chesnot from FEVE (the [European Container Glass Federation\)](https://feve.org/).

#### **5.1.2.1 The container glass industry**

The total glass production (excluding mineral glass wool) in 2022 stood at more than 39.5 million tonnes in 2022. The breakdown of the glass sector in 2022 was as follows: container glass 24,500,000 tonnes; flat glass 11,000,000 tonnes; reinforcing fibre glass 910,000 tonnes; domestic glass 1,161,000 tonnes, special glass 962,000 tonnes.

Container glass (also called packaging glass) represents thus by far the biggest sector of the glass industry. In Europe, it is represented by FEVE.

FEVE members produce glass containers for strategic value chains like food and beverage, perfumery, cosmetics and pharma markets. The industry serves these sectors in domestic markets and is an enabler for the export of high-end products across the world, which in turn delivers wealth to our economies through trade.

FEVE represents more than 90% of the container glass industry, with 60 corporate members belonging to approximately 20 independent industry groups. Their 160+ manufacturing plants are located across 23 European countries. As the whole glass value chain is located in the EU (extraction of most of the raw materials, glass production and glass recycling), the sector maintains an estimated 125,000 direct and indirect jobs along the value chain. Members produce over 80 billion glass containers per year.

The container glass industry produces innovative, stylish and sustainable products, which are present in every aspect of our daily lives. Glass is fully reusable and infinitely recyclable [5], as stated by environmental product footprint [6] in a closed loop as well as inert to keep products safe and secure.

Glass articles are transformed via a mineralogical process into the final article within single, integrated plants: there is no external transport of any glass in an intermediate state, for example to a converting industry. The main raw materials used in glass production are naturally occurring (sand, carbonates) and are not listed as critical [7].

Within the manufacturing process, prior to becoming articles, glass is produced in molten form and contained in tanks or channels which are entirely or largely closed, so that any risk of exposure to humans is minimized, if not avoided altogether.

By 2050 the glass packaging industry aims to transform glass production to offer fully climate-neutral packaging solutions in addition to being fully circular. As an energy-intensive sector, the industry continuously invests in reducing and optimising energy use and switching to green and renewable energies – to reduce the overall environmental impacts and costs linked to glass manufacturing.

Transitioning to climate-neutral glass production represents a long way in decarbonising production to meet the decarbonisation targets set by the Paris Climate Agreement: a -61% CO2 reduction by 2030 compared to 2005 and net carbon neutrality by 2050. Today, glass containers are 30% lighter, 70% less energy-intensive and emits 50% less CO2 than fifty years ago. Nonetheless, significant change are needed to achieve the goal of zero carbon emissions. A large sum of capital and operational expenditure is required. Public sector support and funding is, therefore, important to help industries deploy the technologies needed.

#### **5.1.2.2 Glass as a safe packaging material for food contact**

Made entirely of raw materials found in nature, glass is both uncomplicated and virtually inert and does no significant harm to the environment. Glass always maintains its inherent virgin like properties no matter how many times it is recycled. In addition:

- Glass is the only packaging material that does not need a plastic barrier, layer or liner and always remains healthy and safe for food contact packaging, no matter how many times it is recycled or reused.
- Because of its inherent inertness and protective properties, glass acts as a safe barrier to external agents, meaning products are preserved for longer in glass, even once opened. This makes glass the natural choice for preserving not only the quality of the product, but also for the health of the people who use it. The industry is partnering with customers to

introduce the [Glass Hallmark](https://glasshallmark.com/) onto packaging, serving, in part, to symbolise the commitment made to health and quality when choosing glass.

- Most common used additives in glass containers industry are colouring and decolouring agents for specific production runs: Iron chromite, iron oxide, iron sulphide, cobalt oxide, cerium oxide, selenium or zinc selenite.
- Glass is non-combustible and displays high chemical resistance to attack by water, acids and bases.
- It is considered inert in EU landfill legislation [8].
- It is exempted from registration requirements under REACH [9].
- It is recognized as safe for use in food contact [10], pharmaceutical and cosmetics applications.
- The minimal migration of impurities from glass has been recognized in derogations from EU legislation on packaging [11, 12] and on electrical and electronic equipment [13];
- Toxicological and eco-toxicological testing standards require the use of glass vessels for '*in vitro'* testing; glass waste can be transported across borders without requiring special permission [14].
- Vitrification (encapsulation in glass) of radioactive waste is a long-recognized practice for its long term storage and disposal.
- In the US, glass packaging has also received its seal of approval from the Food and Drug Administration, as it is "generally recognized as safe" (GRAS) by the agency [15].

### **5.1.2.3 Glass as a sustainable packaging material**

The European Container Glass manufacturing is a genuine circular model. Glass is fully reusable and infinitely recyclable in a closed loop as well as inert to keep products safe and secure, offering a Safe and Sustainable solution environmentally friendly alternative for a lot of packaging applications:

- Reusable glass has been a reality in Europe for decades, if not centuries. Glass dominates the refillable beverage containers market, where it accounts for 22% of packaging placed on the market in the key segments of beer, soft drinks and water (representing 96% of refillable beverage containers) in 2017.
- Glass is one of the most recycled packaging materials, with 80.1 % of all glass packaging put on the EU market in 2021 being collected for recycling. Using recycled glass saves energy and avoids CO<sup>2</sup> emissions. Recycled glass is today the most important raw material in glass containers: on average 52% of recycled content in Europe in 2012[. Close the Glass](http://www.closetheglassloop.eu/)  [Loop](http://www.closetheglassloop.eu/) is a stewardship platform that FEVE launched with his value chain partners: municipalities, brand owners, Extended Producer Responsibility Schemes, recyclers, and container glass manufacturers. Its target is to achieve a 90% glass packaging collection for recycling rate in the EU by 2030.

### Social aspects on glass value chain materials:

It is important to underline that sand found on beaches or riverbanks can never be used in glass making because not only is it illegal, but it contains far too many impurities. Sand extraction is strictly regulated by EU, national and local legislation and by no means can the sand used in glass making be the object of illicit trafficking. Extraction permits guarantee its sustainable sourcing as well as the rehabilitation of the quarries at the end of the extraction period.

*Figure 4* **G***lass* **container** *value chain*

- <span id="page-29-1"></span>• The container glass factory uses a mix of virgin raw materials (mainly sand and carbonates) and recycled glass (on average 52%) to produce new glass containers, which are sent to the customers' bottling plants. Raw materials used in glass making are locally sourced and do not travel a lot. The industrial sand used for glass production must comply with very strict technical specifications in terms of chemical purity and dimension. The glass industry value chain is more simple compare to other industries where several intermediates are necessary to convert the raw materials into a container (like plastic). In the glass industry, all manufacturing operations take place under the same roof: the raw materials are melted in a glass furnace, the molten glass is shaped using moulds into a container, which is then cooled down and packed in the same factory.

- The Brand or Customer bottling plant fills the glass containers with their products (beers, wines, soft drinks, food, perfumes, cosmetics, pharmaceutical products) and sends them to retailers, wholesalers or distributers.
- Retailers sell the products to the consumers, which are encouraged to recycle them.
- The collection method is essential to separate glass at source from other materials and avoid unnecessary handling which, by repeated breaking, can lead to small glass pieces difficult to treat and recycle. There is no single model for the collection of glass packaging waste in the European Union. Collection methods vary from street containers (also called "bottle banks") to kerbside collection (also called "door-to-door") to Deposit Return Schemes, from mono-material to comingled with other materials, or from colourseparated to mixed colours. The characteristics of the collection method will largely influence the total amount of glass packaging waste collected for recycling and the levels of purity with regard to the quality of the collected glass packaging waste. In 2021, 80.1% of glass packaging put on the EU market was collected for recycling. The industry initiated [Close the Glass Loop](https://closetheglassloop.eu/) stakeholder platform to achieve 90% collection in the EU by 2030.
- If the glass is reusable, once collected, it can be sent back to the fillers, where it will first undergo a quality check be cleaned to be refilled.
- Once collected, glass is sent to a glass recycling plant to produce furnace-ready cullet (the glass material that is recovered and used to replace raw materials in glass making is known as "cullet"). The sorting process within a Glass Recycling Facility is essential to remove the contaminants (as paper and plastics) from the glass packaging waste to meet industrial specifications for re-melt into new glass packaging products. Sorting happens in different steps (crushing, drying, air blowing for light pieces such as paper and plastics, magnetic separator for ferromagnetic metals, eddy currents separator for non-ferromagnetic metals such as aluminium and finally an optical sorting machine able to separate glass from ceramics and even sort glass by colour).
- After the glass recycling plant, the furnace-ready cullet can be either sent back to a container glass furnace (closed-loop recycling), to another sector of the glass industry (open-loop recycling) or be used in other applications (sand blasting, water filtering, etc).

# <span id="page-32-0"></span>**5.2 The Textiles value chain**

# <span id="page-32-1"></span>**5.2.1 The sub-value chain: Smart textiles**

While no 100% accepted definition exists, smart textiles or e-textiles are most commonly understood as textile fibre based materials or products with integrated electronic components or devices providing a smart functionality which textile materials or products without such components cannot achieve.

Smart functionalities such as heat absorption or release, physical deformation, colour change or controlled substance release among many others can also be achieved by nonelectronic means such as nanomaterials, microcapsules, functional coatings etc. But these cases are more broadly referred to as functional or multifunctional textile materials. Other commonly used terms are flexible electronics or smart wearables. In these cases the overlap with smart textiles is only partial, because these materials or products can also be made of non-textile materials such as films, paper, plastics, rubber, leather etc. And the case of wearables by definition excludes many non-wearable smart textile applications such as smart interiors or structural parts of buildings, cars, airplanes, tents, greenhouses and many more.

For the purpose of following value chain discussion only textile materials/products with integrated electric or electronic components.

Demonstrators and small series smart textile products have been on the market for almost 25 years, but most have remained niche products mostly targeted at medical, personal protection, sports and outdoor or smart interior products. In this sense the smart textiles value chain can be considered as an emerging value chain and an interesting case where 2 IRISS value chains, textile and microelectronics intersect.

The smart textiles value chain comprises multiple actors from three distinctive industries, namely electronics, textile, and ICT (Information and Communications Technology). This underlines both the need for cross-sectoral partnerships and how challenging it is to bring together competencies, energies and strategies based on three very different industries, viewpoints, and mindsets.

Currently, product development is predominantly driven by a technology push. Many products only reach the prototype stage because of the absence of (large-scale) manufacturing capabilities and the challenges related to the difficulty of finding the right long-lasting partnerships.

#### **Current challenges facing European smart textiles**

In short, one can say that there is no clear absence of any specific element, but rather a weakness in many of them that comes from **little collaborative history between the three industries concerned**. Furthermore, the still insufficient development of volume markets prevents most of the large businesses that predominantly supply equipment machinery, chemical, and electronics components from dedicating much effort and investment to smart textiles production.

Starting with **textile equipment manufacturers**, one can observe that the possibilities for simply adapting the equipment to smart textile production are clearly running out. It is up to traditional textile businesses to sensitise their suppliers on the need for fully novel machinery. A closer collaboration with EU research centres could greatly foster the industrialisation of lab innovations to develop such necessary new machinery.

Another gap can be observed in terms of **chemical supplies,** which are key to the functionalisation of smart textiles. Of course, such inputs are mostly outside of the scope of the smart textile value chain (e.g. metals, pigments etc.). They are produced by large players who are used to - and keen on - producing and supplying very large quantities for traditional textiles, whereas smart textile businesses usually need very specific materials in limited quantities, necessitating the involvement of dedicated formulating intermediaries.

On the skills front, one can observe that in order to develop innovative yarns, fabrics, coatings or embroideries, most traditional textile manufacturers are lacking in some **key competencies** to use new processes and new materials, or design new commercial strategies to develop, manufacture, and bring products to the market. In particular, textile companies usually lack specific knowledge of electronics, which prevents them from being actively involved in scaling up the smart textile manufacturing.

Another crucial challenge for the industry lies at the **assembly stage** of functionalised items or garments. The latest design and CAD software, enabling the creation and alignment of cutting patterns for the textile articles assembly can be used, but very few examples of an automated, larger-scale production can yet be seen in the industry.

To meet the existing challenges, several highly promising fields are opening up to the industry. The most important one is the development of **flexible electronics**, which allow for efficient connections between soft material substrates and hard metal components. However, the manufacturing of flexible and stretchable electronics requires processes totally different from the traditional methods used to manufacture conventional rigid integrated circuits, like pattern transfer, solution printing, roll-to-roll capabilities and additive manufacturing.

Another recent promising development lies in **chip platforms** using hybrid architecture, combining a system-on-chip with a low-power coprocessor. This translates into significantly better performance and user experience, faster memory and launching of apps, richer graphics and user interfaces, and improved photo and video functionality.

Currently, aside from the technological developments, the main challenge in the area of silicon chip manufacturing, is their **availability**. The huge demand for chips has created a shortage that could be a threat to the development of smart textiles applications.

Another development would positively impact the very cost structure of smart textiles. The current high cost of traditional electronics manufacturing process prevents the development of significantly large markets for smart textiles and the successful marketing of many applications. Very promising are the lower costs and attractive new properties of organic electronics, constructed from organic (carbon-based) molecules or polymers using synthetic strategies developed in the context of organic chemistry and polymer chemistry.

Further down the manufacturing chain, **assembling textile electronics** objects requires the efficient integration of electronics into textiles at different stages (i.e. at the level of yarn or fabric) and the use of coating or encapsulation techniques to avoid removal of the electronics prior to washing or cleaning. Assembly techniques can be manifold, but presently they largely rely on manual processes.

Most challenging is the need for a robust **integration and contacting technologies** that can resist normal usage of clothing and the miniaturisation of power supply and storage devices. In addition, fully automated manufacturing capabilities are still missing and machine development that covers the entire process chain of the integration of electronic components in textiles clearly needs to be intensified.

The **integration of electronics** or textile electronic objects into garments or other articles (upholstery, bedding, carpets etc.) is made by 'system integrators', essentially SMEs that bridge textile and electronics companies by providing electronics-related expertise to textile companies and textile-related expertise to electronics companies. However, their limited production capacity implies high costs and restricts consumer markets to high-end ones. Two other barriers currently slowing down the development of smart textile assemblies are the lack of specific design software and of guidelines or even standardisation of components specifically dedicated to smart textiles.

In the area of **software components,** connectivity and platform building are two key elements of the successful development of any smart textile. Players relating to this node in the value chain are experts in building interfaces to connect different materials, processes and systems, establishing platforms and the respective interfaces, software and hardware, in order to collect data for the development of additional functionalities.

**Data collection, management and analysis** should not at all be considered outside of the smart textile value-chain. The smart textile community needs to work more closely with those dedicated players that can transform sensor data and processing data into data that is ready for use in software applications to implement the "smart" functionality of textiles.

At the next stage of the chain, one finds the **Smart Devices or Smart Systems** resulting from the smart textile product assembly, which heavily rely on the additional use of software. Each device is part of a network of Smart Devices, the so-called Smart Network. This activity

represents a necessary strong link in the value chain, as data that is used in this Smart Network is in the cloud and is therefore linked to security issues, data analytics, data processing, etc.

**Retailing** currently represents a weak point in the value chain, as design and production costs tend to limit the market to a niche caring to the need of professional performers of various kinds. More generally, a related key issue that limits smart textile success is that the whole value-chain is driven by technology more than by market demand and does not rely enough either on **market research or promotional activities**. From the earliest research and development stages of any project, it is particularly crucial to ensure that the unique properties of textiles are indeed essential for the desired use case and cannot be more competitively supplied by alternative solutions (e.g. wearables, external monitoring etc.). This condition would often need to be more carefully validated.

Should the markets expand rapidly, smart textiles might result in a new kind of waste that could be difficult to recycle and would have to comply with European waste regulations. Therefore, a major challenge for the industry, and increasingly so, is the need to implement **eco-design** in the technological development process and minimise future waste. For the time being, one must admit the existing design for recycling principles for textiles or electronics rarely match with the properties of the combined products. Therefore, life-cycle thinking needs to be implemented *together* with the technological development process.

Throughout the smart textile value chain, businesses suffer from a regulatory gap in terms of guidance when it comes to compliance (e.g. testing, assessment, labelling), since parts of the **regulation** are either missing or are too highly fragmented. At the same time, stakeholders demand more regulatory control at the international level to ensure fair competition, especially between Asian and Western businesses.

More standardisation is also required to facilitate stakeholder interactions, e.g. creating interfaces between electronics and textiles, contacting, data standards, testing standards etc. Both regulations and legislation are needed to support the intended usage scenarios and prevent misuse.

# <span id="page-36-0"></span>**5.2.3 Main SSbD challenges foreseen**

The main SSbD challenges in the smart textiles value chain stem from the fact that the textile and electronics sectors have different approaches to assessing safety and sustainability in the chemicals, materials and processes they use. In addition, textile products that contain electronic components such as wires, connectors, sensors, LEDs, batteries etc. create user comfort and safety issues as well as end of life challenges that differ from conventional textile products.

Specific safety challenges include:

- Potential toxicity of metals (silver, copper) and chemicals used in the electronic components that get integrated into a smart textile product.

- Skin irritations or burns from hard, rough, heat or radiation-emitting components in close contact with the skin.
- Damage or degradation of electronic components during production or use such as washing liquids and detergents, sweat, weather/UV exposure or excessive physical wear and tear.
- Alteration of typical textile material behaviours such as heat and flame resistance through presence of electronic components leading to heightened fire risk especially for smart interior textiles.
- Higher risks for children/infants due to presence of cables or detachable small parts.

Specific sustainability challenges include:

- Higher waste generation in production due to complexity and delicacy of electronics integration into textiles.
- Higher post-consumer waste generation due to higher damage in use or faster technical obsolescence of smart textile products compared to conventional textile products.
- Greater difficulty in reuse and recycling of end-of-life smart textile products compared to conventional ones (electronic components represent disruptors for mechanical and chemical textile recycling processes).
- Lack of established separate end-of-life collection systems for smart textiles.

#### Cross-cutting challenges:

- Cross-sectoral supply chains are more difficult to organize and SSbD data generation and exchange can be more challenging due to differing taxonomies, data standards, test methods, respective process know-how.
- Standardization challenges due to differing standards, standardization bodies and processes for textile products and electronic components and devices.
- Inability of typically SME-sized developers and marketers of smart textile products to practice and finance comprehensive SSbD design principles and processes. Smart textiles development involves a lot of trial-and-error prototyping and user testing.

### <span id="page-38-0"></span>**5.3.1 Insulation Materials sub-value chain**

According to the JRC report of 2018, buildings consume 40% of the EU's primary energy demand and produce about 35% of all greenhouse gas emissions. Buildings have a large energy-savings potential through renovation and upgrading. Under the existing Energy Performance of Buildings Directive, the EU countries must set minimum energy performance requirements for new buildings and for the major renovation of buildings. Therefore, insulation materials contribute significantly to improving the overall energy efficiency and sustainability of buildings, especially by reducing the energy losses through the building envelope (walls, roofs, floors, etc.) [17].

There is a wide range of insulation materials currently available: cellulose, fiberglass, HempCrete (blocks), mineral wool (glass and stone wool), Polystyrene, Polyurethane (PU) foams (with or without blowing agents), straw (panels) and wood (fibre, board). The most used materials for building insulation are *wool minerals* (glass and stone wool) and *polymeric materials* (Expanded PolyStyrene (EPS), Extruded Polystyrene (XPS) and Polyurethane (PUR)) (see figure 7 below) [17].

The focus of this SSbD evaluation will be on the *construction chemicals* sub-value chains (referred to 'organic fossil fuel derived' in the JRC Report) of PU based insulation materials.

## <span id="page-38-1"></span>**5.3.2 Polystyrene or Polyurethane foams sub-value chains**

Polystyrene or Polyurethane foams can be used in combination with structural materials, such as concrete. They are lightweight materials and excellent *insulators*. Foams can be used as part of an insulated panel, where the foam is sandwiched between cement or insulating concrete forms. As the application of the SSbD framework offers more challenges and opportunities for polyurethane foams than polystyrene foams, it's application will be reviewed for PU foams only.

The PU foams sub-value chain comprises four subsequent or overlapping stages:

- 1. **Manufacture of raw materials**, such as diisocyanates and polyols.
- 2. **Manufacture of PU foams** is often done on-site at the building itself.
- 3. **Application / Use of PU foams** in buildings at the building itself.
- **4. Recycling / Reuse of PU Foams**

The manufacture of raw materials is done by (very) large chemical companies with experience and in-depth knowledge of the raw materials and done, where required, under strictly controlled conditions. The manufacture and use of PU foams is typically done at the building site itself, often by SMEs with expert knowledge and experience.

In Europe, there are more than 7,000 companies involved directly in the production and use of polyurethane raw materials, elements and finished goods. If the complete chain of value for these products and their final market uses is considered, the number of companies (largely SMEs) impacted increases tenfold, for a total of 70,250 companies. In addition to the wealth creation of €59.34 billion, this value chain creates close to 3.8 million jobs in the European economy. The largest share of polyurethane products in Europe is used for construction in all types of infrastructure projects. However, its most significant role within this sector is building insulation [18].

## <span id="page-39-0"></span>**5.3.3 Polyurethane (PU) foams – State of Play**

Polyurethane (PU) is essentially made of two raw materials, a (di-)isocyanate and a polyol, both of which are derived from crude oil. After mixing those two process-ready liquid components of the system and various auxiliary materials, such as catalysts, foaming agents, and stabilizers, a chemical reaction starts resulting in the polyurethane. In addition, a blowing agent is required.

As an element of their Research & Innovation efforts, industry has been developing low isocyanate and isocyanate-free foams, to foster safer use of such chemicals. The main challenge for such low-isocyanate and isocyanate-free foams, however, is their performance, as the absence of di-isocyanates results in a significant loss in performance.

The potential of polyurethane foams to be reused, offers clear opportunities for advancing circularity.

Probably, the most challenging SSbD criterion of PU Foams is related to the manufacture of PU foams and the use of di-isocyanates as raw materials. PolyUrethane (PU) foams themselves typically do not attract an EU classification or label but are manufactured using di-isocyanates as raw materials, which are classified as a Substances of Very High Concern (SVHC) and their use is **restricted** under the REACH Regulation (the Restriction was published on the 4th of August 2020 and will apply from the 24th of August 2023, after a transition period of three years).

To meet the requirements of the REACH restriction, raw material and polyurethane manufacturers have, together with the downstream users of polyurethanes, developed training materials and courses for professional users, in the official language(s) of the EU Member State(s) where the substance(s) or mixture(s) are applied [19]. The training covers a wide range of different applications and takes into consideration the specificity of the products supplied, including composition, packaging and design.

Another aspect of PU foams is that they require a **blowing agent**. Traditionally HFC (HydroFluoroCarbon) was used as a blowing agent, but this is an ozone depleting substance with a high global warming potential. The industry has developed an alternative blowing agent: HFO (HydroFluoroOlefin), which is not ozone depleting and has a low global warming potential. Decomposition of HFO in the atmosphere, however, may lead to depositions of trifluoroacetic acid (TCA) in groundwater and soil. Further advances have been reported for water-induced self-blown non-isocyanate polyurethane foams [20].

Recently, a very relevant project has been initiated called '[Circular Foam](https://circular-foam.eu/)' 3 , which is a multistakeholder research project that has been initiated by an SME called 'Circularise' and is focusing on improving the circularity of rigid PU foams. The project runs from October 2021 until September 2025 and the project partners include Research Institutes, raw materials producers, foam producers, municipalities, and other entities. The main goal of the project is to develop a system for communicating production process data.

#### **Energy costs/Green House Gases (GHG) emissions**

Probably the main advantage of polyurethanes is that they lead to reduced energy costs and therefore, make buildings eco-friendlier. 40% of the global energy demand, correlating with one third of greenhouse gas emissions are linked to infrastructures like houses and buildings. Experts estimate that worldwide insulation of buildings to optimal standards could reduce global CO<sup>2</sup> emissions by 20%. Therefore, PU Foams are essential in meeting the GHG goal of the European-Green Deal [21].

#### **Durability**

Another positive SSbD criterion of PU based foams is their durability as they offer a much longer lifetime than other insulation materials.

#### **Life Cycle Assessment**

PU Europe, the industry association representing PU manufacturers in Europe, commissioned PwC to write a comprehensive report on an Environmental and Economic Analysis of insulation materials in low energy buildings [22]. The goal was to provide evidence that the methods developed at European level, provide accurate results with regards to the environmental and cost performance of a building (element) based on specific product choices. Three different insulation materials have been evaluated: Polyurethane (PU), Expanded Polystyrene (EPS), and Stone Wool (SW).

A comprehensive Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) as well as a Life Cycle Cost (LCC) analysis was conducted for all scenarios. The highest costs over the lifetime are energy costs (31%), construction costs (except insulation) (21%), and maintenance (15%), where the costs of insulation were low (5%). The overall costs of construction differ less than 4.3% for the four different scenarios, showing that costs are not a deciding factor in the choice of insulation material.

Finally, the project stressed the importance of building design solutions and demonstrates that the impact of insulation products on the overall environmental burden of a building is negligible compared to their benefits in the **building use phase**.

#### **Raw Materials**

Similar to other PU foams such as those used for mattresses and upholstered furniture, automotive moulded foams are made by reacting di‐isocyanates and polyols. Both products are typically derived from crude oil. Polyols however can also be made of natural oils from renewable sources and since recently so-called "mass-balance" polyols and diisocyanates made from (bio-) circular sources are also available on the market and start being used. Di-isocyanate can be either toluene diisocyanate (TDI) or diphenylmethane diisocyanate (MDI), and both are used in vehicle seating (60-70% MDI and 30-40% TDI). When these ingredients are mixed, they react and foam. Depending on the end product, the chemical formulations may contain other ingredients such as additives, catalysts, colorants, water/carbon dioxide [23].

#### **Production & Transformation (including design)**

Moulded flexible foam is made by injecting pre-determined amount of reactant blend (isocyanate and premix) into a pre-heated mould coated with a release agent into the preheated mould (55°C). When the mixture rises and expands, it occupies the whole space in the mold and solidifies. At the end of the production line, the mould opens and the moulded foam part may be removed from the mould ('demoulded') [24]. After demoulding, the quality control determines the further flow of the parts (e.g. sent to packing and storage area, rework, post-treatment, assembly or scrapping). #### **Use Phase**

Flexible PU foam is a durable material, with the ability to recover its properties. This insures that during normal day-to-day use the product will perform at essentially the same level as when produced over the lifetime of the vehicle [27].

After complying with the stringent tests on mechanical and physical properties in conformity with OEM specifications, the moulded foam stays in the car seat for the lifetime of the car unless there is a major damage.

#### **End-of-life**

Flexible polyurethane foam can be recycled via mechanical recycling and depolymerization. While this can apply to moulded foams, recycling of automotive moulded foams is not common today, contrary to what is happening for mattresses in some countries in Europe. The main reasons for this are: 1) A double mattress can have nearly as much PU foam as an entire car but unlike in a mattress, PU foam in automobiles is often scattered in different places of the vehicles and not always easily accessible 2) Car seats are designed to last for the whole lifetime of the car and therefore include other materials embedded in the foam such as metal inserts or sensors for safety features, which would need to be removed (manually) before recycling, 3) Typically, seats are not removed from cars before an end-oflife vehicle is shredded, meaning PU foams end up being contaminated with oil or other liquids and thus not be clean enough for recycling. There is some promising research ongoing to recycle PU-containing automotive shredder residue (ASR) via chemical recycling. This would have the advantage of allowing to overcome many of the above-mentioned challenges as it would not require expensive manual dismantling or introducing new steps in ELV (End of Life Vehicle) treatment processes. But this is still at the initial R&D stage at the moment [27].

#### *5.4.1.3.1 Priorities on product Safety*

#### **Mechanical properties**

Foams have to undergo very stringent mechanical properties testing (varying from one OEM to the other) to ensure the foams can last the whole lifetime of the car or even longer. The automotive OEM design specifications are linked with aspects as comfort and safety. For instance, the design of seats has implications on safety, to prevent spinal and head injuries, in case of collisions. Every seat element is significant, beginning with head rests, which stabilize the cervical section of the spine, through seats and a back rests, which are supposed to stabilize the lumbar spine and to prevent leaning of the passenger body sideways on curves, ending with safety belts [30]. There are car seat safety standards which guide the design of car seats, meaning that also changes to designs must be carefully assessed including through a series of demanding tests in prototype workshops, in order to ensure that e.g. use of new materials do not affect safety or functionality. The whole process can take several years and varies from vehicle to vehicle. For example, there are differences in seat design between internal combustion engine cars and electric cars due to the differently located centre of gravity in these two types of vehicles.

#### **Emissions**

Automotive OEM requirements for indoor air quality are very relevant for the polyurethane industry. Automotive suppliers need to validate the compliance of their components to certain OEM specifications for emission behaviour prior to their use in the vehicle interior. Below is an overview of the most relevant emissions associated with PU foams in car interior:

- **Volatile Organic Compounds emissions:** In recent years governmental regulation has pushed for the reduction in Volatile Organic Compounds (VOCs) emissions. VOCs can have adverse effects on the human health and can negatively impact the environment. This concern has encouraged the development of new analysis methods and the introduction of emission standards by car manufacturers. The requirement for foam products according to the Verband der Automobilindustrie (VDA) 278 standard is ≤100 μg/g for VOC and ≤250 μg/g for Semi-Volatile Organic Compounds (FOG). The Global Automotive Declarable Substance List (GADSL) includes volatile substances, and its presence in the products must be indicated according to industry standards. Most currently used standards specify the volatile content of products for a given compound (e.g., acetone, toluene), so it is often difficult to determine exactly which component is responsible for the emission. It is thought that the catalysts (e.g. triethylenediamine) and surfactant (e.g. siloxanes) used in the formulation of PU foams are responsible for most of the VOC content. German car manufacturers have set VOC specifications using VDA (Verband der Automobilindustrie) and chamber test methods to measure emissions from materials in the interior of the automobile. The Japanese Automobile Manufacturers Association

(JAMA) set voluntary VOC specifications for the cabin of new models of passenger cars manufactured and sold starting in 2007. The Chinese government introduced regulations beginning in 2007 with the VOC test method HJ-T400-2007 for Cabin of Vehicles. Starting in March 2012, the GB/T 27630-2011 "Guidelines for air quality assessment of passenger car" which are based on the Chinese "Indoor air quality standards" GB/T 18883-2002 were formally implemented [31, 32, 33].

- **Semi-volatile organic compounds**: Besides volatile organic compounds (VOC), plastics and other non-metallic materials release semi-volatile organic compounds (FOG). Such compounds can condense on colder surfaces (e.g. windshields) and lead to a layer that impairs visibility. This effect is also known as window fogging [31].

#### **Flammability**

The criteria for burn resistance standards can vary, meaning that automotive engineers need to consider precise national, international, or industry-specific prerequisites when selecting materials. Moreover, engineers may seek flame retardant foams that align with Original Equipment Manufacturer (OEM) demands for reduced emissions, improved aging characteristics, and enhanced toxicity profiles. As material suppliers introduce innovative products into the market, vehicle designs have the opportunity to integrate flame retardant automotive foams that not only meet safety standards but also contribute to reducing noise, dampening vibration, and mitigating harshness. In Europe, Canada, and Japan ISO 3795:1989 specifies a method for determining the horizontal burning rate of materials used in the occupant compartment of vehicles, after exposure to a small flame. In the United States, the fire performance of automotive foams is tested according to [FMVSS 302.](https://www.nhtsa.gov/sites/nhtsa.dot.gov/files/documents/2017saebhennessey.pdf) China (GB 8410), Britain (BS AU 169a), and many other countries also maintain flammability standards for motor vehicles. Flammability requirements are lower for automotive parts than for trains or planes, which has an impact on the use of flame retardant. Halogenated flame retardants are not used anymore in automotive parts [26].

### *5.4.1.3.2 Climate neutrality and circularity*

Since the automotive sector is a large consumer of resources, it is of fundamental importance to maximise material efficiency through simplified product design. Design choices associated with complexity (e.g., multilayer materials), the nature of materials, the presence of additives and/or specific substances impact the durability but also recyclability of products and materials.

With the recently proposed End-of-Life Vehicles Regulation, the European Commission lays down circularity requirements on vehicle design and production related to reusability, recyclability and recoverability and the use of recycled content, which are to be verified at type-approval of vehicles, and on information and labelling requirements on parts, components and materials in vehicles. Among other things, some of the main objectives are to 1) facilitate and increase the removal, reuse, remanufacturing and recycling of materials, parts and components; 2) increase the use of recycled materials in the production of vehicles, thereby incentivising recycling [27].

The integration of recycled content in plastics, as proposed in the Regulation, has sparked increased dialogue in the automotive supply chain, to reach a common understanding of what can be achieved within the next few years on plastic circularity and what other solutions are existing to increase plastics sustainability profile including in terms of its carbon footprint. Below are a few items which are relevant in this context:

- **Light-weighting:** Similarly to other plastics used in vehicles, PU moulded foams are relatively light-weight materials which allow decreasing fuel consumption. Notwithstanding the fact that seating systems represent almost 40 percent of the weight of a vehicle's main interior components, seating foams have one of the lowest densities of any plastic material used in a car. Continuous investments in R&I are made to further reduce the material density while maintaining performance.
- **CO<sup>2</sup> footprint/raw materials:** The overall impact of PU in the lifecycle of a car is relatively small (< 1% of the car lifecycle GHG emissions) and the biggest share of its impact is due to the raw materials used in its production are responsible for 90% of the total climate impacts from the production of polyurethane foam. While the raw materials used for flexible PU foam production are often derived from crude oil, there is an increased use of bio-polyols, recycled polyols and CO2-based polyols. The first one is by using biomaterials (e.g. castor, rapeseed or soybean oils) as feedstock. More recently, recycled polyols from depolymerisation of end-of-life mattresses are also available on the market. The main way of integrating recycled content in PU for automotive applications is via the mass-balance approach. An increasing number of such raw materials and PU parts made with these materials are already on the market. Both alternatives have been tested and tried in various applications in the automotive sector [27, 29].
- **Design for lifetime maximisation:** Circular vehicles should be designed for longevity and durability, while enabling product life extension features such as maintenance, repair, remanufacturing and updates. This has a positive impact on the environment as materials are kept in use for longer, reducing the need for additional material consumption. Longevity and durability go hand in hand with the ability to easily detect and anticipate possible failures or malfunctioning. Also, maintaining vehicle attractiveness over time is crucial to keep the perceived quality level and the possible residual value for the end customer. Foams are designed to last the entire vehicle lifetime, meaning that need for remanufacturing or repair. The current challenge is around reconsidering the design of car seats so that they can be dismantled more easily and parts can be reused or changed if needed (e.g seat covers)
- **Design for Recyclability:** Recyclability of PU foams from ELVs is strongly linked with the ability to remove and dismantle the car seats from the vehicle structure, for instance to avoid contamination. The dismantling of ELV parts is heavily related to market demands for such parts. Specific models (depending on their brand, style, and age) utilise different parts and therefore rely on what the contemporary car fleet requirements are. This can limit the incentive to recover car seats from ELVs, considering the costs and little economic value, and hence implement changes on the design to improve removability [27].

#### *5.4.1.3.3 Description of known safety and sustainability issues in the specified VC*

- **Limitations of alternative materials:** Automotive vehicles are safety sensitive products, which means that they must comply with strict standards on technical properties. The incorporation of recycled materials into vehicles' supply chain depends on their performance e.g., in terms of impact resistance or deformation, or even emission limits for volatile organic compounds. Currently, secondary polyols can only be used in relatively small fractions in foam formulations without losing quality. Similarly, the issue of availability must be considered. Chemical recycling allows the use of plastic waste, especially feedstock not suitable for mechanical recycling and it can deliver additional material for automotive applications that require high-quality recycled plastics for safety, regulatory and performance reasons. Currently, this process takes place without plastic waste but rather other waste sources. EU harmonised rules for calculating chemically recycled content will need to be in place [27, 29].
- **Accessing and recycling end of life moulded foams**: The primary obstacle to achieving circularity in car moulded foams lies in the challenge of dismantling before contamination, which involves the extraction of foam components before shredding. A significant portion of post-consumer plastics from vehicles is currently directed towards energy recovery (41%) and disposal (40%), while only a mere 19% undergoes recycling. Furthermore, an even smaller fraction (ranging from 5% to 15%) of the recycled plastics is reintegrated into the automotive sector. This situation mirrors the difficulties of recovering materials once they are integrated into complex car components, particularly when the materials have a low economic value (on average, the total value of all plastics used in typical vehicles is estimated at EUR 467 per vehicle). Unlike the case of mattresses, where there is relatively easy access to the PU foam, in car seats there will be other materials glued to the foam or inserted in the foams making it economically unviable to dismantle. This presents a significant challenge to achieving circularity because the cost of material recovery can be high, while the intrinsic economic worth of these materials remains low. Otherwise, if the car seat can be removed from the car before the shredder in a clean and dry state it can in theory be recycled [27].

<span id="page-59-0"></span>The structure of blades usually consists of a spar composed of materials formed of fibreglass and carbon pre-coated with epoxy resin acting as the inner part of the blade. The spar is then covered by outer laminated shells of fibreglass, moulded and cured in halves, then joined using an epoxy adhesive (Figure 14).

The material compositions vary between blade types and blade manufacturers, but blades are generally made of

- 1) Reinforced fibres (glass, carbon, aramid or basalt)
- 2) A polymer matrix (thermosets such as epoxies, polyesters, vinyl esters, polyurethane, or thermoplastics)
- 3) A sandwich core (balsa wood or foams such as polyvinyl PVC, PET)
- 4) Coatings (PE, PUR) 5) Metals (copper wiring, steel bolts, etc.).

# <span id="page-65-0"></span>**5.6 The Electronics value chain**

# <span id="page-65-1"></span>**5.6.1 Update on life cycle stages (from D4.1)**

Since the fall of 2022, there have been developments on regulations as well as announcements and actions by major manufacturers of electronics targeting the improvements in the upgrade/repair and refurbishment loops of the life cycle [\(Figure 17\)](#page-66-0), with additional ramifications for the end-of-life processing of electronics and reduction in the use of raw materials by the value chain.

![](_page_66_Diagram_1.jpeg)

*Figure 17 Life cycle diagram for the electronics value chain*

#### <span id="page-66-0"></span>**5.6.1.1 Regulation**

In 2023, the EC adopted new regulations on common rules promoting the repair of goods [55], also commonly known as *right to repair* regulation. Provisions specific to mobile phones and tablets direct the design of these devices to be energy efficient and durable, provide for consumers to be able to easily repair, upgrade and maintain them, and for possibilities to reuse and recycle the devices [56]. These regulations are part of a broader process of regulatory involvement in the SSbD aspects of electronics, particularly for devices sold to individual consumers in the EU. Two examples of more specific initiatives include the agreement with Nintendo to provide free repairs for Switch controllers [57] and the adoption of a common charging solution for all electronic devices [58].

Other jurisdictions are in the process of implementing their versions of *right to repair* regulations, including Canada, New York state (US)[59], India [60], and Minnesota (US) [61]. An important jurisdiction of California, where many technology companies are headquartered, is preparing a strong *right to repair* legislation, which recently gained support from *Apple* [62].

Regulation is also expected to be a major driver for increasing the use of refurbished electronics in commercial and public/government installations [63].

#### **5.6.1.2 Design for repairability**

There are companies making significant design efforts to ensure that their devices are easy to repair, upgrade, and operate for many years. Such companies offer modular-design laptops and mobile phones, with a proven history of introducing new models and upgrades. This includes long-term support for operating systems, with 8 years for Fairphone 5 [64], produced by *Fairphone*. Modular laptops produced by *Framework* are designed for easy service and repairs, making it easier (and economically viable) to offer refurbished devices for sale [65].

While companies such as *Fairphone* and *Framework* demonstrate that products designed for improved repairability are economically viable and sustainable, similar designs are considered [66] by but have not yet been adopted by major manufacturers of electronics [67]. Nevertheless, design changes introduced by major manufacturers to enable their own *right to repair* and sustainability programs [68], make it easier for professional facilities to repair and refurbish them. In particular, refurbishing of used mobile phones is now economically viable in high-income countries, such as Japan [69] and the UK [70], lowering the amount of e-waste exported for processing in low-income countries.

#### **5.6.1.3 Use of renewable and recycled materials**

Because of *Fairphone*'s emphasis on sustainability and fair-sourced materials, Fairphone 5 serves, perhaps, as the best example of including these considerations for every stage of the life cycle [64]. In 2022 and 2023, many major manufacturers of electronics introduced products that include significant amounts of renewable or recycled materials. For example, *Lenovo* introduced laptop cases made out of woven strands of, plant-derived, Flax Fiber and 75% recycled aluminum [71]. *Samsung* launched devices made using eco-friendly materials, such as repurposed fishing nets [72]. Both *Apple* [73] and *Google* [74] have announced ambitious targets for sustainability and use of recycled materials and are investing significant resources into programs designed to achieve those targets.

# <span id="page-67-0"></span>**5.6.2 SSbD in sub-value chain: Semiconductors for power control**

Semiconductor chips for power control are an excellent example of an Electronics sub-value chain that both has its own SSbD considerations and plays a critical enabling role in sustainability efforts of other value chains, e.g., both Energy and Automotive value chains in IRISS.

Electrification is considered to be one of the critical approaches for reaching long-term sustainability in many activities, whether performed by individuals or at industrial scales. For example, in transport, switching from engines powered by fossil fuels to electric motors will dramatically reduce the associated carbon footprint as the economies switch to renewable energy sources. In many cases, electrified systems and devices are also able to support additional sustainable practices, by taking advantage of "smart" functionalities, such as adaptive lighting, heating, and cooling systems in buildings.

![](_page_68_Diagram_1.jpeg)

Fundamental to all the electrified systems is the challenge of power management. More specifically, the challenge of efficiently controlling and transforming electrical power from generation, to transmission, to storage, to its final use. Along this chain, electrical power often needs to be transformed between alternating and direct current (AC and DC) and/or to control the voltage and current. In modern systems, these control and transformation functions are typically performed by specialized semiconductor chips.

The overall life cycle for this sub-value chain is analogous to that for the full electronics value chain [\(Figure 17\)](#page-66-0), but because the products in current use have been introduced into the market starting in 2020, the majority of them have not yet reached the end of life and the following steps, which, accordingly, have not been extensively considered specifically for power electronics, but rather are expected to follow the same pathways that are under development for the full value chain of electronics. The steps covered in this report include:

- Raw materials / purified raw materials
- Production
- Products in use

### **5.6.2.1 Materials for power-control semiconductors**

The two main semiconductor materials used in advanced power-control electronics are silicon carbide (SiC) and gallium nitride (GaN), as illustrated in [Figure 18.](#page-68-0) The chips also include small amounts of additional metals (e.g., used as contacts), some of which are from the list of the critical raw materials (CRM). The total absolute amounts of such CRMs used in power electronics is relatively minor compared to the amounts used in other value chains (e.g., Power or Automotive), fractions of a gram per individual chip, so the semiconductor industry expects to be able to obtain the necessary raw materials from fair sources [75].

Of the four primary elements in SiC and GaN, gallium is currently the only element for which there is a concern about availability in the short term. On 3 July 2023, China announced restrictions on export of gallium, for which it controls approximately 30% of the worldwide export market [76]. While there are other suppliers and additional capacity to produce both unrefined and refined gallium can be developed elsewhere, the cost of gallium has increased after the announcement of these restrictions [77].

#### **5.6.2.2 Production of semiconductors for power control**

The production of SiC and GaN wafers (or layers of these materials supported on wafers made of materials more common in electronics industry) requires comparable energy inputs to those used for production of the silicon wafers. Details of growing and handling these materials make them more difficult to produce, especially on the larger wafer sizes that are required for large-scale production, leading to higher production costs than those for Sibased chips of comparable size and complexity. As the demand and production volumes continue to increase, however, the cost of producing the materials will be reduced. For example, a major GaN producer aiming to become an automotive-qualified GaN supplier, *BelGaN* [78], is located in the region of Belgium where recently a *GaN Valley*™ has been established [79] to promote the scale-up of producing GaN wafers and devices.

Similarly to the production of other chips for electronics, the production of SiC and GaN chips requires the use of cleanrooms. Accordingly, the safety concerns in the production facilities are handled using technologies that have been developed for the broader semiconductor industry, including the protocols and equipment for safe handling and abatement/disposal of various toxic/dangerous fluids and gases that are involved in production of chips. The use of similar protocols also means that the safe handling and abatement procedures are very intensive in terms of resource consumption and CO<sup>2</sup> emissions, which will need to be addressed following approaches under development in the semiconductor sector [80].

#### **5.6.2.3 Power-control electronics in use**

In another analogy to the other types of chips, power-control chips are encapsulated before being incorporated into devices, meaning that they are protected from the contact with environment during the use phase and thus do not raise safety concerns related to exposure of the users to the materials inside the chips.

Impact of SiC and GaN chips on sustainability of the value chains where they are used, including Power and Transportation, are overwhelmingly positive, stemming from the increased efficiency of the power conversion that they enable. Because the increased efficiency applies to the full flow of energy during the operation, e.g., of an electric vehicle, reducing energy losses even by a few % results in significant energy savings over the lifetime of the power-control chips, exceeding the energy used for their production.

An additional factor for many applications is the small size and weight of power-control devices enabled by SiC or GaN chips, which can be incorporated directly into the components where the energy is consumed. For example, motors for high-performance electric vehicles, e.g., produced by *Tesla* or *Koenigsegg*, use SiC chips in inverters that control the power delivered to their motors [81]. Some of the applications of electrical power in the

transportation sector would not be possible without the use of the SiC or GaN chips, which support very high power density and fast switching times.

#### **5.6.2.4 Power semiconductors sub-value chain stakeholders**

As a growing and developing sub-value chain of the semiconductor industry, the power semiconductors sector is working to establish the ecosystem and supply chains analogous to those established for the traditional semiconductors. Important stages of the supply chain are:

- Source materials
- Production of wafers
- Production of power-control devices

**Source materials.** The main compound semiconductors currently used to make powercontrol devices are SiC and GaN. All four primary elements in these semiconductors are considered commodity materials and can be obtained through the traditional supply chain of the semiconductor industry. The only element for which there is a concern about supplies in the short term is gallium, due to the restrictions on export of gallium imposed by China. Japan stands to increase its role as a supplier of refined gallium because it has capability to produce refined gallium starting from both imported and domestically produced primary gallium. *Ingal Stade GmbH* produced primary gallium in Germany until 2016 and has announced plans to restart the production when gallium prices increased. So while the export restrictions from China have raised the prices of gallium, the supply chains are expected to be able to adjust to this disruption in the medium-to-long term.

**Production of wafers.** The production of SiC and GaN wafers, which are the starting materials for fabrication of semiconductor-based electronic devices, is conceptually similar to that of the Si wafers. Intrinsic properties of SiC and GaN, e.g., the hardness of SiC that is well-known from its applications in the tools, however, make new developments necessary in the basic technologies used to process the wafers, including cutting and polishing.

In particular, the development challenges are being addressed in increasing the diameter of the wafers from 150 mm (6 inches) to 200 mm (8 inches), which will lower the costs per individual device produced on such wafers. Similarly, technologies are being developed for deposition of SiC and GaN layers having sufficient quality for device production. The quality of the wafers is particularly important because most of the producers are aiming to qualify as suppliers for automotive industry.

For SiC, the major RTO supporting the R&D efforts in Europe is Fraunhofer. SiC wafers are produced by several companies in Europe, including *Soitec* and *STMicroelectronics*. *Wolfspeed* announced in February 2023 plans to construct a 200-mm SiC wafer fab and R&D facility in Saarland, Germany.

For GaN, the major RTO supporting the R&D efforts in Europe is IMEC. A recently established in Belgium *GaN Valley*™ includes as stakeholders many companies working to scale-up and improve the production of GaN wafers, such as *BelGaN*, *Aixtron*, *Soitec*, and *Siltronic*.

**Production of power-control devices.** Power-control devices are sold to individual consumers (e.g., as power adapters and chargers for consumer electronics), but also to value chains, such as Energy and Automotive, that impose stringent qualification requirements. Accordingly, the large-volume production of power-control devices based on SiC and GaN is performed in Europe by major semiconductor manufacturers, including *Bosch*, *Infineon*, and *STMicroelectronics*.

# <span id="page-73-0"></span>**5.7 The Fragrances value chain**

Fragrances are unique and complex combinations of natural and synthetic ingredients that are added to products to give them a distinctive smell. Fragrances are not only used in perfumes, but also in many other consumer products, such as cosmetics, personal care and household cleaning products. They satisfy valued emotional needs and solve important functional problems, such as masking bad smells.

Fragrance can be a key driver for consumer choice and consumer brands often rely on innovation with fragrances to differentiate their products and drive value. Fragrances are considered to be a combination of science and creativity. The fragrance industry relies on its suppliers to deliver natural and synthetic raw materials sourced from over 50 countries. It then carries out research and development (R&D) and manufacturing activities using a highly skilled workforce to deliver fragrances to its downstream customers. These customers are manufacturers and retailers of fine fragrance and consumer products. # <span id="page-77-1"></span><span id="page-77-0"></span>**5.7.2 Main SSbD challenges foreseen**

The current framework is a common framework for SSbD that would apply to substances/ materials irrespective of their applications (given that the same chemical could be used in different applications). The SSbD framework needs to take into consideration the different final uses of each chemical and potential mitigation measures that could help make a chemical and the respective product safer.

There are no trade-offs between safety and sustainability, how to rate safety against sustainability? A substance can have a hazardous profile but can be used safely and can have a very good environmental profile. Comparing this to a potential substitute molecule with a better hazard profile, but a weaker Product Environmental Footprint (PEF) / Product Carbon Footprint (PCF) than the first molecule. How can this realistic case be incorporated into the assessment process to avoid such molecules being abandoned at Step 1 of the SSbD assessment?

Substantial work remains to be done on the PEF methodology in order to fill gaps for the assessment of toxicity or of circularity of feedstocks. Other internationally recognised standards could also be validly considered to advance on sustainable approaches, based on a lifecycle approach to the products across the value chains, in order to use the already existing data on life cycle assessment and to feed in the datasets. To illustrate, the uptake of biogenic carbon/ incineration use and biomass balance approach shall be accepted by the PEF methodology. Other standards and validated methods should also be allowed, in order to use the already existing data on LCA and to feed in the datasets.

In general, data transparency and harmonized methods as well as data availability and information flow will be critical for every value chain. Gathering available and reliable data across the whole value chain including smaller stakeholders will be a very complex and a critical step. Standardized processes to gather data and the decision which data is actually reliable and relevant for analysis is not yet established, common tools and practices still need to be aligned.

The capabilities of New Approach Methodologies (NAMs) to address complex endpoints are still under discussion. NAMs are still under development, thus will not cover all aspects and are also not yet validated to fully testing on e.g. adversity or reprotoxicity via NAMs. Furthermore, there will be differences between the acceptance under REACH/CLP and SSbD, does 'no indication' from NAMs, read across or in silico data lead to 'no classification' under SSbD? Or will animal testing be necessary as under REACH requirements?

The overall question of hazard versus risk assessment is a key concern for the safety analysis. A hazardous chemical can be handled and used in a very safe manner, while a less hazardous substance can result in a very similar risk as a hazardous substance. Key concerns here are the acceptance of risk management options and risk management analysis.

This update of D4.1 has allowed the value chains team to develop the analysis of steps in chosen sub-value chains: Glass packaging, Smart textiles, Insulation panels, Car seats, Solar panels, Wind turbines/blades, Semiconductors for power control. The Cosmetics and Detergents sub-value chains of fragrances have been also described, with fragrances as a new associated partner. Major updates were mainly provided for Plastics packaging, Wind energy and Electronics.

The value chains have many SSbD related challenges in common, like the sourcing of critical raw materials and their necessary recyclability, the energy intensive manufacturing processes, the use of hazardous substances and their treatment in the design process, the need for sharing data along value chains, and many more. These numerous SSbD challenges have to be transformed to proposed solutions, which will be developed in the specific value chains roadmaps (D4.5) and associated case studies to be developed in the second testing phase of the SSbD framework in collaboration with JRC and DG RTD in 2024. This work, done in collaboration with WP2 and WP3, on challenges and solutions along life cycles, will be proposed to integrate the Horizon Europe work programmes.
