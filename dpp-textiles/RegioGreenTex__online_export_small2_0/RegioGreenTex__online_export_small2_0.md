To support this transition, enhanced collection systems are needed to reduce contamination and enable largerscale operations. This must be backed by alternative routes and upgraded infrastructure, with early harmonization to ensure functional and efficient textile waste pathways across borders.

In the long term, clearer regulations and targeted incentives will be essential to streamline waste management and accelerate the adoption of sustainable practices.

Coordinated efforts, both technical, behavioural, and regulatory, could unlock the full potential of a truly circular textile economy. This section of the guide goes reach this goal.

## Where textile waste comes from

Waste refers to any material that is being discarded by the holder. For the textile industry the waste occurs across the value chain during the production, distribution, or consumption of textiles. This waste can come from various sources, including manufacturing scraps, unused fabric, damaged or defective products, and post-consumer textiles like clothing and household textiles.<sup>v</sup> Post consumer textiles are responsible for 82% of the textile waste in the EU.<sup>vi</sup> Sorting helps to identify different types of textiles based on parameters such as material composition, condition, and potential for reuse or recycling.

There are several key sources of textile waste,<sup>vii</sup> generally:

- • **Pre-consumer waste:** This includes scraps and leftovers from textile manufacturing processes, such as spinning, weaving, and cutting. These scraps can be generated at various stages of production and are typically considered waste if they cannot be reused or recycled within the manufacturing process.
- • **Post-consumer waste:** This includes textiles that have been used by consumers and are discarded at the end of their life cycle. This category encompasses clothing, household textiles (such as bedding and towels), and other textile products that are no longer wanted or usable.
- • **Post-industrial waste:** By-products from other industries that use textiles in their in-going or out-going processes. This could be textiles used in the medical or automotive sector or packaging textiles.

## The R-ladder

The waste hierarchy described in the Waste Framework Directive,<sup>viii</sup> is a framework used to prioritize waste management strategies based on their environmental impact. It encourages reducing waste at the source and maximizing resource efficiency. This framework has been further reworked and is known as the R-ladder. The "R" refers to different strategies, typically structured in a ladder-like order, with the highest priority at the top.

The textile industry generates a significant amount of waste, and applying the R-ladder helps to shift from a linear "take-make-dispose" model to a circular approach. Prioritizing refuse, reduce, and reuse leads to fewer new textiles being produced, which lowers environmental impacts such as water use, chemical pollution, and carbon emissions.

Recycling is often seen as a solution, but it comes lower in the hierarchy because it requires energy and can degrade fibre quality. Disposal is the least desirable, yet 87% of used clothes still end up in landfills or incinerators.<sup>ix</sup>

## Sorting of textiles: A crucial step

Sorting of textile waste is an essential step in the process of managing textile waste effectively. There is a big potential to increase value retention within Europe by collecting more waste, sort for reuse and repair and scale the fibre-to-fibre recycling. This way a larger share of European waste can be put to value in European industry. For a large-scale recycling to be profitable, reliable sources of well-defined textile materials are needed.

When a recycling company handles waste, the process must be specifically adapted to the type of material that is being recycled into new fibres. This can take time and be quite costly. The chemical content of the fibre might affect the recycling process. Even the colour might have an impact or contaminate between batches. Therefore, it is important that there is a continuous stream of harmonized waste that enters the recyclers processes. The more material that can be recycled from a similar batch, the better is the chance to keep costs down.

Therefore, breaking down waste into components, i.e. converting waste into a series of data points such as condition, material composition, chemicals, colour and prints, could harmonize sorting processes and facilitate more efficient waste streams.

While automated sorting technologies are advancing for textiles, sorting is almost exclusively done manually in the industry today. The human eye, feel and perception will struggle to handle some of the important data points like chemical content of a garment that is needed to make accurate decisions. Defined and harmonized basic channelling principles will aid in building more efficient sorting solutions in the future.

## Digital Product Passports

A digital shift is essential for the circular transformation, providing accurate and efficient access to granular information and cost efficiency for circular business models. The upcoming Digital Product Passport (DPP), part of the EU Strategy for Sustainable and Circular Textiles, will offer access to various data points, helping to identify which recommerce businesses can develop first.

The DPP is a digital record accompanying a textile product throughout its lifecycle, containing data on material composition, sustainability indicators, supply chain details, and instructions for repair, reuse, and recycling. Brands and manufacturers will document and disclose information on environmental impact, chemical composition, and recyclability, ensuring compliance with EU regulations like REACH and the Eco design for Sustainable Products Regulation.

# TO TRANSFORM WASTE INTO A RESOURCE

## Turning waste into value: understanding the power of collaboration

**Textile waste isn't just a problem, it's also a possibility. With the right mindset and collaboration, discarded materials can become valuable resources again. To make this shift from waste to resource, it's important to understand how value is created. A material only becomes a "resource" when it's useful, especially when combined with other materials, tools, or knowledge.<sup>xi</sup> In other words, it's not just about what something is, but what it can do in the right setting.**

Textiles, like other resources, are not one-size-fits-all. Their value depends on how they're used, what they're combined with, and the purpose they serve. When different actors, such as designers, recyclers, manufacturers,

or remanufacturers, work together and share what they know, new opportunities emerge. A discarded fabric might become insulation, upholstery, or part of a new garment, depending on how and where it's reimagined.

This is what we mean by resource renewal, an ongoing, collaborative process where waste materials are rethought, reshaped, and reused in new ways.<sup>xii</sup> Circularity isn't just about recycling, it's about relationships. It's about understanding how different players in the system, companies, innovators, and consumers, can come together to unlock the hidden potential in what we throw away.<sup>xiii</sup>

By taking a systemic view and considering the full life of a product, from production to disposal, we can design smarter, reduce waste, and extend the value of our materials. In the circular economy, progress happens through cooperation,

innovation, and a shared belief that today's waste can be tomorrow's solution.

*It is important to remember that waste is only waste if we consider it waste and refuse to see its potential. By reimagining discarded textiles as resources, we can unlock possibilities for reuse, innovation, and creativity, transforming what was once waste into resources.*

## Moving from a value chain perspective to a business network perspective

In the light of the coming legislation such as the Eco-design for sustainable products regulation (ESPR),<sup>xiv</sup> the Green Claims Directive<sup>xv</sup> and the Waste Framework Directive, large volumes of textiles are to be handled and directed towards the most suitable processing solution, for reuse as well as for recycling. Managing larger and

more specific volumes, will require new knowledge, business practices, an industry technology leap and finally a consumer behaviour shift - great challenges that also hold big opportunities to build stronger sustainable regional value chains in Europe.

A circular value chain requires innovative solutions jointly developed by different stakeholders. Connecting existing companies and initiatives is a crucial first step to enhance business exchanges and scale up circular businesses. However, instead of focusing on a specific value chain and optimizing each step to increase efficiency and reduce costs, one should consider the importance of adopting a business network perspective. To clarify, a business network, or value network, is a

system of interconnected companies and organisations that collaborate to create and deliver value.<sup>xvi</sup> Unlike the value chain, the business network is more complex and involves many different actors who can have relationships with each other, not just with their customers or suppliers. By applying a business network perspective, it becomes easier to understand the lock-in effects and opportunities that different business relationships can contribute to.

*Business networks emphasize collaboration, resource sharing, and innovation to create value in a more dynamic and flexible way. During an innovation process, companies and organisations can take on different roles in the business network depending on who they collaborate with, such as developers, producers, and users.*

Moreover, to develop new solutions, organisations must progress through different levels of interaction.<sup>xvii</sup> It begins with networking, which involves loosely connected relationships and weak ties between the company and other organisations. Weak ties are essential for acquiring new information and identifying new opportunities. Typical interactions include communities, innovation networks, crowdsourcing, mass-customization, idea competitions, and intermediation. The next stage is coordinating, which, like networking, involves loose relationships. However, coordinating differs as it has a joint direction in the interaction process.

Cooperating involves stronger ties between actors compared to networking and coordinating. These relationships are based on mutual trust and aim to create mutual value. Frequent interactions help organisations access and leverage resources within the network.<sup>xviii</sup> Collaboration at higher levels, such as open innovation ecosystems and open-source developments, involves stronger ties and relationships. It is not only about trust but also about commitment. It requires higher investments to enhance the capabilities of the other party and is typically characterized by a higher degree of uncertainty. The highest level of interaction is co-creating, which represents the strongest relationships, engagement, uncertainty, and alignment towards a shared purpose. This strategy is used when the goal exceeds the capabilities of both parties, necessitating joint exploration and the creation of shared meaning and benefits.

All stages involve learning how resources, such as waste, can be valuable in other actors' products and processes. The first stage involves mapping the potential use of waste, while the final stage involves mutual adaptations to embed waste as a resource in ongoing operations. Throughout this interactive learning phase, it will become evident how established business relationships may either hinder or facilitate the transformation of waste into a valuable resource.

## Building regional partnerships to repurpose waste

The closing of textile loops in Europe demands a unified and collaborative approach that acknowledges regional strengths and challenges. Through consistent policies, investments in technology, and cross-regional collaboration, Europe can transform textile waste into a valuable resource, paving the way for a sustainable and circular textile future. The opportunity can, according to the report *Scaling textile recycling in Europe – turning waste into value*,<sup>xix</sup> lead to scaling of textile recycling in Europe, the prognosis show that an 18% to 26% fibre-to-fibre recycling rate could be achieved by 2030. However, advanced, accurate, and automated fibre sorting and pre-processing processes are not yet developed. In 2030, where automated sorting and technology would be further advanced, the industry has a potential to create 15 000 new green jobs and save four million tonnes CO<sup>2</sup>-equivalent emissions according to the report. Resale, rental, repair, and remaking markets could grow their share of the total textiles industry worldwide.

The combination of laws and directives, along with the development of suitable business models that generate revenue, requires the right types of collaborations where new solutions can be developed. Encouraging new contacts to become business relationships where we create new solutions together is crucial. ### BUILDING THE ECOSYSTEM: A REGIONAL BLUEPRINT FOR CIRCULAR INNOVATION

In Romania's North-East region, the textile industry has deep roots. Once home to the country's only academic-level textile research centre, the region is now becoming a hub for circular innovation, thanks in large part to the Regional Development Agency (RDA) and its smart specialisation strategy.

At the heart of this strategy is a belief that innovation must be co-created. Through a process called the Entrepreneurial Discovery Process, the RDA brings together companies, researchers, and civil society to identify challenges and design solutions. These aren't just brainstorming sessions, they're structured focus groups that lead to projects, funding, and real change.

One of the agency's key moves was to create thematic hubs, clusters of companies and organisations working on shared goals. In the case of textiles, the

focus was clear: reduce waste, starting from the design phase. This approach laid the groundwork for partnerships like the one between Katty Fashion and Mai Bine/ REDU, two very different organisations united by a common purpose.

*"We started thinking not just about the challenges, but about the solutions, and those solutions became projects, regional, individual, and most importantly, collaborative."*  
*-Agatha Filimon, RDA.*

### KATTY FASHION: TRANSFORMING TRADITION THROUGH TECHNOLOGY

When Caterina Ailiesei founded Katty Fashion, her goal wasn't just to build a successful business, it was to create a workplace rooted in ethics, fairness, and professional pride. Over two decades later,

the company has become a model for how traditional manufacturing can evolve into a driver of circular innovation.

*"I didn't start the company to become rich, I started it to surround myself with people who share values, ethics, professionalism, and to do the best possible for those who depend on us."*

-Caterina Ailiesei, Katty Fashion

Katty Fashion specialises in high-end, bespoke manufacturing for over 50 European brands. But as the fashion industry's environmental impact became harder to ignore, Caterina and her team began asking deeper questions: How can we reduce waste before it's even created? How can we empower our team to focus on meaningful work, not repetitive tasks? And how can we bring our clients along on this journey?

The answer lies in digital transformation, but not the kind that's imposed from the top down. Instead, Katty Fashion introduced 3D prototyping and digital design tools through an iterative, collaborative process. Clients were initially hesitant to abandon physical samples, so the team developed a co-creation platform that allowed them to visualise and refine designs digitally.

This approach not only reduced material waste, but it also created space for new ideas. By analysing fabric consumption during the design phase, the team

began identifying leftover materials that could be repurposed. That's where the collaboration with Mai Bine/REDU began to take shape.

MAI BINE AND REDU: GRASSROOTS INNOVATION WITH A PURPOSE

While Katty Fashion was transforming from the inside out, another kind of innovation was taking root in the same region, this time from the ground up.

Mai Bine, a Romanian NGO focused on sustainability, launched REDU to raise awareness about textile waste and promote conscious consumption. It didn't start with a business plan or a product, it started with a question: What if we could change the way people think about clothes?

*"We started by questioning the status quo of consumption, it was about creating a space where people could swap clothes, bring in old garments, and give them a new life."*

-Elvys Sandu, Mai Bine/REDU

REDU quickly became a community hub - part workshop, part movement. People gathered not just to recycle, but to connect. The emotional attachment to the mission was strong, and it's what kept the initiative alive through the inevitable ups and downs.

But REDU didn't stop at awareness. With support from the RDA and connections made through events and networks like

Textile Clothing Business Lab (TCBL), the team began exploring how to turn textile waste into new products, and eventually, into a viable business model.

That's when the collaboration with Katty Fashion began. What started as a simple collection of fabric scraps evolved into a shared vision for circular design. Together, they created prototypes from leftover materials, tested them with brands, and even sold them through partner websites. The partnership also inspired REDU to begin developing a digital tool to track and manage textile waste. It's still in progress, and the market isn't quite ready, but the vision is clear: a smarter, more transparent system for circular production.

*"We always say we hope for a future where we don't need to exist, because that would mean the problem is solved."*

-Elvys Sandu, Mai Bine/REDU

REDU's story is a powerful reminder that innovation doesn't always start with technology. Sometimes, it starts with empathy, community, and a willingness to do things differently.

COLLABORATION IN ACTION: CHALLENGES AND BREAKTHROUGHS

Collaboration sounds ideal in theory, but in practice, it requires patience, compromise, and a willingness to change. For Katty Fashion and REDU, working together meant more than just pooling

resources. It meant actively learning about each other's resources through aligning workflows, adapting technologies, and building trust across very different organisational cultures. One of the first hurdles was waste sorting. REDU needed specific types of textile scraps to create new products, but in a busy factory environment, separating materials by size, type, or colour wasn't part of the routine.

For Caterina, the solution wasn't to issue orders, it was to invite participation. She encouraged her team to reflect on the purpose behind the change and to come up with their own ways of implementing it. This approach created a sense of ownership and mutual respect. It also laid the foundation for deeper collaboration, including the integration of REDU's upcoming digital waste tracking tool with Katty Fashion's design and production systems.

But collaboration didn't stop at the factory floor. RDA played a crucial role in facilitating dialogue, adapting funding instruments, and creating spaces, both physical and digital, where ideas could be exchanged.

*"Circularity isn't one-size-fits-all. Each value chain, each product, each region needs its own approach, and that means working together."*

-Caterina Ailiesei, Katty Fashion

Through networks like TCBL, Regenova, and RegioGreenTex, the partners connected with like-minded organisations across Europe. These relationships brought not only inspiration, but also access to knowledge, funding, and new markets.

### LESSONS FOR ASPIRING CIRCULAR ENTREPRENEURS

For those looking to start or transform a textile business with sustainability in mind, the experiences of Katty Fashion, REDU, and their regional partners offer more than inspiration, they offer a roadmap.

Here are some of the most important lessons they've learned along the way:

1. START WITH WHAT YOU HAVE, AND WHERE YOU ARE  
Circularity doesn't require a perfect setup. It begins with understanding your local context: What kinds of waste are available? What infrastructure exists? Who are the people and organisations already working toward similar goals?

2. BUILD RELATIONSHIPS BEFORE YOU BUILD PRODUCTS

The most successful projects in this story didn't start with a business plan, they started with conversations. Whether it was by chance meeting at a regional event or a shared frustration with waste, the foundation was always human connection. It is essential to show curiosity and take the time to understand each other's perspective, and to remain flexible and open to adapting alongside your partners.

3. JOIN (OR CREATE) AN ECOSYSTEM

No one can go circular alone. That's why networks like TCBL, Regenova, and

RegioGreenTex are so important. They provide access to knowledge, funding, and collaborators who share your values.

4. BE PATIENT, BE FLEXIBLE, BE REALISTIC  
Circular transformation is not a straight line. It involves trial and error, shifting regulations, and sometimes resistance from within. But with the right mindset, these challenges become opportunities for growth.

every purchase. Transparency and smart regulation can help guide people toward better decisions.

Finally, the broader ecosystem matters. Businesses must create value, universities must generate knowledge, and society must shape culture. In short, the future of fashion depends on collaboration, clarity, and commitment. The hopes are high, but so is the need for action. As Agatha Filimon from RDA stressed:

*"Let's be sure that innovation really improves things, we don't want to make things more complicated, we want to make them better."*

...find alternatives to current waste handling, including recycling, reuse, or resale options.

...manage dead stock efficiently (redistribution, resale, upcycling).

...turn incineration costs into revenue by exploring secondary markets.

...identify logistics and storage solutions to optimise waste flows.

...identify the right partners and technologies to execute sustainability projects.

...solve diverse challenges (e.g., material sourcing, waste handling, traceability).

...gain internal buy-in from teams and leadership for sustainability strategies.

...find instant ways to build knowledge (guides, expert insights, training).

...stay continuously updated on regulatory changes to ensure compliance.

...measure and communicate sustainability impact effectively (LCA, carbon footprint).

Västra Götalandsregionen (VGR) is a key player in advancing sustainable textile systems in Sweden and Europe.
